What an azeotrope means on the plant

An azeotrope is a mixture that boils at constant temperature and gives a vapour of the same composition as the liquid. Simple distillation stops at that composition: you cannot get dry ethanol by distilling 95 % ethanol, however many plates you add.

  • Minimum-boiling azeotropes (most of them) boil below both pure components and come over first. Toluene and water distil together at about 84 °C, which is how toluene is used to dry a batch.
  • Maximum-boiling azeotropes boil above both components and stay behind in the still, as acetone with chloroform and water with formic acid do.
  • Heterogeneous azeotropes condense into two layers. Decant the water layer and return the solvent layer, and the azeotrope is broken. This is the basis of Dean–Stark water removal.
  • Pressure changes the azeotrope. Its temperature and composition move with pressure, and some disappear under vacuum. Use the calculator’s pressure table to see whether vacuum or pressure distillation could get past it.

How the estimates are made, and how good they are

The calculator finds the boiling point of every liquid composition at your pressure, and looks for the point where the vapour has the same composition as the liquid. It uses:

  • Activity coefficients from modified UNIFAC (Dortmund), with the published group parameters. Pairs whose groups have no published interaction parameters are not estimated.
  • Vapour pressure from the Antoine constants on each solvent page.
  • Two liquid layers: when the model predicts that the liquid splits, the azeotrope is found at the three-phase temperature, as a heterogeneous azeotrope.
  • An ideal vapour, which is reasonable from deep vacuum up to a few bar.

Check against the measured data. For the 126 measured pairs at 1 atm that the model covers, it found 118 of the 123 azeotropes. Its temperature was within 1 °C for 77 % of them and within 3 °C for 92 %, with a median error of 0.4 °C. Composition was within 5 mol % for 72 %, with a median error of 2.5 mol %. It missed methyl acetate–water, diethyl ether–water, methanol–isopropanol, benzene–heptane and ethylene glycol–toluene. It predicted a very weak azeotrope for acetic acid–water, where the handbooks disagree.

On the plant

Treat an estimate as a screening answer. Before you design a column, a solvent swap or a drying step around an azeotrope, confirm it with measured VLE data or a lab distillation. Where measured and estimated values differ by more than 5 °C, the table flags the row so you can check another source.
