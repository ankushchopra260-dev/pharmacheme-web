Why a process that works in the lab can fail in a big vessel, and what to check before the first batch

Ankush Chopra · PharmaChemE

Welcome. Every product we make started in a flask. Scale-up is the job of moving it into a vessel thousands of times bigger without losing yield, quality or safety. Today is not about equations for their own sake. It is about five things that change when a vessel gets bigger, and the questions that catch problems before the batch does. Ask the room: who has seen a batch behave differently from the lab result? Take two quick stories. Those stories are the reason we are here.

The same chemistry, a different world

22x

less cooling surface per litre in a 10 m3 vessel than in a 1 L flask

3.2 h

shortest dosing time for a step the flask can run in about 9 minutes (illustrative)

51 min

to heat the plant vessel by 40 °C, against a few minutes in the flask

The chemistry did not change. The vessel did.

These numbers are calculated, not measured, and you will redo them yourself on the live page. Take a 1 litre flask and a 10 cubic metre vessel, both shaped the same. The bigger vessel holds ten thousand times more material but has only about 464 times more wall to cool through, so each litre has about 22 times less cooling surface. (Click.) A step that the flask could take in 10 minutes needs a few hours in the plant if the same heat has to leave through the jacket. (Click.) And everything that is heated or cooled takes longer. (Click.) Scale-up is mostly the story of those three effects: heat, mixing and time.

What stays the same, what changes

Stays the same

  • The chemistry and the reaction paths
  • Rate constants at the same temperature and concentration
  • Heat of reaction per kilogram
  • Solubility and physical properties

Changes with size

  • Cooling and heating surface per litre
  • How fast things mix
  • Times for heating, cooling, charging and transfer
  • Local concentration where a feed enters
  • Wall material and hold times
First, clear the idea that scale-up changes the chemistry. It does not. On the left, what stays the same. A kilogram of reaction mass releases the same heat in a flask or a reactor. (Click.) On the right, what changes with size. Most of these are about the equipment, not the reaction. The surface per litre falls, mixing slows, every operation takes longer, and a feed that disperses in a second in a flask may take much longer to spread in a large vessel. Our job is to find which of these matter for this process.

Here are three vessels that all have height equal to diameter, with the jacket on the side wall. (Click through the rows.) Volume grows with the cube of the diameter. Wall area grows only with the square. So the area per litre falls as the vessel grows. This is the single most important fact in scale-up. Real vessels differ in shape and have a bottom jacket and coils, but the trend is the same. (Click.) Notice the plant vessel has 22 times less jacket per litre than the flask.

Guess: how much less cooling per litre?

A 1 L flask and a 10 m3 vessel have the same shape. How many times less jacket area does each litre of the large vessel have?

about 22x

Area per litre is 4 divided by the diameter for this shape. The flask is 11 cm across and the plant vessel 234 cm, a ratio of 21.5, so each litre has 22 times less wall.

Let the room guess first. Most people say two or three times, because 10 cubic metres feels like a normal vessel. Write the guesses on the board. (Click.) The answer is about 22. The wall grows with diameter squared while the volume grows with diameter cubed, so the ratio is the ratio of the diameters. Ask: if the jacket is that much weaker per litre, what does that mean for a reaction that gives out heat?

Heat made grows faster than heat removed

  • Heat released grows with the volume of the batch
  • Heat removed through the wall grows only with its area
  • The gap widens as the vessel grows
  • Fast steps that were easy in the flask become the limit

V vs A

volume goes with size cubed, wall area with size squared

Four short points. Heat released by a reaction depends on how much material is reacting, so it grows with volume. (Click.) Heat removed through the wall depends on the wall area, which grows more slowly. (Click.) So the gap widens with size. (Click.) The practical result is that a step the flask handled by simply adding everything in ten minutes may be the step that limits the plant. Ask the room which steps in their own products are exothermic, and write them on the board.

Same reaction: the shortest safe dosing time

Lab flask, 1 LPlant vessel, 10 m3

Shortest dosing time that the jacket can follow9 min3.2 h

Illustrative: cooling capacity = U x area per litre x driving force. Real values come from your own reactor data and calorimetry.

Here is the same reaction in both vessels. Assume it releases 25 kilocalories per litre of batch, the jacket coefficient is 150 kilocalories per hour per square metre per degree, and the jacket can hold a 30 degree driving force. (Click.) Per litre, the flask can remove about 166 kilocalories an hour. (Click.) The plant vessel removes about 7.7. (Click.) If the heat is released as fast as it is added, the flask can follow a dosing time of about 9 minutes. The plant needs at least 3.2 hours. That is not a mistake in the recipe. It is the new limit. This is why the recipe for the plant is not the recipe for the flask.

Dosing faster than heat can leave

  • If feed goes in faster than it reacts, unreacted feed piles up
  • If cooling then fails, all of it can react at once
  • A reaction that "went fine" on every batch can still hide this
  • Dose-controlled means the feed rate sets the heat release

Stop the feed

and the heat should stop. If it does not, find out why before scaling up.

Two limits sit behind the dosing time. One is the jacket, which we just calculated. The other is the reaction itself. If the feed goes in faster than it reacts, unreacted material accumulates. (Click.) If something then stops, such as the agitator, the cooling or the temperature falling too low for the reaction to start, that stored material can react quickly. (Click.) This is the root of many runaway incidents, and the earlier runaway reactions module covers it in detail. (Click.) A good scale-up wants a reaction that is dose-controlled, where stopping the feed quickly stops the heat. (Click.) A short lab test can show which type you have.

Mixing rules: keep one thing the same

Geometrically similar vessels, 1 L to 10 m3, length ratio 21.5. Power per litre goes with speed cubed times diameter squared.

This is the hard truth about mixing: you can keep only one thing the same. Read across each row for a scale of 1 litre to 10 cubic metres, which is a length ratio of 21.5. Keep the speed and the tip speed rises 22 times and the power per litre rises by about 464 times, which is impossible. (Click.) Keep the power per litre the same, which is the common choice, and the speed drops to about one eighth, the tip speed rises, and the blend time grows by about 7.7 times. (Click.) Keep the tip speed and the power per litre collapses and blending takes 22 times longer. (Click.) Keep Reynolds number and the numbers become absurd. So we choose the rule that protects the step that matters, and we accept the rest. If the step is fast and mixing sensitive, blend time is what we protect.

Three scale-up myths

"Same rpm gives the same mixing."

Myth

Tip speed and power per litre change a lot.

"If it works at 1 L, it works at 10 m3."

Myth

Heat, mixing and time all change.

"A slower dosing time at scale is normal."

Fact

The jacket sets the limit, not the recipe.

Three statements. Ask the room to call each one before you click. (Click.) Same rpm in a bigger vessel does not give the same mixing. We just saw the tip speed and power per litre change by large factors. (Click.) Working at one litre does not guarantee ten cubic metres. The lab shows the chemistry works, not that the plant can carry the heat and the mixing. (Click.) A longer dosing time at scale is often exactly right. If the jacket cannot follow, the dosing has to slow down, and that is a design decision, not a failure.

Where mixing hurts a process

Fast reactions

Feed meets a hot, concentrated zone near the inlet and forms by-products.

Crystallisation

Uneven supersaturation gives fines, lumps or a different crystal size.

Gas and liquid

Transfer rate into the liquid falls when bubble size and gas hold-up change.

Solids

Solids settle in dead zones or lift too slowly from the bottom.

Four places where poor mixing shows up. Fast reactions: if the reaction is quicker than the mixing, the product is made in a patchy way and by-products appear. (Click.) Crystallisation: the feed and the cooling reach different parts of the vessel at different times, so the crystals differ. (Click.) Gas and liquid: the amount of gas absorbed depends on bubble size and gas hold-up, which change with vessel size and agitator. (Click.) Solids: a catalyst or a slurry may sit in a corner of a large vessel. Ask the room which of the four applies to their own product.

Time is the third effect. The heat-up row is calculated: 2 minutes in the flask, about 51 in the plant vessel with these assumptions. (Click through the rows.) The other rows are typical trends and will differ by plant. Charging, transfer, filtering and drying all take longer. Why does it matter? Because the material sits longer at temperature, in contact with other things, or between steps. A product that is stable for twenty minutes in a flask may degrade over three hours in the plant.

Hold times and cycle time

  • Time at temperature adds up across heating, hold and cooling
  • Impurities can grow while material waits
  • Every extra hour of a slow step is an hour of plant time
  • Ask the lab: how long can this hold, and at what temperature?
Hold time is how long a product or intermediate can sit before it degrades. (Click.) In the lab, nobody notices. In the plant, the heating, the transfer and the filtration add up. (Click.) And the cycle time of the batch grows, which affects how many batches the plant can make. (Click.) So before scale-up we ask the lab for hold-time data at the planned temperatures. If there is none, that is a gap to close, not an assumption to make.

Solids behave differently at scale

Crystallising

Use the same cooling profile shape and seeding point, not the same time.

Filtering

A taller cake is more resistant. Measure the cake resistance in the lab.

Washing

Wash ratio is the same, but channels in a large cake can leave patches.

Drying

A deeper bed and a slower heat supply mean longer, uneven drying.

Solids need their own thought. Crystallising: copy the shape of the cooling profile and the seeding point, not the clock time. The metastable zone and the seeding rules from the lab still apply. (Click.) Filtering: a thicker cake offers more resistance, so measure the cake resistance in the lab and use it to estimate the plant filtering time. (Click.) Washing: the wash ratio is the same, but channels in a thick cake can leave unwashed patches. (Click.) Drying: a deeper bed and the limited heat supply give longer, less even drying. The crystallisation scale-up article on this site covers the solubility and seeding side.

This is the lab work that makes scale-up predictable. Read each row aloud. Calorimetry gives the heat and the rate, so we can compare with the plant's cooling. (Click.) The accumulation test shows how much feed stays unreacted. (Click.) The stirring test shows whether the product depends on mixing. (Click.) Solubility and the metastable zone drive the crystallisation steps. (Click.) Cake resistance and hold time are cheap to measure and expensive to guess. If a row is missing for your product, that is the first job.

Scale up in steps, and scale down to test

  • Move through lab, kilo lab, pilot, then plant
  • Rule of thumb: keep each step to tens of times in volume, not thousands
  • Use a small vessel to copy the plant, such as the same dosing time and mixing
  • Compare each step with the one before and explain every difference
Nobody goes from a flask to a ten cubic metre reactor in one jump if they can avoid it. (Click.) A common rule of thumb keeps each step to a factor of tens in volume. It is a guide, not a law. (Click.) Scale-down is the other half. Use the small vessel to copy what the plant will do, for example the planned dosing time and a similar mixing level. If the product is the same under plant-like conditions, you gain confidence. (Click.) And at every step, write down what is different from the last one and why.

Before the first plant batch

Agree the limits

Dosing time, temperature, hold times and stop points, written down.

Check the equipment

Jacket, condenser, agitator and relief capacity against the planned batch.

Plan the sampling

Extra samples and temperature checks at the steps the lab found sensitive.

Agree who can stop

Anyone who sees the warning signs can stop the feed.

A first plant batch is an experiment with a plan. Agree the limits first: the dosing time, the temperature range, the hold times and where the batch can safely be stopped. (Click.) Check the equipment against the plan, including the jacket, the condenser, the agitator and the relief. (Click.) Plan extra samples and temperature checks at the steps the lab found sensitive. (Click.) And make sure everyone on shift knows they can stop the feed if the signs appear. A batch that is stopped early and understood is better than a batch that is finished and not understood.

During the batch: signs to watch

  • Temperature rising above the set point at the planned dose rate
  • Jacket outlet temperature climbing or the coolant flow at maximum
  • Reaction slower to start than in the lab, with no heat seen
  • Agitator current, foam or colour different from the lab or earlier batches
These are warning signs. Read them together. Temperature above the set point at the planned dose rate means the heat removal is behind. (Click.) A jacket outlet that is warming and a coolant valve at full open say the jacket is at its limit. (Click.) A reaction that does not start when it should is a warning in a semi-batch process, because feed may be accumulating. (Click.) Different agitator current, foam or colour tells you the mixing or the chemistry is not the same as before. Stopping the feed is the first action, then ask why.

Five questions to ask in every scale-up

  1. Can the jacket remove the heat at the planned dosing time?
  2. Is the mixing enough for the step that is mixing sensitive?
  3. Which steps take longer, and does the product tolerate it?
  4. What is the worst case if cooling or the agitator stops?
  5. What did we measure, and what did we only assume?
