Trainer’s guide: slide by slide

Open any slide for its notes. Printing opens them all.

  1. Static electricity and solvent fire safety (title slide)

    Welcome. Today is about one thing: how a spark too small to feel can set fire to the solvents we handle every day. Everyone in this room has a part in stopping it, from the operator charging a drum to the plant head who approves the budget for an earthing clamp. Keep it interactive: I will ask questions throughout, and towards the end we will try it live on screen.

  2. Ever felt a shock getting out of a car?

    Start with a show of hands: who has felt a shock from a car door, a lift button or a trolley? That shock is roughly 10 to 25 millijoules. (Click: the first number appears.) You can feel a discharge from about 1 mJ. (Click.) Toluene vapour ignites at 0.24 mJ and methanol at 0.14 mJ, which is below what you can feel. (Click.) So when someone says “I didn’t feel any spark”, that proves nothing. Ask: how many drums of toluene or methanol did we handle this week?

  3. Try it now: bend water with a balloon

    A quick live demo to wake everyone up. You need a balloon, a wool cloth or sweater, a plastic water bottle with a pin hole in the cap, and a few bits of paper. Ask a volunteer from the plant head or executive group to do it; people enjoy watching a senior person get it to work. (Click through the steps as they do it.) Then make the point: nothing special happened here except rubbing two materials. That is all a solvent needs to charge up in a pipe or a filter, and all a powder needs when it slides out of a bag. Note: on a very humid day the balloon loses its charge faster; that is a useful talking point, because humidity helps with surfaces but does nothing for charge inside a liquid.

  4. A solvent fire needs three things at once

    The fire triangle, but for vapour. Liquid does not burn; the vapour above it does, and only between the lower and upper flammable limits. Ask the room: which side of the triangle is hardest to remove in our plant? Usually the answer is fuel, because the solvent is the process. Oxygen we can remove by nitrogen inerting. Ignition we control by earthing, bonding, flameproof equipment and permits. Static is the ignition source that is invisible, so that is today’s focus.

  5. Know the solvents we charge

    Walk down the table. Two things to notice. First, every flash point here is below a warm day, so the vapour above these liquids can be flammable at room temperature. Second, the last column: hydrocarbons like toluene, hexane and heptane hold static charge because they conduct electricity very poorly. Alcohols and ketones let charge leak away, but only if the container and equipment are earthed. A conductive solvent in a plastic bucket or an unearthed drum still charges up. Ask: which of these do we use most?

  6. On a warm day, the air is hotter than the flash point

    The blue band is a warm day at many Indian plants, about 25 to 45 °C. (On a cold winter night near 5 °C, methanol and IPA fall below their flash points, but toluene, hexane, acetone, ethyl acetate and heptane do not.) (Click.) Now the flash points: every one of them sits to the left of the band. (Click.) This means: above the flash point, enough vapour is given off to reach the LFL. Every half-empty drum is a flammable headspace waiting for a spark. The hexane point is a trap: at 30 °C the drum headspace is about 25 % hexane, above the UFL of 7.5 %, so it is too rich. But while you empty it, air comes in and the mixture passes through the flammable range. Ask: when is a drum most dangerous? Answer: when it is nearly empty, or just emptied.

  7. What is static charge?

    Start from basics; some people in the room will never have had this explained. Everything is made of atoms with positive nuclei and negative electrons, normally in balance, so objects are neutral. Watch the animation: when two different materials touch, a few electrons move from one to the other. When they are pulled apart, those electrons do not all go back. One material ends up positive, the other negative. That is static charge: a separated charge that stays where it is. (Click through the three points.) The more contact and the faster the separation, the more charge: this is why flow velocity, fine filters and pouring speed matter. And it is not only solids: a solvent flowing along a pipe wall or powder sliding out of a bag charges in exactly the same way. Link back to the balloon demo.

  8. How charge builds up: a leaking bucket

    This is the key idea of the whole session, so slow down. Static is a race between generation and leakage. Think of a bucket: the tap is charge being generated by flow, filtering, pouring or walking. The hole is the path to earth. Left bucket: earthed metal, a big hole, the level never rises, no spark. Right bucket: an isolated drum, a plastic container or low-conductivity toluene, a tiny hole. The level keeps rising until it overflows: the voltage gets high enough that the air breaks down, roughly 3 kV per millimetre, and the stored charge jumps as a spark. Then it starts again. (Click through the points.) Voltage is charge divided by capacitance, V = Q ÷ C. The message: in a chemical plant we cannot stop charge being generated; our job is to make the leak bigger than the tap. Every control we discuss later, earthing, bonding, slow filling, waiting time, is about the size of the tap or the hole.

  9. Static is made whenever things rub or separate

    The animation shows the basic mechanism. When liquid flows through a pipe, charges separate at the wall. The liquid carries one charge (here shown as plus) into the vessel, and the opposite charge stays on the pipe wall. If the pipe is earthed, its charge leaks away safely. The charge in the liquid collects in the receiving vessel. (Click: first row.) These are the process sources: pumping, filters (a filter can make a hundred times more charge than the pipe), splash filling and powder charging. (Click: second row.) And these are everyday sources people forget: stirring two phases, people walking, plastic containers and gas jets. Ask: which of these happened in our plant today?

  10. Charge becomes a hazard when it cannot escape

    Static only matters when it builds up. (Click.) Left: an isolated conductor. A drum on a plastic pallet, a flange with a PTFE gasket, a scoop, a person in ordinary shoes. Metal stores charge like a capacitor and dumps all of it in one spark, which is the most dangerous kind. (Click.) Right: low-conductivity liquids. Toluene and hexane hold their charge even inside an earthed metal tank, because the charge cannot move through the liquid to the wall quickly. That is why we wait before dipping or sampling. Classes from IEC TS 60079-32-1. Ask: can someone name an isolated conductor they saw this week?

  11. Guess: how long does the charge stay?

    A guessing game. Give each table 30 seconds to write three guesses, then reveal one by one. (Click.) Methanol: a few microseconds, because it conducts well; the charge reaches the earthed wall almost instantly. (Click.) Toluene: tens of seconds; with a conductivity around 1 pS/m and a dielectric constant of 2.4, the relaxation time is about 20 seconds. (Click.) Hexane: its conductivity can be 100 times lower, so the charge can stay for many minutes, up to about half an hour. (Click.) The formula: permittivity divided by conductivity. These are typical values; real conductivity depends on purity and water content, and very pure, dry solvent is the worst. Award a chocolate to the closest table.

  12. Every type of discharge beats the solvent

    This chart uses a log scale because the numbers cover four orders of magnitude. (Click.) The shaded band is where our solvent vapours ignite. (Click through each bar.) A brush discharge from a plastic bag or liner: up to about 4 mJ, already well past the solvents. A spark from an unearthed drum or a person: about 20 to 25 mJ. A propagating brush discharge from a non-antistatic FIBC or a plastic-lined pipe: a thousand mJ or more, enough to ignite almost anything, including dust. Message: no static discharge is “too small” for solvent vapour. Typical values from IEC TS 60079-32-1 and NFPA 77.

  13. Work it out: an operator walks to the reactor

    Let us calculate one together. Ask the room to guess first: how much energy is in the spark when an operator touches the reactor manhole? (Click.) The formula is half C V squared. (Click.) A person has about 100 to 300 pF of capacitance; take 200. (Click.) Walking on a non-conductive floor in ordinary shoes easily charges you to 10 kV or more. (Click.) Half times 200 picofarads times 10,000 volts squared is 0.01 joule, 10 millijoules. (Click.) Toluene needs 0.24 mJ: 10 divided by 0.24 is about 40. Methanol 0.14 mJ: about 70 times. The fix is cheap: ESD footwear, a conductive floor, and touching an earthed point before opening anything.

  14. Myth or fact? Round 1

    A quick round. Read each statement, ask everyone to raise a hand for myth or keep it down for fact, then click to reveal. (Click.) One: myth. You feel discharges above about 1 mJ; solvents ignite far below that. (Click.) Two: myth. Conductive solvents relax quickly only when everything around them is earthed. In a plastic jerry can, or with an operator in non-ESD shoes, they can still give a spark. (Click.) Three: fact, with a big but. Hexane at 30 °C saturates the headspace at about 25 %, above its UFL of 7.5 %. The moment you open or empty it, air enters and the mixture passes through the flammable range. Keep score by table if you like.

  15. Control 1: bond and earth every metal part

    Our first and most basic control. (Click through the four cards.) Clamp before opening and remove last. Bond across every break in the metal path: PTFE gaskets and glass-lined sections are insulators. Test with a meter: below 10 ohms for a metal bond, which is a continuity check, and below one megohm to earth, which is the limit for static dissipation per IEC TS 60079-32-1. Where we can, buy clamps that check the earth path and show a green light, and interlock them with the pump. Ask the engineers: when did we last test the earthing points at the solvent yard? Ask the plant head: is there budget for monitored clamps?

  16. Control 2: fill slowly, from the bottom

    The animation compares the two ways of filling. On the left, liquid falls through the vapour space, breaks into droplets and mist and generates charge; that is splash filling. On the right, a dip pipe brings liquid in at the bottom and the surface stays calm. (Click through the four rules.) One: start slowly, 1 m/s or less, until the outlet is submerged. Two: dip pipe to the bottom. Three: avoid air and water slugs; the Barton Solvents explosion we will see shortly involved stop–start filling. Four: give the charge time to relax before you put a dip tape or sample bottle into the tank, especially for low-conductivity solvents. Ask the supervisors: do all our solvent receivers have dip pipes?

  17. Control 3: take the oxygen away

    Our third control is to remove oxygen with nitrogen. The LOC for most solvents is around 10 to 12 percent; methanol is about 10. Good practice, following NFPA 69, is to keep a margin below the LOC: we aim for 8 percent or less and confirm with an oxygen analyser rather than by assumption. (Click.) The formula for pressure purging. (Click.) Example: pressurise with nitrogen to 2 barg, which is 3 bar absolute, then vent to atmosphere. (Click through the bars.) Air is 21 percent. After one cycle, 21 divided by 3 is 7 percent: just under target, no margin. After two cycles, 2.3 percent. Ask: how many purge cycles does our SOP specify, and does anyone check the oxygen afterwards? Remember: an open manhole undoes the inerting.

  18. Control 4: powder into a solvent reactor

    This slide matters most for us. Ask first: who has charged powder into a reactor that already had solvent in it? That is the classic flash fire. The powder and the plastic bag charge up as the powder slides out; a brush discharge from the bag (up to 4 mJ) easily ignites toluene or methanol vapour at the manhole. (Click.) Stop doing these. (Click.) Do these instead. The first option, solids before solvent, costs nothing if the chemistry allows; engineers, check with R and D. Type C FIBCs must be earthed; Type D dissipate charge without earthing. Plant head and executives: closed charging systems need capital, and this is where the risk is.

  19. Control 5: the person is a conductor too

    (Click through the four cards.) The person is often the isolated conductor. ESD shoes only work on a conductive floor, so both need checking. Touching an earthed point first drains your charge safely. Mobile phones are not flameproof. Taking off a synthetic jacket or wiping a surface with a dry cloth can generate several kilovolts. (Click.) The last line is for the managers and executives in the room: people follow what you do, not what the SOP says. If you walk into the solvent store in leather shoes on a phone call, the rule is dead.

  20. Two-minute mystery: the sample bottle

    Read the story slowly. (Click: clues, one at a time.) Then give each table two minutes to agree on the ignition source and every condition that had to be true. Take answers from two or three tables before showing the next slide. Good answers mention: the filter charged the toluene; toluene holds charge; no time was allowed for it to relax; the metal can on a nylon rope is an isolated conductor; and the headspace had air in it because the reactor was opened earlier.

  21. Solved: four things lined up

    (Click through the four answers, and give a point to each table that found it.) Notice that the clamps were on and tested, just like at Barton Solvents. The tank being earthed did not help, because the charge was inside the liquid and the spark came from the sample can, which was not earthed. Ask the supervisors: how do we take samples from solvent reactors today, and how long do we wait after a transfer?

  22. Barton Solvents, Kansas, 17 July 2007

    A real case from the US Chemical Safety Board. Read the summary. (Click.) Then reveal the four conditions one by one and ask the room, before each click, “what else had to be true?” One: the naphtha is nonconductive, like our toluene and hexane. Two: stop–start filling introduced air, adding charge. Three: the headspace was flammable and not inerted. Four: the level float had a loose linkage, an isolated conductor inside the tank, and a spark likely jumped the gap. Eleven residents and a firefighter needed medical attention, and the facility was destroyed. Ask: which of these four could exist in our tank farm today?

  23. The tank was earthed. It still exploded.

    Let this sink in. The CSB found that bonding and grounding of the transfer equipment was likely in place. Most safety data sheets for these solvents only say “bond and ground”, and people stop there. (Click.) For toluene, hexane and heptane, earthing is the start. You also need inerting, slow filling, time to relax before dipping, and every internal part, like floats, bonded. Ask the engineers: are our low-conductivity solvent tanks inerted?

  24. Myth or fact? Round 2

    Second round, same rules. (Click.) One: myth. Humid air makes surfaces of plastic and clothing slightly conductive, so they charge less. But the charge generated inside a liquid flowing through a pipe or filter is the same in any season. (Click.) Two: only if proven. Nitrogen protects only while the oxygen stays below the LOC, which you prove with an analyser. Opening a manhole for charging, or a nitrogen header running low, brings air back. (Click.) Three: fact. Micro filters and strainers generate far more charge than plain pipe; standards ask for residence time downstream of a filter before the liquid enters the tank. Who scored 6 out of 6 across both rounds?

  26. What each of us will do from Monday

    Before showing these, ask each group: what is one thing you can change next week? Write their answers on the board, then reveal these suggestions one by one. (Click four times.) The point is that each role has a different lever. Supervisors control the moment of transfer. Engineers own the hardware. Executives own documents and purchasing. The plant head owns money and example. Close by agreeing a date to review progress, for example in 30 days.

  27. Five rules to take back to the plant

    Read the five rules aloud and ask the room to repeat the first one. Then take questions. If time allows, ask each table to name the one rule they think is most often broken here, and why.
