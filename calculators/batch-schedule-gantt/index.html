Batch Scheduler: Gantt Chart, BCT and STE

Plan a run of batches through a multi-stage train and see the Gantt chart by equipment or by batch. It gives the batch cycle time (BCT, the gap between batch starts), the start-to-end time (STE) of each batch, the output per month and the bottleneck. Analysis holds, cleaning, equipment downtime for modification or maintenance, and batches already in progress are all included.

BCT and STE: two different times

BCT (batch cycle time) is the interval between one batch starting and the next one starting. It decides output: at a BCT of 16 hours the plant makes 45 batches in 30 days, whatever happens downstream. STE (start-to-end time) is how long one batch takes from its first charge to final release, including every wait and analysis. It decides how many batches are in progress at once and how long a customer order takes.

The shortest possible BCT is the occupancy of the bottleneck equipment per batch: its stage time, plus any analysis while the material stays in it, plus cleaning, shared between parallel units. The scheduler shows that figure next to the BCT the plan actually achieves. When the two differ, something is holding the bottleneck: usually material waiting for the next stage, or downtime.

How the schedule is built

  • Batches are taken in order: the batches already in progress first, from the stage they are in, then the new batches. Each stage starts as soon as the batch has finished the previous stage and its analysis, and the equipment is free and clean.
  • Analysis held in equipment (the box ticked): the equipment stays occupied during the analysis and until the next stage takes the material. That is right for a reaction mass waiting for an in-process check, or a dryer waiting for the LOD result. If the next stage is busy, the time spent waiting shows as a dashed bar.
  • Analysis not held: the material is unloaded, for example wet cake in drums or packed product awaiting QC release, so the equipment is free for the next batch. The analysis still delays the batch, and for the last stage it is included in the STE.
  • Downtime blocks the equipment for the hours entered. A stage is never started if it would run into downtime; it waits until after it.
  • Minimum gap between starts holds back new batches. Without it, the first stage starts batches as fast as it can, which can pile up work in progress in front of a slower stage. Setting the gap to the bottleneck time gives a steadier schedule with less waiting.

The plant is assumed to run round the clock. For shift working, add the non-working hours as downtime on the equipment concerned.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between BCT and STE?

BCT (batch cycle time) is the gap between one batch starting and the next one starting; it sets the output. STE (start-to-end time) is how long one batch takes from its first charge to release, including waits and analysis; it sets the work in progress and the lead time.

Why is my BCT longer than the bottleneck time?

Material held in a piece of equipment while it waits for the next stage, analysis holds in the equipment, cleaning and downtime all add to the time the bottleneck is occupied. The dashed bars in the chart show where batches are waiting.

How do I schedule parallel equipment such as two dryers?

List both in the stage's equipment field separated by a comma, for example Dryer A, Dryer B. Each batch goes to whichever is free first, and the hours per batch are shared between them.
