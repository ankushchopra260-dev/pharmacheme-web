Batch Scheduler: Gantt Chart, BCT and STE

Lay out a campaign stage by stage on a day chart, the way it is planned on a spreadsheet: one row per stage, BCT and STE in hours, the number of batches (calculated for a regular product, entered for a campaign), batches already in progress, the hours before the first batch for cleaning, transfer, analysis or modification, and whether a stage starts after the first or the last batch of the stage before. A detailed mode schedules named equipment in hours. In the detailed mode you can plan a run of batches through a multi-stage train and see the Gantt chart equipment-wise or batch-wise. It gives the batch cycle time (BCT, the gap between batch starts), the start-to-end time (STE) of each batch, the output in a 28-day month (or any number of days) and the bottleneck. In-process tests, final product analysis, cleaning, 8-hour shifts, equipment downtime for modification or maintenance, and batches already in progress are all included.

BCT and STE: two different times

BCT (batch cycle time) is the interval between one batch starting and the next one starting. It decides output: at a BCT of 16 hours the plant makes 42 batches in 28 days, whatever happens downstream. In-process tests are done while the material is still in the equipment, so they are part of the stage time and of the BCT. STE (start-to-end time) is how long one batch takes from its first charge to final release, including every wait and the final product analysis after unloading. It decides how many batches are in progress at once and how long a customer order takes.

The shortest possible BCT is the occupancy of the bottleneck equipment per batch: its stage time with in-process tests, plus cleaning, shared between parallel units. The scheduler shows that figure next to the BCT the plan actually achieves. When the two differ, something is holding the bottleneck: usually material waiting for the next stage, or downtime.

Stage-wise mode

Each stage is one row, with times in hours and the chart drawn in days. The first batch of a stage starts after its hours before first batch (the grey block: cleaning, transfer of material from another site, analysis, equipment modification); each following batch starts BCT hours after the one before and lasts STE hours.

A stage usually has more than one piece of equipment: a reactor, then a filter, then a dryer. BCT is the longest time any one of them holds a batch, so a new batch can be charged every BCT hours while the previous one is still moving through the rest of the stage. When BCT is shorter than STE the batches overlap and are drawn one under the other.

Choose the kind of product. A running product has been made month after month, so the line is already full on the first day. Each stage charges a batch every BCT hours, giving 24 h × days ÷ BCT batches (14 in 28 days at a BCT of 48 h). Batches charged last month and still running are the opening WIP: they are shown in grey and their output counts in this month. Batches charged this month that are still running at month end are the closing WIP, outlined in red; their output falls in the next month. Both are worked out by the tool.

A new product or a campaign starts with empty equipment, so there is no opening WIP. Enter the number of batches for each stage, the hours before the first batch and whether a stage starts after batch #01 or the last batch of the stage before. Batches still running at month end show as closing WIP, and any that start after it are listed as next month.

A stage can start after batch #01 of the previous stage, when the stages run on different equipment and the first lot can move on at once, or after the last batch of the previous stage, when the stage needs the equipment the previous stage was using, or needs the whole lot. In the first case a batch also never starts before the matching batch of the previous stage has finished.

Detailed mode: how the schedule is built

  • Batches are taken in order: the batches already in progress first, from the stage they are in, then the new batches. A batch in progress holds its equipment from the start of the plan, whatever the shift or downtime. Each stage starts as soon as the batch has finished the previous stage and the equipment is free and clean.
  • In-process tests (an IPC sample, an LOD check) belong in the stage time: the equipment is occupied while the result comes back.
  • Material waits in the equipment (box ticked): if the next stage is busy, the material stays put and the equipment is still occupied, shown as a dashed bar. That is right for a reaction mass in a reactor. Untick it when the material is unloaded into drums or a holding tank and the equipment is freed.
  • Final product analysis starts after the last stage unloads the product. The equipment is free, so it does not affect the BCT, but it adds to the STE: a batch counts as released only when the analysis is done.
  • Downtime blocks the equipment for the hours entered. A stage is never started if it would run into downtime; it waits until after it.
  • Minimum gap between starts holds back new batches. Without it, the first stage starts batches as fast as it can, which can pile up work in progress in front of a slower stage. Setting the gap to the bottleneck time gives a steadier schedule with less waiting.

Shifts and the month

The BCT shown is the average gap between batches in the plan, taken from both batch starts and unloading of the last stage, and never less than the bottleneck allows; output is worked out from it. The default is three 8-hour shifts a day, starting at 06:00, so the plant runs round the clock. With one or two shifts, a stage can start only inside a working shift; a stage that has started runs to the end, as a reaction or a drying cycle would. Hours outside the shifts are shaded in the chart, and the A, B and C shifts are marked along the top. Output is worked out for a 28-day month by default; change the days to match how you report.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between BCT and STE?

BCT (batch cycle time) is the gap between one batch starting and the next one starting; it sets the output. STE (start-to-end time) is how long one batch takes from its first charge to release, including waits and analysis; it sets the work in progress and the lead time.

Why is my BCT longer than the bottleneck time?

Material held in a piece of equipment while it waits for the next stage, downtime and, with fewer than three shifts, stages waiting for the next shift all add to the time between batches. The dashed bars in the chart show where batches are waiting.

How do I schedule parallel equipment such as two dryers?

List both in the stage's equipment field separated by a comma, for example Dryer A, Dryer B. Each batch goes to whichever is free first, and the hours per batch are shared between them.
