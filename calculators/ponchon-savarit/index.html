Ponchon-Savarit Calculator

Designs a binary distillation column with the enthalpy-composition (Ponchon-Savarit) method. Unlike McCabe-Thiele it does not assume equal molar flows: it follows the real liquid and vapour enthalpies, so the vapour and liquid flows change from stage to stage and the condenser and reboiler duties come straight from the diagram. How the Ponchon-Savarit method works

The method uses an enthalpy-composition diagram: the saturated liquid enthalpy hL against x, and the saturated vapour enthalpy HV against y, for the light component. A straight tie line joins a liquid and the vapour in equilibrium with it: that is one stage. Energy and material balances around the top and bottom of the column turn into two fixed difference points. Every operating line in the rectifying section passes through ΔD, and every one in the stripping section through ΔB. Stepping alternately along tie lines and operating lines counts the stages.

ΔD at (xD, Q′D) with Q′D = hD + QC/D
ΔB at (xB, Q′B) with Q′B = hB − QR/B
Feed point F (zF, hF) lies on the straight line ΔD – ΔB: F·hF = D·Q′D + B·Q′B
Total condenser: QC = D(R + 1)(HV(xD) − hD)

Why use it instead of McCabe-Thiele?

McCabe-Thiele assumes constant molar overflow: the same moles of vapour rise through every stage of a section. That holds when the two components have similar molar latent heats and heat of mixing is small. When the latent heats differ (methanol 8,400 against water 9,700 kcal/kmol) or the mixture releases or absorbs heat on mixing, the flows change down the column. Ponchon-Savarit follows those changes, and gives the condenser and reboiler duties directly. The calculator shows the McCabe-Thiele answer on the same equilibrium data next to every result, so you can see when the simpler method is good enough.

Minimum reflux and feed stage

Raising ΔD means more condenser duty and more reflux. At the minimum reflux an operating line through ΔD or ΔB coincides with a tie line, and the stages become infinite. The calculator finds it by checking the whole column, so it also catches pinches away from the feed. The optimum feed stage is where switching from ΔD to ΔB gives the larger step.

Enthalpy data

  • Two solvents, real mixture: bubble and dew temperatures and the equilibrium curve from modified UNIFAC; liquid heat capacities and latent heats from the solvent pages, with latent heat corrected for temperature; heat of mixing from the temperature change of the activity coefficients.
  • Two solvents, ideal: the same without heat of mixing.
  • Constant relative volatility with two latent heats: the clearest way to see the effect of unequal latent heats. With equal latent heats it gives exactly the McCabe-Thiele answer.
  • Your own table: rows of x, y, hL, HV from a handbook or a textbook enthalpy-composition chart, for systems such as ammonia–water.
Where the method stops

The column is taken as adiabatic with saturated reflux and no heat losses. Mixtures that separate into two liquid layers at their boiling point (such as toluene–water) are refused here; use the McCabe-Thiele calculator, which handles the two-liquid range, or a full process simulation. Model enthalpies are a design screen; use measured heat-of-mixing data for final design of strongly non-ideal systems.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Ponchon-Savarit method?

A graphical method for binary distillation on an enthalpy-composition diagram. It uses energy balances as well as material balances, so it does not need the constant molar overflow assumption of McCabe-Thiele, and it gives the condenser and reboiler duties directly.

What is the difference between Ponchon-Savarit and McCabe-Thiele?

McCabe-Thiele assumes the molar vapour and liquid flows are constant in each section, so its operating lines are straight. Ponchon-Savarit uses the real enthalpies, so the flows change from stage to stage and the operating lines on the x-y diagram are curved. The two give the same answer when the molar latent heats are equal and heat of mixing is zero.

What are the difference points ΔD and ΔB?

Fixed points on the enthalpy-composition diagram at the distillate and bottoms compositions. Their heights include the condenser and reboiler duties per mole of product, and every operating line in the top or bottom section passes through one of them.

How is the condenser duty found?

From the height of ΔD above the distillate enthalpy: QC = D (Q′D − hD). For a total condenser with saturated reflux this equals D (R + 1) times the latent heat of the top vapour.

When is McCabe-Thiele good enough?

When the components have similar molar latent heats (within about 10 %) and a small heat of mixing, for example benzene and toluene. Compare the stage counts shown by this calculator; if they agree, the simpler method is fine.
