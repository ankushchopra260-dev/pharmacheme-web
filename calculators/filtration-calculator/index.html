Filtration Evaluation Calculator

Turn a bench-scale Büchner filtration trial into specific cake resistance and flux, with a plain Good / Moderate / Slow read on filterability.

How to Use

Run a small Büchner or Nutsche trial at a known vacuum/pressure, record filtrate volume against time, and enter the trial data along with filter area and slurry concentration. The tool fits the standard cake-filtration relationship to estimate specific cake resistance and expected flux.

The Formulas

For cake filtration at constant pressure, Ruth’s equation links time and filtrate volume:

t / V = (μ α c / 2A²ΔP) × V + μ Rm / (A ΔP)
α = 2 A² ΔP × slope / (μ c)
Flux = filtrate volume / (A × time)

Plot t/V against V from your trial readings; the straight line’s slope gives the specific cake resistance α (m/kg). Here A is the filter area, ΔP the applied vacuum or pressure, μ the filtrate viscosity and c the mass of dry solids per volume of filtrate. The intercept gives the cloth resistance Rm.

Worked Example

A 5 cm Büchner (A = 0.00196 m²) at 500 mmHg vacuum (66,660 Pa) filters a slurry with c = 46 kg/m³ and filtrate viscosity 0.3 cP. The t/V against V readings give a slope of 2.77 × 1010 s/m6.

α = 2 × 0.00196² × 66,660 × 2.77 × 1010 / (0.0003 × 46)
α ≈ 1.0 × 1012 m/kg: just above 1012, a very slow-filtering cake

650 mL collected in 20 minutes is a flux of 0.65 / (0.00196 × 20) ≈ 16.6 L/m²·min.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many readings do I need?

At least five, spread over the whole filtration. Leave out the first minute or two, before the cake has formed, if those points sit off the straight line.

Can I scale this up to a Nutsche filter or ANFD?

Yes, as a first estimate: use the same α, c and μ with the plant filter area and pressure in Ruth’s equation. Compressible cakes give a higher α at higher pressure, so test at the plant pressure if you can.

What makes a cake filter slowly?

Fine particles, a wide size spread and compressible solids. Crystallisation changes (slower cooling, seeding, ageing) usually help more than a bigger filter.

Engineering Notes & Assumptions

Bench-scale trials don't always scale linearly to production filters. Cake compressibility, medium fouling, and pressure differences between bench and plant equipment can all shift the real result. Use this as a first-pass filterability screen, and confirm with a larger trial before committing to production filter sizing.
