NPSH Calculator

NPSH available at a centrifugal pump, drawn as a line diagram from the source vessel through the pump to the destination. Pick the source (open tank, suction lift, nitrogen-padded vessel, vacuum receiver, column bottoms or hot condensate tank), the liquid and the flow, then enter the level, pipe sizes, lengths and fittings. The diagram shows NPSH available against the pump's NPSHr, the pump head and power, and the calculator gives how low the level, how hot the liquid or how high the flow can go before the margin is lost.

How NPSH available is worked out

NPSH available is the head at the pump inlet above the vapour pressure of the liquid, in metres of the liquid being pumped:

NPSHa = (Psurface − Pvapour) / ρg + Z − hf

  • Psurface is the absolute pressure on the liquid surface: atmospheric pressure at the site altitude for a vented tank, the pad pressure plus atmospheric for a nitrogen-padded vessel, the absolute pressure for a vacuum vessel.
  • Pvapour is the vapour pressure at the pumping temperature, from the Antoine constants on the PharmaChemE solvent pages (two constant sets for water, so that condensate above 100 °C comes out right).
  • Z is the lowest liquid level above the pump centre line; it is negative for a suction lift. Use the low-level alarm or trip level, not the normal level.
  • hf is the suction line loss at the flow: pipe friction from the Darcy–Weisbach equation with the Churchill friction factor, plus K values for the tank outlet (0.5), each 90° elbow (0.75), each open valve (0.2) and the strainer (2 clean, 8 half blocked or for a foot valve).

NPSHr from the vendor curve is the suction head at which the pump head has fallen 3 % because of cavitation, so a pump running at NPSHa = NPSHr is already cavitating. Keep a margin: a common plant rule is at least 1 m at the highest flow, more for hot or boiling liquids. ANSI/HI 9.6.1 gives margin ratios by application.

The six arrangements

Open tank, flooded suction

The easy case: atmospheric pressure gives about 10 m of water, the liquid level adds to it and cold liquids have little vapour pressure. Trouble comes from a long or undersized suction line, a blocked strainer, or a solvent that is warmer or more volatile than the pump was selected for.

Suction lift from a sump or pit

The lift comes straight off the atmospheric head, and the suction line is under vacuum. Lifts beyond 4 to 5 m of water are rarely practical, and much less for warm or volatile liquids. The pump has to be self-priming or have a foot valve, and every joint on the suction side must be air tight.

Closed vessel under nitrogen

A nitrogen pad raises the surface pressure and adds head, but only while it is held. If the pad is vented to transfer liquid or the regulator fails, the NPSH falls back to the open-tank value. Check the lowest pressure the vessel actually runs at; the calculator gives the lowest pressure at which the margin still holds.

Vacuum receiver

Distillate in a receiver under vacuum is close to its boiling point at the receiver pressure, so pressure and vapour pressure nearly cancel and the liquid height does almost all the work. Mount the pump well below the receiver, keep the line short and watch the distillate temperature: a few degrees warmer can take the whole margin.

Liquid at its boiling point

For a column bottoms or reboiler pump the liquid is saturated, so NPSHa is just the liquid level minus the suction loss. That is why these pumps sit several metres below the column, with a large, short and insulated suction line and no high points where vapour can collect. The calculator shows the boiling temperature at the vessel pressure.

Hot condensate tank

Water at 95 °C has a vapour pressure of about 634 mmHg, so a vented tank gives only about 1.8 m of pressure head before the liquid level is counted. Condensate pumps cavitate when flash steam from the return lines heats the tank to boiling; mount the pump low and keep the tank vented.

How far it can go

For the pump and margin entered, the calculator finds the lowest liquid level (or highest lift), the highest liquid temperature, the highest flow through the suction line and, for closed vessels, the lowest vessel pressure. NPSH available falls as flow rises, because the suction loss grows with the square of the velocity, and NPSHr rises with flow too, so check the vendor curve at the highest flow the pump will see, not only at the rated point.

Pump head on the discharge side

The discharge line runs from the pump to the destination at the height and pressure entered. Pump head = (destination pressure − source pressure) / ρg + (destination height − source level) + suction loss + discharge loss. The discharge loss counts the elbows and open valves entered, a check valve (K = 2) and the exit into the vessel (K = 1). Power is ρgQH divided by the pump efficiency. Pipe sizes are schedule 40 inside diameters.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between NPSH available and NPSH required?

NPSH available (NPSHa) is set by the plant: the pressure at the liquid surface, the liquid level, the vapour pressure of the liquid and the loss in the suction line. NPSH required (NPSHr) is set by the pump and read from the vendor curve; it is the suction head at which the pump head has already dropped 3 %. NPSHa must be higher than NPSHr with a margin.

How much NPSH margin should I keep?

A common plant rule is at least 1 m above NPSHr at the highest flow, and more for hot liquids, liquids at their boiling point and large or high-speed pumps. ANSI/HI 9.6.1 gives margin ratios by application. The calculator lets you set the margin you want and shows the limits for it.

Why is NPSH so low for a column bottoms pump?

The liquid at the bottom of a column is at its boiling point, so the vessel pressure and the vapour pressure cancel. Only the liquid height above the pump minus the suction loss is left, which is why bottoms pumps are placed several metres below the column with a short, straight suction line.

Does a vacuum vessel always cause cavitation?

No, but the margin is small. The pressure in the vessel is close to the vapour pressure of the liquid in it, so the liquid level does most of the work. A pump below a vacuum receiver with enough liquid height runs well; the same pump cavitates when the level drops or the distillate comes in warmer.
