Heat of Reaction Calculator

Draw or name the reactants and products and get the heat of reaction at 25 °C in kcal, worked three ways: from measured heats of formation, by Benson group additivity, and by bond energies. Each component can be gas, liquid or solid, and the working is shown so you can check it.

How the number is worked out

The heat of reaction is the sum of the heats of formation of the products minus those of the reactants, each multiplied by its coefficient. The calculator picks the best available heat of formation for every component, in this order:

  1. Measured value. A built-in table of about 6,700 compounds with gas, liquid and solid heats of formation from the Active Thermochemical Tables (ATcT), the CRC Handbook, the NIST WebBook, NIST-JANAF and the API Technical Data Book. Where several sources exist they are compared: ATcT is preferred, otherwise the median. When published values disagree by more than 3 kcal/mol, or a published value is more than 8 kcal/mol from the group-additivity estimate, the published value is still used but the card shows the conflict and the uncertainty is widened.
  2. Group additivity. For compounds not in the table, the gas-phase heat of formation is built from Benson group values: each carbon, oxygen or nitrogen with its neighbours is one group, plus corrections for ring strain, cis double bonds, ortho substituents and branching (gauche). Most values for C, H, O and N are Benson’s own (Thermochemical Kinetics, 1976); chlorine, bromine and fluorine groups come from the RMG database. About 25 groups missing from both (among them methyl nitro, aryl nitro, formate, carbamate, urea, the tertiary amide nitrogen and the iodides) were derived here from one to seven well-measured compounds each, as Benson did. The card lists the source of every group used.
  3. Phase. Group additivity gives a gas value. For a liquid, the heat of vaporisation at 25 °C is subtracted. It is measured where the table has both phases, otherwise estimated from the structure with a model fitted to 1,160 compounds. For a solid without a measured value, the liquid value is used and the card warns that the heat of fusion (usually 3 to 8 kcal/mol) is missing.

You can type your own heat of formation for any component, for example a value from your own calorimetry or a supplier’s data, and it overrides everything else.

The bond energy method

The bond energy method counts the bonds broken in the reactants and the bonds formed in the products: ΔHr ≈ Σ bonds broken − Σ bonds formed. It is the quickest way to see where the heat comes from, and the calculator lists the net bonds on each side.

Instead of one average value per bond, the table separates bonds by their surroundings: a C=O in a ketone, an ester and CO2; an O–H in an alcohol, an acid, a phenol and water; aromatic C–C and C–H; and so on. The values were fitted to the measured gas-phase heats of formation of 863 compounds, and small molecules such as H2, O2, Cl2, HCl, water and ammonia use their exact values. Even so, bond energies cannot see ring strain, resonance or branching, so they are a check and a teaching tool, not the headline number. Ethylene oxide is the classic case: bond energies miss its ring strain and the answer is off by over 40 kcal/mol.

The reaction tests are textbook industrial reactions (hydrogenations, esterifications and hydrolyses, chlorinations, nitrations, oxidations, Friedel–Crafts reactions, hydration, etherification and amination), compared in the gas phase where every species has a reliable measured value. Some of the derived groups were fitted to compounds that are also in these tests, so the figures for ordinary organics are slightly flattering. The ± next to the result combines the typical error of each value used, larger for halogenated, fluorinated and fused-ring compounds; treat it as an indication of the likely error rather than a strict bound.

What it does not do

  • Solutions and ions. Values are for pure components. Heats of solution, dilution and mixing are not included, so a neutralisation in water, a reaction in concentrated sulfuric acid or a salt formation in a solvent needs those added separately. The thermochemistry article gives typical values.
  • Element coverage. Group additivity covers C, H, O, N, F, Cl, Br and I in common bonding. Sulfur, phosphorus, silicon, boron, metals, organometallics and aromatic nitrogen rings such as pyridine have no group values here; use a measured value if the table has one, or enter your own.
  • Temperature. All values are at 25 °C. For most reactions the change up to a typical reaction temperature is small, but it matters when the heat of reaction itself is small (an esterification is only about 1 kcal/mol) or when a product such as water leaves as vapour at the reaction temperature: then choose Gas for it.
  • Design and safety. For cooling design and thermal safety, measure the real heat of reaction by reaction calorimetry and check decomposition by DSC. See how to read DSC, ARC and RC1 data.
