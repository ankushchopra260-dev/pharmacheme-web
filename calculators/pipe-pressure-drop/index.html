Pipe Pressure Drop and Pump NPSH

Pressure drop in a liquid line with pipe friction, fittings and valves by the 3-K method, entrance, exit and elevation, and the NPSH available at your pump.

  • Pressure drop
  • NPSH available
  • How it works

How the pressure drop is calculated

Pipe friction uses the Darcy–Weisbach equation, ΔP = f (L/D) ρu²/2, with the Churchill (1977) friction factor. Churchill covers laminar, transitional and turbulent flow in one equation and agrees with Colebrook within about 0.5 % in turbulent flow.

Fittings and valves use Darby’s 3-K method: K = K1/Re + K∞(1 + Kd/Dn0.3), with Dn the nominal size in inches. A single fixed K for an elbow is fine for one pipe size but under-predicts losses in small lines and at low Reynolds number; the 3-K method corrects for both. Entrance from a tank is K = 0.5 and exit into a tank K = 1.0.

ΔPtotal = f (L/D) ρu²/2 + ΣK ρu²/2 + ρ g Δz + fixed drops

NPSH available

A pump cavitates when the pressure at its eye falls to the liquid’s vapour pressure. NPSH available is the head above vapour pressure at the pump inlet:

NPSHa = (Pvessel − Pvapour) / ρg + liquid level above pump − suction line loss

The vapour pressure comes from the Antoine constants on our solvent pages. Hot liquids and vacuum vessels are where pumps cavitate: a methanol receiver under vacuum at 30 °C has only a few metres of NPSH available, all from the liquid level.

On the plant

If a transfer pump from a distillation receiver “loses suction” at the end of a batch, the level has dropped and NPSHa with it. Lower the pump, keep a minimum level, shorten and enlarge the suction line, or cool the liquid.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate pressure drop in a pipe with fittings?

Add pipe friction from the Darcy–Weisbach equation, the losses of each fitting and valve (sum of K times ρu²/2), the elevation change ρgΔz, and any fixed drops such as a control valve or filter.

What is the 3-K method?

A fitting loss method by Darby in which K = K1/Re + K∞(1 + Kd/Dn^0.3). It accounts for both the Reynolds number and the pipe size, so it is more accurate than a single K value for small pipes and viscous liquids.

How do you calculate NPSH available?

NPSHa = (vessel pressure − vapour pressure)/(ρg) + liquid level above the pump inlet − suction line losses, all in metres of liquid. It must exceed the NPSH required by the pump with a margin.
