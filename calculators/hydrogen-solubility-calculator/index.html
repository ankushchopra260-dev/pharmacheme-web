Hydrogen Solubility in Solvents

Hydrogen dissolves according to Henry's law: the mole fraction in the liquid equals the hydrogen partial pressure divided by a Henry's constant that depends on the solvent and temperature. At 25 °C, each bar of hydrogen dissolves about 8 mg/L in methanol, 6 mg/L in toluene, 6 mg/L in IPA and only 1.6 mg/L in water. Pick a solvent, temperature and pressure below; every constant comes from published measurements, and the result says whether you are inside the measured range.

The method

pH2 = Ptotal, abs − pvap, solvent(T)
xH2 = pH2 / H(T)    ln H = a + b/T (fitted to the measured points)
C (mol per L of solvent) = x / (1 − x) × ρ / M    mg/L = C × 2.016 × 1000 H in MPa, ρ solvent density (kg/m³), M solvent molar mass (g/mol). Vapour pressure from the Antoine constants on our solvent pages. Water uses the IUPAC evaluated equation ln x = −48.1611 + 55.2845/(T/100) + 16.8893 ln(T/100) at 1 atm.

Worked example

A hydrogenation in 2,000 L of methanol at 25 °C under 4 bar g hydrogen.

Total pressure = 4 + 1.013 = 5.013 bar a; methanol vapour pressure at 25 °C = 0.169 bar
pH2 = 4.844 bar = 0.4844 MPa; H(25 °C) = 626 MPa
x = 0.4844 / 626 = 7.74 × 10−4
C = 7.74 × 10−4 × 792 / 32.04 = 0.0191 mol/L = 38.6 mg/L
In 2,000 L: about 77 g of dissolved hydrogen

77 g is tiny next to what a reaction consumes: reducing 1 kmol of a nitro group to an amine takes 3 kmol, about 6 kg, of hydrogen. The liquid holds less than 2 % of that at any moment, so the gas must keep dissolving as fast as it reacts. That is why plant hydrogenations are usually limited by gas–liquid mass transfer (kLa), not by how much hydrogen the solvent can hold. See gas–liquid reactions and kLa.

What the numbers show

  • Pressure: solubility rises in direct proportion to the hydrogen partial pressure. Doubling the pressure doubles the dissolved hydrogen and, at the same kLa, the maximum transfer rate.
  • Temperature: in organic solvents hydrogen dissolves better when hot, about 15–25 % more at 50 °C than at 25 °C. In water it is the opposite up to about 50 °C. At a fixed total pressure the solvent's vapour pressure eats into the hydrogen partial pressure, so methanol or acetone near their boiling points gain little.
  • Solvent: per mole, hydrocarbons dissolve the most and polar aprotic solvents (DMF, NMP) the least. Per litre, small molecules like methanol and acetone come out high.
  • Water in the solvent: water dissolves 2 to 7 times less hydrogen per litre than these solvents, so a wet solvent holds less than the dry one.

Frequently asked questions

How much hydrogen dissolves in methanol?

About 8 mg/L per bar of hydrogen at 25 °C (mole fraction 1.6 × 10−4 at 1 atm). At 4 bar g and 25 °C that is about 39 mg/L, or 77 g in 2,000 L.

Does hydrogen solubility increase with temperature?

In organic solvents, yes, by roughly 15–25 % from 25 to 50 °C. In water it falls from 0 to about 50 °C, then rises slowly.

Which solvent dissolves the most hydrogen?

Hydrocarbons per mole (n-hexane about 7 × 10−4 at 1 atm, 25 °C). Per litre, hexane, methanol and acetone are high. Water is lowest, about 1.6 mg/L per atm.

Engineering notes and assumptions

Henry's law with a constant fitted as ln H = a + b/T to the measured points; no pressure correction (good to about 100 bar). Pure, dry solvents; dissolved reactants, products, salts and water change the result. Densities at 20 °C. Accuracy is about ±5–10 % inside the measured range and worse outside it, which the calculator flags. Solvents without a published source we could verify (ethyl acetate, acetic acid, DCM, cyclohexane) are left out rather than estimated.
