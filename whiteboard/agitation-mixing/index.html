Agitation and Mixing: Whiteboard Lesson

Six questions on agitation: how power climbs with speed, why baffles matter, which impeller lifts solids or disperses gas, why blend time grows on scale-up and where to dose a fast reaction. Pick an answer, hear what Bubbles thinks of it, then watch the whiteboard explain it. Turn the sound on; there are captions too.

The six questions and answers

The same lesson as text, for reading or revision. More lessons: all whiteboard lessons. More interview questions: Interview Prep.

In turbulent flow, you double the agitator speed. What happens to the power draw?

In turbulent flow, agitator power P = Np × ρ × N³ × D⁵, where Np is the power number, N the speed and D the impeller diameter. Doubling the speed raises power 2³ = 8 times; doubling the diameter at the same speed raises it 2⁵ = 32 times. Check motor current whenever agitator speed is changed.

A tank with no baffles, agitator at high speed. What forms?

Without baffles the liquid rotates with the impeller like a solid body, giving poor top-to-bottom mixing, and the surface forms a vortex that can draw air or vapour into the batch. Wall baffles, usually four about one-tenth of the vessel diameter wide, break the rotation into axial flow. Glass-lined reactors have only one or two baffles and vortex more easily.

You need to keep heavy solids off the bottom. Which impeller is the usual choice?

A pitched-blade turbine pumping down sends flow to the base, across the bottom and up the walls, lifting solids. Mount it low, about a quarter of the tank diameter off the base, to reach the corners. Radial disc turbines throw flow sideways and are better suited to gas dispersion than solids suspension.

Scale up 10 times in diameter at the same power per volume. What happens to blend time?

At constant power per volume, impeller speed scales with diameter to the power −2/3, and in turbulent flow blend time scales with 1/speed, so it grows with diameter to the power 2/3. Ten times the diameter gives about 4.6 times the blend time: a 10 s lab blend becomes about 46 s in the plant. Fast reactions may give more impurities on scale-up unless dosing is slower or into a better-mixed spot.

A fast reaction where local excess makes impurities. Where should the reagent go in?

For a fast reaction where local excess gives impurities, dose below the surface near the impeller, where turbulence spreads the reagent quickly. Dosing onto the surface near the wall leaves a rich patch in slow flow, giving side reactions a chance. Dose only with the agitator running and proven, fit the dip pipe with a siphon break so the batch cannot suck back, and dose slowly enough that each addition is mixed before the next arrives.

Gas is fed through a ring sparger under the impeller. Which impeller handles the most gas?

With gas from a sparger under the impeller, a radial disc turbine handles the most gas: it throws gas and liquid outward through a high-shear zone, making small bubbles. Pitched-blade turbines flood at lower gas rates. If the gas rate is too high for the speed, the impeller floods, spinning in a gas pocket while gas rises straight through; large bubbles erupting around the shaft, with power falling further as gas increases, are the signs. Reduce gas, or raise speed if the motor has margin.
