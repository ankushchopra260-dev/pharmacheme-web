Control Valves: Whiteboard Lesson

Six questions on control valves: what Kv means, how flow follows pressure drop, why an oversized valve controls badly, choosing a characteristic, cavitation noise and what a positioner does. The six questions and answers

The same lesson as text, for reading or revision. More lessons: all whiteboard lessons. More interview questions: Interview Prep.

A control valve has a Kv of 10. What does that mean?

Kv is the flow of water in m³/h through a valve with a 1 bar pressure drop across it. For other conditions, flow Q = Kv × √(ΔP ÷ SG), with Q in m³/h and ΔP in bar. Cv, the US equivalent in gallons per minute at 1 psi, is about 1.16 × Kv. Catalogue Kv values are for the fully open valve.

Same valve opening, but the pressure drop across it rises 4 times. What happens to flow?

At a fixed opening, flow through a valve goes with the square root of the pressure drop, so four times the pressure drop doubles the flow. Doubling the flow at the same opening would need four times the pressure drop, which is rarely available.

A new control valve runs at only 10 % open at normal flow. What's the problem?

A valve running at only 10 % open is oversized. Near the seat small movements cause large flow changes, so control is jumpy, and high velocity through the small gap wears the seat and plug. Size the valve on real flows so the range from minimum to maximum flow sits within roughly 20 to 80 % open, with normal flow often around 50 to 70 %; control valves are often a size or two smaller than the line.

The valve takes a small share of the system pressure drop. Which characteristic suits?

A valve's characteristic is how flow changes with opening at constant pressure drop. In most systems pipe friction takes more pressure as flow rises, leaving less for the valve. An equal percentage characteristic opens slowly at first and faster later, which compensates, so the installed response is closer to linear. If the valve takes most of the system pressure drop, a linear characteristic suits better.

A liquid control valve sounds like gravel flowing through it. What's going on?

Liquid accelerates through the narrowest gap in the valve and its pressure falls sharply. If it drops below the vapour pressure, bubbles form, then collapse as pressure recovers downstream, sounding like gravel and eroding the trim and nearby pipe. Fixes include sharing the pressure drop across two valves or an orifice, anti-cavitation trim, or locating the valve where pressure is higher.

What does a valve positioner do?

Packing friction can stop a valve stem short of where the control signal wants it. A positioner compares the signal with the actual stem position and adds or vents actuator air until they match, reducing stick-slip and speeding response. Smart positioners also report travel and friction, helping spot a sticking valve early.
