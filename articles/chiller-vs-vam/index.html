Chiller vs VAM: Calculations and Running Cost

Utilities · Refrigeration · Updated October 2026

Chilled water and brine run crystallisers, reactor cooling, solvent condensers and HVAC. Most plants make it with electric chillers; some also run a vapour absorption machine (VAM) on steam or waste heat. This article shows how to measure what a chiller really delivers, how much steam a VAM needs, and how to decide which one should run.

How each machine works Basics

Electric (vapour compression) chiller: refrigerant boils in the evaporator and takes heat from the chilled water; the compressor raises its pressure; it condenses in the condenser and gives the heat to cooling water; an expansion valve drops the pressure again. The compressor is where the electricity goes.

Vapour absorption machine: water is the refrigerant. It boils at very low pressure (about 8 mbar abs, around 4 °C) in the evaporator and chills the water. Concentrated lithium bromide solution absorbs the vapour. A generator heated by steam or hot water boils the water back out of the solution, and a condenser turns it to liquid again. Instead of a compressor, heat does the work. A double-effect machine uses the vapour from a high-temperature generator to heat a second, low-temperature generator, which is why it gets nearly twice the cooling from the same steam.

Measuring a chiller in the field Working level

Cooling (kW) = flow (m³/h) × ρ / 3,600 × cp × (Treturn − Tsupply)
TR = kW / 3.517    (water: TR ≈ m³/h × ΔT / 3.024)
kW/TR = compressor power / TR    COP = cooling kW / power kW = 3.517 / (kW/TR)

Example 1: water chiller

120 m³/h of chilled water from 12 °C to 7 °C: 120 × 5 / 3.024 ≈ 198 TR (698 kW). The compressor draws 140 kW: 0.71 kW/TR, COP 5.0. That is a normal water-cooled screw chiller.

Example 2: glycol brine

60 m³/h of 30 % ethylene glycol (ρ ≈ 1,040 kg/m³, cp ≈ 3.7 kJ/kg·K) from 0 °C to −5 °C:

Cooling = 60 × 1,040 / 3,600 × 3.7 × 5 ≈ 321 kW ≈ 91 TR
Compressor power 180 kW → about 2.0 kW/TR

That is not a bad chiller. Making cold at −5 °C simply takes far more work than at 7 °C, so only compare brine chillers with other brine chillers at the same temperatures. And use the right cp: treating glycol as water here would overstate the load by about 9 %.

Measurement tips: temperature sensors in thermowells close to the machine, calibrated together (0.25 °C error on a 5 °C ΔT is 5 % error in TR); a clamp-on ultrasonic flow meter on a straight run; and power from a true-power meter, not from current alone.

VAM steam and hot water consumption Working level

Heat input (kW) = TR × 3.517 / COP
Steam (kg/h) = heat input × 3,600 / hfg at the supply pressure
Hot water (m³/h) = heat input × 3,600 / (4.186 × ΔT × 1,000)

A 200 TR double-effect VAM (COP 1.3) on 8 barg steam needs 200 × 3.517 / 1.3 ≈ 541 kW, about 960 kg/h of steam or 4.8 kg/h per TR. A 100 TR single-effect machine (COP 0.7) on 90 °C hot water cooled by 10 °C needs about 502 kW, or 43 m³/h of hot water: a good use for DG jacket water, hot condensate or process cooling water that would otherwise go to a cooling tower.

The cooling tower each one needs Working level

Both machines reject their heat to the cooling tower: the cooling load plus whatever drove the machine.

Electric chiller: cooling + compressor power ≈ 1.2–1.25 × cooling
VAM: cooling × (1 + 1/COP) ≈ 1.77 × cooling (double effect), ≈ 2.4 × (single effect)

Replacing a 200 TR electric chiller with a VAM therefore needs a cooling tower about 40 % bigger, more condenser water flow, and more make-up water. Count this before deciding.

Which is cheaper to run? Going deeper

Electric chiller cost per year = TR × kW/TR × hours × ₹/kWh
VAM cost per year = TR × steam (kg/h per TR) / 1,000 × hours × ₹/tonne + auxiliary power cost
Break-even steam cost = (chiller kW/TR − VAM auxiliary kW/TR) × ₹/kWh / (steam per TR-hour in tonnes)

With 0.85 kW/TR for the chiller (including its share of pumps and cooling tower fans), 0.15 kW/TR of VAM auxiliaries, power at ₹8/kWh and 4.6 kg/h of steam per TR, the break-even is about ₹1,200 per tonne of steam. Steam from furnace oil at ₹3,700 per tonne never gets there; steam from a well-run biomass boiler can come close; waste heat, exhaust gas or back-pressure steam from a turbine beats it easily. That is why VAMs make the most sense with cogeneration, DG or gas-engine waste heat, or a cheap solid-fuel boiler with spare capacity.

Other points in the decision: a VAM cannot make sub-zero brine (water is the refrigerant); it has few moving parts and runs quietly; it needs good vacuum and tight control of solution concentration to avoid crystallisation; and it uses almost no electricity, which helps where the electrical supply or DG backup is the limit.

Saving chiller energy Going deeper

  • Raise the chilled water set point as far as the process allows: roughly 2–3 % power saved per 1 °C.
  • Keep the condenser water cold and the condenser tubes clean: roughly 2–3 % per 1 °C colder.
  • Run one chiller well loaded rather than two lightly loaded, unless the machines are designed for good part-load efficiency.
  • Find the cause of a low chilled water ΔT: too much flow, three-way valves bypassing, or an oversized pump.
  • Insulate and seal chilled water and brine lines; wet insulation is both a heat gain and a corrosion problem.

Questions people ask

How do you convert kW/TR to COP?

COP = 3.517 / (kW/TR). At 0.7 kW/TR the COP is about 5.0; at 1.2 kW/TR it is about 2.9.

How much steam does a VAM use per TR?

About 4.5–5 kg/h per TR for a double-effect machine on 8–9 barg steam, and about 8 kg/h per TR for a single-effect machine on low-pressure steam.

Can a VAM supply brine below 0 °C?

No. A lithium bromide VAM uses water as the refrigerant, so chilled water down to about 5–7 °C is its practical limit. Brine duties need a vapour compression chiller.
