Maintenance Planning: Preventive, Predictive, MTBF and MTTR

Plant Departments · Maintenance · October 2026

Maintenance decides whether the plant runs when production needs it. Good maintenance is mostly invisible: the planned job done on time, the spare on the shelf, the bearing changed before it seized. This article covers the strategies and the few numbers that tell you if they are working.

Breakdown, preventive and predictive Basics

  • Breakdown (run to failure): fix it when it fails. Acceptable for cheap, non-critical items with a spare available.
  • Preventive (time-based): service at fixed intervals or running hours: lubrication, seal and gasket changes, filter changes, calibration.
  • Predictive or condition-based: measure condition and act when it deteriorates: vibration on pumps and agitators, thermography of electrical panels, oil analysis on gearboxes, ultrasonic thickness on vessels and lines.

Criticality decides the strategy Basics

Rank each asset by the consequence of its failure: safety and environment, product quality, production loss, and repair cost. Critical items (the only reactor for a bottleneck step, the brine chiller, the main vacuum pump, the scrubber fan) get condition monitoring, strategic spares and priority. Low-criticality items can run to failure. This puts effort where it matters.

MTBF, MTTR and availability Working level

MTBF (mean time between failures) = total operating time ÷ number of failures. MTTR (mean time to repair) = total repair time ÷ number of repairs. Availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR).

Example: a pump runs 4,000 hours in a period with 4 failures and 32 hours of total repair time. MTBF = 1,000 h, MTTR = 8 h, availability = 1,000 ÷ 1,008 = 99.2 %. Rising MTBF shows reliability is improving; falling MTTR shows the repair process (spares, skills, access) is improving.

Repeated failures Working level

The same equipment failing again and again is the best place to invest effort. Record each failure with its cause, not just “seal replaced”. Common root causes in chemical plants: pumps running far from their best efficiency point, wrong seal flush plan, misalignment, corrosion from the wrong MOC, and dry running. See pump and seal troubleshooting and glass-lined reactor care.

Spares, safety and GMP Going deeper

Keep critical spares based on lead time and consequence of failure, not just usage. All work follows permit to work and isolation. In GMP areas, maintenance on product-contact equipment is recorded in the equipment log, may need change control if anything is changed, and the equipment is cleaned and released by QA before use. Calibration of critical instruments follows a schedule with out-of-tolerance results investigated for impact on batches made since the last good calibration.

On the plant

Put the five worst bad actors, the equipment with the most breakdown hours last quarter, on the wall of the maintenance office with their MTBF. Fixing those five usually does more than any new system.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Same preventive schedule for every asset regardless of criticality.
  • Failures recorded without a cause.
  • Critical spares with long lead times not stocked.
  • Calibration failures not checked for batch impact.
  • Equipment returned to production without cleaning and release.

Questions people ask

How is MTBF calculated?

Total operating time divided by the number of failures in that time.

What is availability?

The fraction of time equipment is able to run: MTBF divided by (MTBF + MTTR).

What is condition-based maintenance?

Maintenance triggered by measured condition, such as vibration, temperature, oil quality or wall thickness, rather than by a fixed time interval.
