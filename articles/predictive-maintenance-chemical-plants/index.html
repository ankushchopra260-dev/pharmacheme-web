Predictive Maintenance in Chemical Plants

A pump that fails in the middle of a batch costs more than the repair: the batch, the overtime, sometimes the safety risk. Most of those failures gave weeks of warning that nobody was watching. This article explains how much warning you get (the P-F curve), what to measure on chemical plant equipment, how to read vibration limits, where machine learning genuinely helps, and how to run a pilot that proves its value.

Four ways to maintain a machine Basics

StrategyWhen you actGood for
Breakdown (run to failure)After it failsCheap, non-critical items with a spare on the shelf
Preventive (time-based)Every fixed number of hours or monthsWear that follows the calendar: filters, belts, lubrication
Condition-basedWhen a measured condition crosses a limitRotating equipment with clear warning signs: vibration, temperature, current
PredictiveWhen a model says failure is coming, ideally with how long is leftCritical machines with good sensor and failure history

Predictive maintenance is condition-based maintenance with better eyes: more signals, watched continuously, with software (often machine learning) spotting the pattern earlier than a fixed alarm limit would. Which strategy each machine deserves depends on its criticality; see maintenance planning, MTBF and MTTR.

The P-F curve: how much warning you get Basics

Most failures of rotating equipment give warning. P is the point where a coming failure first becomes detectable (a rise in vibration, a hot bearing); F is the functional failure (the pump stops delivering). The time between them is the P-F interval. Reliability-centred maintenance practice says to check at intervals of about half the P-F interval, or less for high-risk failure modes, so that at least one check falls inside the warning window with time left to plan the repair.

Continuous online monitoring makes the check interval almost zero, which is why it pays on machines with short P-F intervals, such as high-speed pumps and compressors, where a monthly route would miss the warning.

Most of these signals already exist in the DCS or can be added with low-cost wireless sensors. The first job is usually to get them into the process historian with sensible tag names.

Vibration severity: ISO 20816 zones Working level

For general industrial machines, ISO 20816 classes overall vibration velocity (RMS, mm/s) into four zones: A newly commissioned machines, B acceptable for unrestricted long-term operation, C unsatisfactory for continuous operation (plan the repair), and D likely to cause damage. The boundaries depend on machine size and how it is mounted.

Vibration zone check

Boundaries from the ISO 20816 group 1 and 2 values as published by Fabrico (see sources). Check the standard and the machine maker’s limits for your machine; trends matter more than one reading.

Where machine learning adds value Working level

  • Anomaly detection across many signals. A model learns how vibration, temperature, current and flow move together in normal running, and flags a pump that drifts away from its own normal, even when no single value has crossed an alarm limit. This is what Shell scaled to more than 10,000 pieces of equipment, with about 11,000 models in production.
  • Context. In batch plants, an agitator’s current depends on the batch phase and fill level. Models that know the phase avoid the false alarms that make people ignore fixed limits.
  • Remaining useful life. Predicting how many days are left needs a history of real failures with their signals. Most sites do not have enough failures per machine type; start with anomaly detection, which does not.
  • Work-order text. Grouping years of free-text failure notes from the CMMS often finds the “bad actors” faster than any sensor project.

McKinsey estimates the potential of AI in chemical-plant maintenance at 30 to 40% higher maintenance labour productivity. That is an estimate, not a measured result.

A practical pilot in 12 weeks Going deeper

  1. Pick 10 to 20 critical machines with repeat failures and real production or safety impact. Use your criticality ranking and the last two years of breakdowns.
  2. Baseline them. Record failures, downtime hours and repair cost for the last two years, so savings can be measured later.
  3. Instrument the gaps. Add wireless vibration and temperature sensors where the DCS has nothing; bring everything into the historian.
  4. Start with limits, then models. ISO-based alarm zones give quick wins; anomaly models come once there are a few months of normal data.
  5. Close the loop. Every alert must create a work order in the CMMS with an owner, and every finding at inspection is fed back to tune the model.
  6. Measure. Breakdowns avoided, downtime hours, emergency work orders and spares cost, against the baseline.
On the plant

The most common failure of a predictive maintenance project is not the model: it is alerts that nobody acts on. Agree before you start who receives an alert, how fast they respond, and how the result is recorded.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Monitoring everything instead of the 10% of machines that cause most of the loss.
  • Fixed alarm limits on agitators and pumps whose normal values change with each batch phase.
  • Sensors installed, data not stored, or stored without the equipment tag that links it to the CMMS.
  • Calling a model “predictive” when there is no failure history to predict from.
  • Skipping the baseline, so no one can prove the savings a year later.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between preventive and predictive maintenance?

Preventive maintenance is done on a fixed schedule, whether or not the machine needs it. Predictive maintenance is done when measured condition, often analysed by software or machine learning, shows a failure is developing.

How often should vibration be checked?

Reliability-centred maintenance practice suggests a check interval of about half the P-F interval for that failure mode, shorter for high-risk failures. Machines with short P-F intervals are better monitored online.

Do we need machine learning for predictive maintenance?

No. Many sites get most of the benefit from good sensors, ISO-based vibration limits and a disciplined work-order process. Machine learning helps when many signals interact or normal values change with batch phase.
