Materials of Construction

Plant Engineering · Materials · October 2026

“Use SS316, it’s stainless.” Many corrosion failures start with that sentence. Stainless steel resists a lot, but chlorides, hot caustic and reducing acids can destroy it quickly, while cheap carbon steel happily holds strong sulphuric acid. This article explains how the common plant materials behave, the forms of corrosion that catch people out, and how to choose.

The common materials Basics

  • Carbon steel: cheap and strong. Fine for neutral solvents, steam, strong sulphuric acid at ambient, caustic at moderate temperature. Rusts in water and dilute acids.
  • SS304 / SS316L: chromium forms a thin passive oxide film that protects the steel. Molybdenum in 316 improves resistance to chlorides. “L” means low carbon, which avoids corrosion at welds.
  • Duplex stainless (2205): stronger and much more resistant to chloride stress-corrosion cracking than 316L.
  • Nickel alloys (Hastelloy C-276, C-22): resist chlorides, many reducing acids and mixed acid streams. Expensive, used where stainless fails.
  • Titanium: excellent in chlorides, wet chlorine and oxidising conditions; poor in reducing acids such as hot HCl and attacked by fluorides.
  • Glass-lined steel: resists almost all acids at most temperatures, except HF and hot concentrated phosphoric acid; damaged by hot alkali and mechanical or thermal shock. See glass-lined reactor care.
  • PTFE and PFA linings: almost universally resistant; permeation, vacuum collapse and static (they are non-conductive) are the limits.
  • FRP, PP, HDPE, PVDF: tanks and piping for acids and caustic at modest temperatures.

Forms of corrosion Basics

  • General corrosion: even thinning, predictable from corrosion rate tables and coupons. See the corrosion coupon calculator.
  • Pitting: local holes where the passive film breaks down, typically caused by chlorides. Can perforate a vessel while the rest looks perfect. The pitting resistance equivalent number, PREN = %Cr + 3.3 × %Mo + 16 × %N, ranks alloys: about 24 for 316L, 35 for duplex 2205, over 40 for super-duplex and Hastelloy.
  • Stress-corrosion cracking (SCC): cracks from the combination of tensile stress and a specific environment, such as chlorides with 304/316 above about 50–60 °C, or hot caustic with carbon steel. Sudden and dangerous.
  • Crevice corrosion: under gaskets, deposits and in tight gaps, where the chemistry inside differs from the bulk.
  • Galvanic corrosion: two different metals in contact in an electrolyte; the less noble one corrodes faster.
  • Erosion-corrosion: high velocity or slurries wear away the protective film, common at elbows and pump casings.

Choosing by service Working level

Selection should follow the worst condition the equipment will see, not the normal one: the cleaning cycle, the start-up with concentrated acid, the hot spot near the steam inlet, the chloride left from a previous product. In a multi-purpose plant, list every chemical the equipment may meet, including cleaning agents, and check them all. The materials compatibility tool helps with that list.

Gaskets, seals and linings Working level

A vessel in Hastelloy with the wrong gasket still leaks. Elastomers are usually the weakest link:

  • EPDM: good for water, steam, dilute acids and alkali; swells in hydrocarbons and oils.
  • Viton (FKM): good for hydrocarbons and many acids; poor in ketones, esters and amines.
  • FFKM (perfluoroelastomer): resists almost everything; expensive.
  • PTFE gaskets resist chemicals but creep under load; use filled or expanded PTFE and the right bolt torque.

Checking and monitoring Going deeper

  • Corrosion coupons in critical services, removed and weighed on a schedule.
  • Ultrasonic thickness surveys on vessels and piping, compared with the minimum thickness from design.
  • Material certificates and positive material identification (PMI) for alloy parts, so a carbon steel fitting doesn't end up in a Hastelloy line.
  • Review materials through MOC whenever a new chemical or cleaning agent is introduced.
On the plant

Keep the failed part. A pitted gasket face, a cracked weld or a thinned elbow tells a corrosion specialist almost exactly what happened. Thrown into the scrap bin, it tells nobody anything.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Assuming stainless steel resists chlorides.
  • Choosing material for the process but not for cleaning chemicals.
  • Glass-lined vessels exposed to hot caustic.
  • Elastomers chosen without checking every solvent.
  • No PMI on alloy spares.

Questions people ask

Why does stainless steel corrode in chloride solutions?

Chlorides break the passive film, causing pitting and stress-corrosion cracking.

What is PREN?

%Cr + 3.3×%Mo + 16×%N; about 24 for 316L, 35 for duplex 2205.

Can glass-lined reactors handle caustic?

Only cold and dilute; hot alkali dissolves glass.
