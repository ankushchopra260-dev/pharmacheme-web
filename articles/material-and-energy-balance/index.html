Material and Energy Balance for Batch Plants

Plant Engineering · Fundamentals · October 2026

Every chemical engineer learns material balances in the first year, then meets a real plant where the numbers never add up. 3,800 kg go into the reactor and only 3,670 kg come out the other end, and nobody can say where the rest went. Those missing kilograms are money, solvent emissions and sometimes a safety problem. This article shows how to do a balance that is useful on a real batch plant, and how to find what is missing.

The idea: what goes in comes out Basics

Mass is conserved. For any part of a plant over any period:

In − Out + Generated − Consumed = Accumulated

For total mass, nothing is generated or consumed by a chemical reaction, so for a complete batch (empty to empty) the balance is simply In = Out. For a single chemical, reactions do create and destroy it, so its balance needs the generation and consumption terms from the stoichiometry. Energy follows the same rule: heat in from steam, plus heat released by reaction, equals heat out to cooling water and surroundings, plus the change in the batch's own heat content.

Draw the boundary first Basics

Most balance arguments are really arguments about the boundary. Before writing any numbers, draw a box on the process flow diagram and decide:

  • What is inside: one reactor, a whole stage, or the whole plant?
  • Over what period: one batch, a campaign, a month? Monthly balances smooth out batch-to-batch hold-up.
  • Every stream crossing the line: raw materials, solvents, washes, nitrogen, water, steam condensate, vents, samples, effluent, product, residues.

The streams people forget are almost always the small ones crossing the boundary sideways: the line wash, the sample, the vent to the scrubber, the water used to rinse the filter.

A batch mass balance Working level

Take a reaction stage. Into the reactor go 500 kg of starting material, 300 kg of reagent, 400 kg of caustic lye, 2,000 kg of toluene and 600 kg of water: 3,800 kg in total. Out come 520 kg of dry product, 1,850 kg of recovered toluene, 1,160 kg of aqueous layer to the ETP and 140 kg of distillation residue: 3,670 kg.

Closure = total out / total in × 100 = 3,670 / 3,800 = 96.6 % 130 kg unaccounted per batch.

A batch balance closing within about 97–103 % is good, given weighing and level-gauge accuracy. Below that, something is being missed or lost.

Component balances: following the solvent Working level

A total balance can close even when individual components don't, because errors cancel. To reduce consumption you need a balance on each important component, and toluene is the obvious one here:

  • In: 2,000 kg charged (fresh plus recovered).
  • Out: 1,850 kg recovered, plus toluene dissolved in the aqueous layer (toluene's solubility in water is about 0.05 %, so under 1 kg), plus toluene in the wet cake before drying, plus toluene in the residue, plus vapour lost through vents and vacuum.

Analyse each stream: GC on the aqueous layer and residue, LOD and GC on the wet cake, and a vent estimate from the condenser outlet temperature (see the vacuum systems article). Usually the balance points to two or three streams that hold most of the loss. In my experience they are vacuum and vent losses, the wet cake going to the dryer, and the residue.

Do the same for the product: dissolved in the mother liquor, left in the reactor and lines, lost as fines through the filter cloth, and degraded in the dryer. Product losses usually cost more than solvent losses.

The energy balance of a distillation Working level

Energy balances answer questions like “how much steam does this distillation need?” and “is the condenser big enough?”.

Heat needed = mbatch × cp × (Tboil − Tstart) + mdistilled × λsolvent
Steam = heat needed / λsteam × (1 + losses)

Distilling 1,850 kg of toluene (λ ≈ 86 kcal/kg at atmospheric pressure) takes 1,850 × 86 ≈ 1,59,100 kcal of latent heat. With steam at about 3.5 barg (λ ≈ 509 kcal/kg) that is 313 kg of steam, plus roughly 10–15 % for losses and heating the vessel: about 360 kg. Add the sensible heat to bring the batch up to boiling if it starts cold.

The condenser has to remove the same 1,59,100 kcal. With cooling water warming by 8 °C: 1,59,100 / (1 × 8) ≈ 19,900 kg, about 20 m³ of cooling water per batch. If the distillation runs over 4 hours, that is a 39,800 kcal/h average load, with a higher peak at the start. The heat exchanger article shows how to check the condenser area, and the steam article how to put a price on the steam.

On the plant

Compare the calculated steam with the steam flow meter, if there is one. A large gap usually means wet steam, a failed trap passing live steam, or an uninsulated vessel top. The balance turns a vague “steam consumption is high” into a specific number to investigate.

Finding the missing kilograms Going deeper

  1. Check the measurements. Load cells drift; level gauges on agitated, foaming or jacketed vessels are often several percent out; drum weights are sometimes assumed rather than weighed. Calibrate before chasing losses.
  2. Check hold-up. A batch balance is only exact from empty to empty. Material left in the reactor, filter, lines and dryer from one batch shows up in the next. Use campaign balances to average it out.
  3. List the unrecorded streams. Line washes, filter washes, samples, spillage, nitrogen sweeping vapour to the vent.
  4. Look at the vents. Every vessel that breathes during charging, heating or nitrogen purging carries solvent vapour out. With nitrogen sweeping a warm vessel, the loss can be tens of kilograms per batch.
  5. Look at the chemistry. Gas given off (CO2, HCl, SO2) leaves the balance through the scrubber and must be counted as an output.

A good balance also has a safety benefit. Material that is “unaccounted” is somewhere: in the air of the plant, in the ETP, or building up in a vessel. A persistent gap in a toxic or flammable component deserves an investigation, not a correction factor.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Starting with numbers before agreeing the boundary.
  • Balancing total mass and never balancing the components.
  • Forgetting gases given off by the reaction.
  • Treating a 5 % gap as “normal losses” year after year.
  • Estimating steam from the latent heat alone, without the losses and sensible heat.

Questions people ask

What is mass balance closure?

Total outputs divided by total inputs. A good batch closes within about 97–103 %.

How do you calculate steam for a distillation?

Sensible plus latent heat, divided by the latent heat of steam, plus 10–15 % for losses. 1,850 kg of toluene needs about 360 kg of steam.

Why do component balances matter?

Errors cancel in a total balance. Component balances show where each material is actually lost.
