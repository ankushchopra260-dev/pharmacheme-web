Solvent Selection and Solvent Swaps

Scale-up · Solvents · October 2026

Many API processes use three or four solvents across a sequence of steps, and every change from one to the next costs time, energy and solvent. The swap is often the longest step in the batch and the one that most often leaves too much of the old solvent behind. This article covers how to choose solvents in the first place and how to swap them efficiently.

Choosing a solvent Basics

The chemist picks a solvent for the chemistry: solubility, reactivity, selectivity. At scale, more questions matter:

  • Safety: flash point, static (conductivity), peroxide formation (THF, ethers), toxicity, thermal stability with reagents (DMF and DMSO with strong bases and hydrides).
  • Work-up: water miscibility, density difference from water, emulsion tendency.
  • Recovery: boiling point, azeotropes with water and other solvents, ease of drying.
  • Regulatory and environmental: ICH class, emission limits, effluent load.
  • Cost and supply.

Published solvent selection guides (for example from the ACS GCI Pharmaceutical Roundtable and CHEM21) rank common solvents on these factors and are a good starting point for replacing problem solvents like DCM, DMF or 1,4-dioxane.

Residual solvents and ICH classes Basics

ICH Q3C groups solvents by toxicity: Class 1 (avoid: benzene, carbon tetrachloride), Class 2 (limit, with a permitted concentration for each: toluene 890 ppm, methanol 3,000 ppm, DCM 600 ppm, acetonitrile 410 ppm), and Class 3 (low toxicity, generally up to 5,000 ppm: ethanol, acetone, ethyl acetate, IPA). The class of the last solvents used before isolation matters most, because they are the hardest to remove completely.

Two ways to swap Working level

To change from a low-boiling solvent (say THF) to a higher-boiling one (say toluene):

  • Put-and-take: distil down to the minimum volume, add the new solvent back to the working volume, distil down again, repeat. Simple to run, with several “chases”.
  • Concentrate, then constant volume: distil down to the minimum volume, then keep distilling while feeding new solvent at the same rate as distillate leaves, holding the volume constant. Finally top up to the working volume.

Both work because the old solvent is more volatile: the vapour is richer in it than the liquid. With a relative volatility of 3.5, distilling down to half volume and swapping at that volume, put-and-take needs about 2 batch volumes of new solvent in four chases to reach 1 % residual; the constant-volume method needs about 1.4, roughly 30 % less. The advantage of constant volume grows as the minimum volume gets smaller and the target tighter.

The single biggest lever is the minimum volume: the lower you can go, the fewer volumes of new solvent you need. That is set by the agitator coverage, the temperature probe position, and whether the product stays in solution or becomes a stirrable slurry.

Azeotropes and other traps Working level

  • Azeotropes: methanol and toluene, ethanol and toluene, IPA and water all form azeotropes. A swap that crosses one can't reach low residuals by simple distillation; the residual levels off. Check azeotrope data before designing a swap, and sometimes swap through a third solvent.
  • Product stability: concentrating to a low volume at high temperature can degrade the product or crystallise it on the wall. Use vacuum to keep the temperature down. See vacuum systems.
  • Water: water from a work-up often has to come out with the old solvent; toluene's heterogeneous azeotrope with water helps here (azeotropic drying with a Dean-Stark separator).
  • Swapping to a lower-boiling solvent by distillation doesn't work; use crystallisation, a different isolation, or a high-boiler-tolerant next step instead.

Doing it in the plant Going deeper

  • Define the end point by analysis (GC of the pot), not by the number of chases.
  • Know the true minimum stirrable volume of the reactor, with the agitator covered and the temperature probe immersed.
  • Watch the top temperature: as the old solvent runs out, it rises toward the new solvent's boiling point at that pressure.
  • Collect the distillate separately for recovery; mixed distillate of two solvents is harder to recover than either alone. See distillation basics.
On the plant

If the residual old solvent stalls at the same value after each chase, suspect an azeotrope or a dead volume (a condenser leg, a dip pipe, a sample line) that keeps feeding old solvent back. More chases won't fix either.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Fixing the number of chases instead of testing the residual.
  • Ignoring an azeotrope between the two solvents.
  • Not knowing the true minimum stirrable volume.
  • Overheating the product during concentration.
  • Mixing the distillates of different solvents.

Questions people ask

What is a put-and-take solvent swap?

Repeated distilling down and refilling with the new solvent.

What is constant-volume distillation?

Distilling while feeding new solvent at the same rate, keeping the volume constant.

Why does a solvent swap sometimes stall?

An azeotrope, or a dead volume returning old solvent.
