AI in the Chemical Industry: Use Cases, Tools and Digitalization

Plant Engineering · Digitalization · October 2026

Every conference now has an AI session, and every vendor has an AI product. On the plant floor the questions are simpler: which problems can AI actually solve here, what data does it need, what do the regulators expect, and where do we start on Monday? This article answers those questions with real results, the GMP rules as they stand in late 2026, and interactive tools: a prompt builder for first-time users, a data-stack explorer, a use-case finder, an AI tool finder and a readiness self-check. It is written for chemical and pharma engineers who have never used AI and for those who already build models.

What “AI” means in a plant Basics

“AI” is used for very different tools. Knowing which one you are talking about decides the data you need, how you validate it and who can build it.

TypeWhat it doesPlant example
Machine learning (ML)Learns a relationship from historical numbers: inputs to an outputPredict the impurity at end of batch from temperature, dosing rate and stirrer power
Anomaly detectionLearns what “normal” looks like and flags departuresA pump’s vibration and bearing temperature drift weeks before failure
Computer visionRecognises patterns in images or videoLabel and packaging defects; PPE checks at a charging area
Optimisation (incl. Bayesian optimisation)Searches for the best settings with the fewest trialsChoosing the next lab experiment for a new route
Generative AI / large language models (LLMs)Reads and writes text, code and summariesSearch SOPs and past deviations in plain language; draft a first version of a report
Digital twinA model of the process (first-principles, data-driven or both) kept in step with live dataTry a new temperature profile on the model before the reactor

Most of the value reported by plants so far comes from the first three, built on historian and lab data. Generative AI is newer, faster to try, and under the most regulatory caution in GMP areas.

Never used AI? Start here Beginner

You do not need to code or buy anything to start. A general AI assistant works like a very well-read colleague who answers instantly, writes fast and is sometimes confidently wrong. Use it for words and ideas; check every number yourself.

Five things to try this week

  1. Explain something. “Explain NPSH to a new production officer in five lines, with one plant example.”
  2. Summarise a manual. Paste a non-confidential section of a pump or dryer manual and ask for the start-up checks as a list.
  3. Draft a document. “Draft a one-page toolbox talk on static electricity when charging solvents.” Then correct it.
  4. Spreadsheet help. “Write an Excel formula that flags batches where yield is below 92% and cycle time above 30 h.” Test it on a few rows you know.
  5. Prepare questions. “Give me 15 what-if questions for a HAZOP of a solvent recovery column.” Use them as prompts for your team, not as the HAZOP.
Three rules from day one

Use only the AI tools your company has approved, and never paste confidential process data, recipes or customer names into public tools. Treat every number, regulation or reference as unverified until you have checked it. You remain responsible for anything you sign or send.

Prompt builder

Good answers come from good prompts: say who you are, what you need, the context and the format. Pick options and copy the prompt into your company-approved AI assistant.

Digitalization comes first: the data stack Basics

AI is only as good as the data under it. ISA-95, the international standard for integrating business and manufacturing systems, describes a plant in five levels, from the physical process up to ERP. Most AI projects fail or stall not in the model but in getting clean, time-aligned data out of these levels. Tap a level.

The plant data stack (ISA-95 levels)

Where the data for AI lives, and what AI can do with it.

Three things decide whether this stack can feed AI:

  • Data integrity. FDA’s 2018 data-integrity guidance expects records to be attributable, legible, contemporaneously recorded, original or a true copy, and accurate (ALCOA). A model trained on edited, back-filled or untraceable data inherits those problems, and in GMP areas the training data itself becomes a record.
  • Context. A temperature value is useless without the batch, step, equipment and time it belongs to. Linking historian tags to MES batch phases and LIMS sample IDs is usually the biggest job in any AI project.
  • Cybersecurity. Connecting the control network to analytics or cloud tools opens a path into the plant. The ISA/IEC 62443 series sets out how asset owners, suppliers and integrators share responsibility for securing industrial control systems.
On the plant

Before buying an AI platform, try this test: can someone pull last year’s batches of one product, with the temperature trend, dosing record and final assay for each batch, into one spreadsheet in a day? If not, the first project is a data project, and it pays for itself in faster investigations even before any AI.

How a soft sensor works Working level

A soft sensor estimates a variable that is slow or expensive to measure (an assay, an impurity, a moisture) from fast, cheap measurements the plant already logs (temperatures, pressures, flows, power). Kadlec and co-workers’ widely cited review describes data-driven soft sensors as an alternative to traditional ways of getting critical process variables, used for monitoring and control. The catch is drift: catalysts age, raw materials change, sensors foul, and the model slowly stops matching reality unless it is checked against lab results and recalibrated.

Demo: soft sensor against lab results

Simulated data, to show the idea. The lab gives a result every 4 hours; the soft sensor gives an estimate every minute. Add drift and watch the check against the lab catch it.

Actual value (not visible in a real plant)Soft sensor estimateLab result

In a real plant the check-and-recalibrate step is written into a procedure, with limits agreed in advance. Under GMP, that is exactly what draft Annex 22 asks for: defined performance metrics, monitoring in use, and change control when the model is updated.

AI under GMP: FDA, EU Annex 22, GAMP Going deeper

Chemical plants outside GMP are governed by ordinary process safety and quality systems. Pharma plants have specific, fast-moving expectations. The status below is as of September 2026; check for updates before you rely on it.

DocumentStatusWhat it says
FDA discussion paper: Artificial Intelligence in Drug ManufacturingDiscussion paper, March 2023Gives examples: process design and scale-up, advanced process control, process monitoring and fault detection (including AI visual inspection of packaging and labels), and trend monitoring of complaints and deviations. Raises questions on cloud data, IoT data volumes, standards for developing and validating models, and continuously learning systems.
FDA draft guidance: Considerations for the Use of AI to Support Regulatory Decision-Making for Drug and Biological ProductsDraft, January 2025; still draft in September 2026A risk-based credibility framework for a model’s context of use: define the question, define the context of use, assess model risk (model influence × decision consequence), plan, execute, document, and decide whether credibility is adequate.
EU GMP Annex 22 (Artificial Intelligence), with revised Annex 11 and Chapter 4Consultation draft 7 July 2025; consultation closed October 2025; final text targeted for late 2026Covers static models (fixed after training) in critical GMP use. Requires a documented intended use approved by a process expert, test data kept separate from training data, acceptance criteria at least as good as the process replaced, explainability, confidence scores and change control. The draft keeps continuously learning models and generative AI out of critical GMP use; generative AI is allowed in non-critical tasks with a qualified person responsible for the output. EMA is reconsidering the generative AI position.
ISPE GAMP Guide: Artificial IntelligencePublished 1 October 2025Industry guidance on key AI concepts and on implementing AI in GxP-regulated environments, building on GAMP 5.
EU AI ActIn force since 1 August 2024; obligations phase inProhibited practices from February 2025, general-purpose AI rules from August 2025, and most high-risk obligations scheduled from August 2026. Relevant if you sell into the EU or use AI in regulated products; most plant analytics is not classed as high-risk, but check your case.
On the plant

The practical reading for a GMP site today: keep a person in the decision for anything that touches product quality, write the intended use before you build, freeze and version the model you validate, keep the test data locked away from the developers, and put model updates through change control like any other computerised system.

Generative AI at work: do and don’t Going deeper

Chat assistants are the AI most engineers touch first. NIST’s generative AI risk profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024) names “confabulation” as a core risk: the production of confidently stated but false content. In a plant, a confident wrong number is worse than no number.

Good uses (with review)

  • Searching your own SOPs, batch records and past deviations in plain language, with links back to the source document
  • A first draft of a report, training material or email that an expert then corrects
  • Summarising long documents: vendor manuals, regulations, literature
  • Writing and explaining spreadsheet formulas or small analysis scripts, then testing them on known data
  • Brainstorming what-if questions for a HAZOP pre-read

Keep it out of

  • Batch release, OOS conclusions and any decision a qualified person signs
  • Relief, safety-interlock and equipment sizing numbers without independent calculation
  • Reaction hazard conclusions: use calorimetry data (see reading DSC, ARC and RC1)
  • Confidential process data pasted into public tools: use company-approved, access-controlled systems
  • Citations you have not opened yourself

For practitioners: building models that last Expert

If you already build models, the hard parts in a chemical plant are rarely the algorithm. These are the points that most often decide whether a model survives its first year.

TopicPractice that works in plants
Splitting dataSplit by batch and by time, never by random rows: rows from the same batch in train and test make a model look far better than it is. Hold back the most recent campaigns as the final test.
Batch data structureAlign batches by phase (charging, reaction, quench, distillation) before comparing. Multivariate methods such as PCA and PLS on unfolded batch data are a strong, explainable baseline before deep learning.
Features from chemistryEngineer inputs with meaning: heat released from jacket ΔT and flow, dosing rate integrals, time above a temperature. They beat raw tags and make the model explainable.
Operating envelopeRecord the range of every input in the training data. Flag or refuse predictions outside it; plants change grades, scales and suppliers.
ExplainabilityReport which inputs drove each prediction (for example SHAP values). Draft Annex 22 expects this in critical GMP use, and operators trust what they can follow.
Drift and monitoringTrack prediction error against lab or inspection results and input distributions; set limits and an owner. Retraining is a change, not a routine refresh.
Documents and generative AIFor SOP and record search, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) that answers only from your approved documents, with a link to the source paragraph, is far safer than a model answering from memory. Test it with questions whose answers you know, including ones it should refuse.
Where it runsEdge or on-site servers for control-adjacent, low-latency or connectivity-sensitive use; cloud for heavy training and cross-site analytics. Either way, design the OT connection with ISA/IEC 62443 zones and conduits.
GMP lifecycleIntended use, data provenance, frozen model version, independent test set, acceptance criteria, periodic review and change control: the same discipline as any computerised system, with the additions in GAMP’s AI guide and draft Annex 22.

The people: plant digital transformation roles

Plants are now hiring for this directly. A typical 2026 posting for a “Plant Digital Transformation” role at a manufacturing site in India asks for someone who can bridge plant operations and digital technology, and says plainly that it is not a dashboarding or MIS job: the person must take a problem from the shop floor through business case, solution, adoption and measured results. The technologies it lists are the ones in this article: IoT and IIoT, MES, automation, AI and machine learning, analytics and Power BI, SAP, and IT/OT integration.

Readiness self-check and a 90-day start

Answer honestly for one plant or one block. The result is our own rough guide to what to do next, not a formal maturity standard.

How ready is your site for AI?

Ten yes-or-no questions.

A 90-day start that works in most plants

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: pick one problem with money attached. A bottleneck step, a frequent deviation, a pump that keeps failing. Write the question, the decision it supports and how you will measure success.
  2. Weeks 3 to 6: build the data set. Pull historian, lab, batch and maintenance data for that problem; fix units, time stamps and tag names; agree the data owner. Often this step alone finds the answer.
  3. Weeks 7 to 10: simple model first. Trend charts and regression before deep learning. Test on batches the model has not seen.
  4. Weeks 11 to 13: run it in advisory mode. Operators see the prediction but decide as before. Compare, measure, and decide whether to scale, change or stop.

Questions people ask

Do we need a big digital project before using AI?

No, but you need clean, time-stamped data for the problem you pick. Most plants start with one unit or one product, connect the historian, lab and batch data for it, and prove value there before scaling.

Can AI be used in GMP manufacturing?

Yes, with validation and human oversight. The EU draft Annex 22 covers static, trained models in critical GMP use and keeps generative AI out of critical decisions in the draft. FDA’s draft guidance describes a risk-based credibility framework. Both were still drafts in September 2026.

How can a chemical engineer start using AI?

Start with a general AI assistant approved by your company for everyday text work: explaining a concept, summarising a manual, drafting a report or writing a spreadsheet formula. Give it context, ask it to show its working and list assumptions, and check every number. The prompt builder above helps.

Which AI tools are used in the chemical industry?

General assistants for text work; process-data analytics on top of historians; predictive maintenance software; retrosynthesis tools for R&D; and Python, KNIME, JMP or Power BI for data analysis. See AI tools for chemical engineers.

What is the easiest first AI project?

Projects where the data already exists and a person still makes the decision: predictive maintenance on critical rotating equipment, a soft sensor for a slow lab result, or text search across SOPs and past deviations.

Will AI replace process engineers?

The published lighthouse results come with reskilling, not fewer engineers: Jubilant Ingrevia reports reskilling more than 2,000 employees. The models need people who understand the chemistry to choose inputs, judge results and own the decisions.
