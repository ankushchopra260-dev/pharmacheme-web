HR in Chemical and Pharma Plants

Plant Departments · Human Resources · October 2026

A plant runs on people working shifts around the clock with hazardous chemicals under GMP. HR in such a plant is far more than payroll: it decides whether every shift has enough trained people, whether training records stand up to an audit, and whether contract and permanent staff work safely side by side.

Manpower planning for shifts Basics

Start from posts, not people: how many operators, chemists and technicians each area needs on each shift. A post manned 24 hours, 7 days a week needs 168 hours of cover every week. With a 48-hour working week, that is 168 ÷ 48 = 3.5 people per post. Allowing about 10 % for leave and absence gives 3.5 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 3.9, so plan 4 people per round-the-clock post. A block with 6 posts per shift therefore needs about 24 people, plus supervisors and relievers for training.

The occupancy chart of the products tells you which areas are busy at once, which sets the posts per shift.

Recruitment and induction Basics

Plants recruit graduate engineer trainees and chemists from campuses, experienced operators and technicians, and specialists for QC, QA, EHS and maintenance. Tests and interviews should check what the job really needs: reading a P&ID, basic calculations, chemical safety awareness, attitude to procedures. Induction covers site safety rules, emergency response, GMP basics, data integrity, and a walk through the plant with a mentor before any independent work.

Training and the competency matrix Working level

In a GMP plant, an untrained person may not perform a GMP task, and the training record must prove it. A competency matrix lists every person against every SOP and task with their status: trained, qualified to work independently, or qualified to train others. Training on a new or revised SOP is completed before the SOP becomes effective. Good training mixes:

  • Classroom sessions on GMP, safety and the chemistry of the process.
  • On-the-job training with a qualified operator, signed off by observation.
  • Periodic refreshers and drills: fire, spill, gas leak, evacuation.
  • Training on lessons from incidents and deviations, so the same mistake is not repeated.

Contract labour Working level

Plants often use contractors for loading, cleaning, construction and maintenance support. The principal employer remains responsible for their safety on site and must check that the contractor meets statutory obligations: registration and licence where required, wages, provident fund and ESI, appointment letters and working hours. Contract workers need the same safety induction, PPE and permit discipline as permanent staff; many serious incidents involve contractors doing non-routine work.

Payroll compliance and industrial relations Going deeper

HR keeps the statutory side correct: wages and overtime, provident fund, ESI, gratuity, bonus, leave and registers, now under the new labour codes. It also runs the employee relationship: grievance handling, discipline according to standing orders where they apply, works committees and union negotiations, health check-ups and welfare (canteen, transport, rest rooms). Engagement and retention matter more in plants than many assume: an experienced shift operator who leaves takes years of process knowledge with them.

On the plant

Review the competency matrix of each shift, not just of the department. A department can look fully trained while the night shift on Sunday has nobody qualified to run the centrifuge.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Planning 3 people per round-the-clock post and running on overtime.
  • SOPs made effective before the training is complete.
  • Training records signed without observation.
  • Contract workers starting work without safety induction.
  • Exit interviews that never reach the plant head.

Questions people ask

How many people are needed for a 24x7 post?

About 168 hours of cover per week divided by 48 working hours gives 3.5; with about 10 percent leave and absence, plan 4 people per post.

What is a competency matrix?

A table of every person against every SOP and task, showing whether they are trained, qualified to work independently, or qualified to train others.

Who is responsible for contract workers safety?

The principal employer remains responsible for safety on its site, alongside the contractor; contract workers need the same induction, PPE and permits as employees.
