Acids, Bases and pH

Basic chemistry · pH, pKa and neutralisation · November 2026

pH is the most adjusted number on a chemical plant. It decides which layer the product sits in during a wash, whether a salt precipitates, how fast steel corrodes and whether the effluent can leave the site. Yet many pH problems come from treating it as a single reading rather than understanding what sets it.

What acids and bases are Basics

Three definitions are in use, and each is useful in its place.

  • Arrhenius: an acid gives H+ in water, a base gives OH−. Fine for hydrochloric acid and caustic soda.
  • Brønsted–Lowry: an acid is a proton donor, a base is a proton acceptor. This one covers amines, carbonates and reactions in organic solvents, and it is the one to think in.
  • Lewis: an acid accepts an electron pair, a base donates one. This explains why aluminium chloride, boron trifluoride and zinc chloride act as acid catalysts even though they have no proton.

When an acid gives up its proton, what is left is its conjugate base: acetic acid becomes acetate. The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.

The pH scale Basics

pH = −log10[H+]     pOH = −log10[OH−]     pH + pOH = pKw = 14.0 at 25 °C [H+] in mol/L. Each pH unit is a factor of ten in acidity: pH 2 is ten times more acidic than pH 3.

Neutral means equal H+ and OH−, which is pH 7.0 only at 25 °C. Water ionises more as it warms, so at 60 °C neutral is about pH 6.5. A hot sample that reads 6.5 is not acidic. pH meters correct the electrode response for temperature, but they cannot correct the chemistry; for comparisons, measure at a fixed temperature.

Strong and weak acids: pKa Basics

A strong acid such as hydrochloric, sulfuric (first proton) or nitric acid is completely ionised in water. A weak acid such as acetic acid only partly ionises, and the balance is described by its acid dissociation constant Ka, usually quoted as pKa = −log Ka. The lower the pKa, the stronger the acid.

Bases are compared through the pKa of their protonated form. Triethylamine (pKa 10.7 for Et3NH+) is a stronger base than pyridine (5.2). Values in organic solvents are different, sometimes by several units.

Weak acid, pH ≈ ½(pKa − log C)
0.1 mol/L acetic acid: ½(4.76 + 1.00) = 2.88 Good when the acid is less than about 5 percent ionised. Strong acid: pH = −log C.

Buffers and the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation Working level

A mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base resists changes in pH. This is a buffer.

pH = pKa + log ( [base form] / [acid form] ) When the two forms are equal, pH = pKa. One pH unit either side, the ratio is 10 : 1.

Buffers explain two things engineers see often. First, why it takes much more acid or alkali than expected to move the pH of an effluent containing acetate, carbonate or phosphate: the buffer soaks it up. Second, why a pH titration curve is flat near the pKa and steep near the end point, which makes pH control around neutral difficult with strong reagents.

Using pH to move a product between layers Working level

The neutral form of an organic acid or base dissolves in the organic layer; the charged form (salt) dissolves in water. pH decides which form you have.

Compound typeTo pull into waterTo push into the organic layer
Carboxylic acid, pKa about 4 to 5pH above pKa + 2 (about 7 or more): forms the carboxylatepH below pKa − 2 (about 2 or less)
Amine, pKa of the salt about 9 to 11pH below pKa − 2: forms the ammonium saltpH above pKa + 2 (about 12 or more)
Phenol, pKa about 10pH above about 12pH below about 8

Two pH units from the pKa gives about 99 percent in one form. One unit gives only about 91 percent, which is why a wash at the “right” pH can still lose product. This is also how acidic and basic impurities are removed: a bicarbonate wash takes out a carboxylic acid but leaves a phenol behind. More in work-up and phase separation.

Neutralisation heat and safe dilution Working level

A strong acid and a strong base release about 13.3 kcal per mole of water formed. That is a lot of heat in a small volume.

H2SO4 + 2 NaOH → Na2SO4 + 2 H2O
100 kg of 98 percent H2SO4 = 98 / 98.07 = 0.999 kmol → 2.00 kmol water formed
Heat ≈ 2,000 mol × 13.3 kcal/mol ≈ 26,600 kcal Neutralisation only. Diluting the concentrated acid releases a large additional amount of heat on top of this.
  • Acid into water, never water into acid. Water added to concentrated sulfuric acid boils locally and throws hot acid out.
  • Dose the neutralising agent slowly with cooling and agitation, and watch the temperature, not only the pH.
  • Carbonate and bicarbonate neutralisations also release CO2. Allow for foam and vent capacity.

The thermochemistry article shows how to turn a heat like this into a temperature rise.

Measuring pH on the plant Going deeper

  • Calibrate with two or three buffers (pH 4, 7 and 10) that bracket the range you expect. Check the slope; replace the electrode when it drifts.
  • Mixed solvents. In methanol–water or acetone–water mixtures the reading is not a true pH and can be off by a unit or more. Use it as a trend, and fix the method for each process.
  • Two-phase mixtures. Stop the agitator, let the layers settle and sample the aqueous layer. A reading taken in an emulsion is unreliable.
  • pH paper is useful for a quick check but reads only to about half a unit, and colour is hard to judge in coloured streams.
  • Fluoride, strong alkali and hot samples shorten electrode life. Use the right electrode type.

Questions people ask

Is pH 7 always neutral?

No. pH 7 is neutral only at 25 °C. Water ionises more as temperature rises, so at 60 °C neutral water is about pH 6.5. Compare pH readings at the same temperature.

What is the difference between pH and pKa?

pH describes a solution: how acidic it is now. pKa describes a compound: how readily it gives up a proton. When the pH equals the pKa, half of the compound is in its acid form and half in its base form.

At what pH should I extract an amine into the organic layer?

At least two pH units above the pKa of its protonated form. For a typical aliphatic amine with a pKa near 10 to 11 that means pH 12 or more, so that over 99 percent is in the neutral, organic-soluble form.

Why does it take so much acid to bring the effluent pH down?

The effluent is probably buffered by carbonate, acetate, phosphate or amines. The acid first converts the buffer before the pH moves much. A titration curve of a real sample shows how much acid is needed.
