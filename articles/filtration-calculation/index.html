How to Calculate Filtration Performance

Chemical Engineering · Solid–Liquid Separation · Updated October 2026

A 15-minute Büchner test in the lab can tell you whether the plant filtration will take one hour or eight, if you record the right numbers. This article explains what slows a filtration down, how to turn a simple bench trial into one number that scales (the specific cake resistance), and how to use it to predict a Nutsche filter or centrifuge batch.

What actually slows a filtration Basics

Liquid has to squeeze through two things: the filter cloth and the cake of solids building up on it. At the start there is no cake, so filtrate pours through. As solids deposit, the cake thickens and every layer adds resistance, so the flow keeps slowing. In almost every plant filtration the cake, not the cloth, ends up controlling the time.

The cake filtration equation Basics

The flow through a cake behaves like flow through any porous bed (Darcy's law): rate is proportional to pressure and area, and inversely proportional to viscosity and resistance. The resistance has two parts, the cake (which grows with the filtrate collected) and the cloth (fixed):

dV/dt = ΔP · A / [ μ ( α c V / A + Rm ) ] V filtrate volume (m³), t time (s), ΔP pressure difference (Pa), A filter area (m²), μ filtrate viscosity (Pa·s), c dry cake mass per m³ of filtrate (kg/m³), α specific cake resistance (m/kg), Rm cloth resistance (1/m).

Integrated at constant pressure, it becomes a straight line, which is the trick that makes it useful:

t / V = (μ α c / 2 A² ΔP) · V + μ Rm / (A ΔP) Plot t/V against V: the slope gives α, the intercept gives Rm.

α is the number that transfers from lab to plant. It belongs to the solid (its particle size, shape and how it packs), not to the filter you tested it in.

Running and reading a bench trial Working level

Use a Büchner or a small pressure filter with a known area, the same cloth or paper type as the plant if possible, and a vacuum or pressure close to the plant's. Record the filtrate volume at regular times, every 15–30 s, not just the total at the end. Note the slurry concentration so you know c.

Worked example

A 20 cm² (0.002 m²) filter at 0.5 bar (50,000 Pa), filtrate viscosity 1 cP (0.001 Pa·s), c = 40 kg/m³:

t/V rises by 0.25 s/mL for every 50 mL, a straight line. In SI units the slope is 5 × 109 s/m⁶ and the intercept 1 × 105 s/m³. Then:

α = 2 × slope × A² × ΔP / (μ c) = 2 × 5×109 × (0.002)² × 50,000 / (0.001 × 40) = 5 × 1010 m/kg
Rm = intercept × A × ΔP / μ = 1×105 × 0.002 × 50,000 / 0.001 = 1 × 1010 1/m

Predicting the plant filter Working level

The plant Nutsche has 2 m² of cloth and must collect 2 m³ of filtrate at the same 0.5 bar. Put the lab α and Rm back into the integrated equation with the plant area:

Slope = μ α c / (2 A² ΔP) = 0.001 × 5×1010 × 40 / (2 × 4 × 50,000) = 5,000 s/m⁶
Intercept = μ Rm / (A ΔP) = 100 s/m³
t = 5,000 × 2² + 100 × 2 ≈ 20,200 s ≈ 5.6 h

The shortcut: what matters is filtrate per square metre. The lab filter collected 0.1 m³/m² in 220 s; the plant needs 1 m³/m², ten times more, and because the cake term goes with V², it takes roughly a hundred times longer. The cake will be 40 kg/m² of dry solids, several centimetres thick.

Compressible cakes and why pressure disappoints Going deeper

The equation assumes α stays constant. For hard, regular crystals it nearly does. Many organic products, amorphous solids, and anything with fines form compressible cakes: pressing harder squeezes the pores shut, so α rises with ΔP. A common fit is α = α0 ΔPn, with n between 0 (incompressible) and 1 (so compressible that more pressure gains nothing).

To find out, run the bench trial at two pressures, say 0.5 and 1.5 bar. If α triples, n ≈ 1 and the plant should not expect a pressure filter to beat a vacuum one. Run the lab trial near the plant pressure, never extrapolate far.

Fixing a slow cake Going deeper

Because α depends on the particles, the cheapest fix is usually upstream, in the crystallisation:

  • Grow bigger crystals: slower cooling or antisolvent addition, seeding, and a hold before filtering. Resistance rises steeply as particle size falls.
  • Avoid fines: reduce agitator speed or impeller tip speed during crystallisation and transfer; pumps with high shear crush crystals.
  • Raise the temperature of the filtration if the product allows: filtrate viscosity falls and the time falls with it.
  • Filter aid (diatomaceous earth, perlite) for gelatinous or very fine solids, if the product can tolerate it.
  • Thinner cakes: split the batch into two loads, or use a centrifuge, which applies a far higher driving force and keeps the cake thin.
On the plant

A filtration that suddenly takes twice as long, with the same product and filter, almost always means the crystals changed: a faster cool-down, a different seed, a temperature excursion. Check the crystallisation log before blaming the cloth.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Recording only the final volume and time: one point can't separate cake from cloth resistance.
  • Testing at a very different pressure from the plant on a compressible cake.
  • Forgetting that slurry from the lab beaker is not the same as slurry after a plant transfer pump.
  • Ignoring temperature: filtrate viscosity can change by half between 20 and 50 °C.

Questions people ask

How do you calculate specific cake resistance?

Plot t/V against V from a timed trial, fit a line, and use α = 2 × slope × A² × ΔP / (μ c).

What is a good specific cake resistance?

Below 1010 m/kg is fast, 1010–1011 moderate, 1011–1012 slow, and above 1012 very slow.

Does doubling the pressure halve the filtration time?

Only for an incompressible cake. For compressible cakes the resistance rises with pressure and the gain is much smaller.
