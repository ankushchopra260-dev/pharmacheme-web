Steam Energy and Condenser Selection

Plant Engineering · Heat Transfer · October 2026

Two questions come up in almost every new batch process: how much steam will this heating step need, and is the reactor condenser big enough for the distillation? Both come down to the same physics: latent heat, given up by steam when it condenses in the jacket, and taken up again by the solvent vapour that has to condense in the condenser. This article goes through both, with the numbers engineers actually use.

What steam gives you Basics

The enthalpy of saturated steam has two parts. Sensible heat (hf) is what it took to heat the water to boiling. Latent heat (hfg) is what it took to boil it. In a jacket or coil, steam condenses and gives up its latent heat; the condensate leaves through the trap still carrying its sensible heat. So the useful heat per kg in ordinary heating is the latent heat, about 500 kcal/kg at typical process pressures.

Which steam pressure to use Basics

Higher pressure gives a higher temperature, so a bigger temperature difference and faster heating. But it gives less latent heat per kg, and the hot condensate carries more energy away (recoverable only as flash steam). The rule: use the lowest steam pressure that gives the temperature difference you need. Many plants distribute at 8–10 barg to keep pipe sizes small and reduce to 2–4 barg at the users. Remember the product's wall temperature limit and the glass-lined vessel's thermal shock limits when choosing.

Steam consumption for a duty Working level

Steam (kg/h) = Q (kcal/h) / hfg (kcal/kg) × (1 + losses)

86,000 kcal/h of heating at 3 barg (hfg ≈ 509 kcal/kg) needs about 169 kg/h of steam, plus 10–15 % for losses from the vessel and lines. To distil 1,850 kg of toluene, about 1,59,000 kcal of latent heat, needs roughly 360 kg of steam with losses. See material and energy balance and the steam and boiler calculators.

Wet steam (with water droplets), a failed trap that waterlogs the jacket, or air in the steam all reduce the heat delivered. If a jacket heats slowly, check the trap and the condensate drain before blaming the steam pressure.

Selecting a reactor condenser Working level

The condenser must remove the latent heat of all the vapour the reactor boils off, at the highest rate the steam can produce it:

Duty = vapour rate × [λ + cp (Tcondensing − Tcondensate)]    Area = Duty / (U × LMTD)
  1. Start from the reboil rate: the jacket heat input at full steam sets the maximum vapour rate. A condenser sized for the “normal” rate will be overloaded the first time someone opens the steam valve fully.
  2. Use the latent heat of the solvent at the condensing temperature: methanol (about 263 kcal/kg) needs three times the condenser of toluene (about 87 kcal/kg) for the same mass.
  3. Check the approach to the cooling water outlet: with 32/37 °C water, solvents boiling below about 50 °C (MDC, acetone at reduced pressure) need chilled water or brine.
  4. Choose U realistically: condensing organic vapour on cooling water is typically 345–690 kcal/h·m²·°C, lower with non-condensables. Add 15–25 % margin.
  5. Choose the type: shell-and-tube with vapour on the shell side for most duties; graphite or glass for corrosive vapours; vertical condensers drain better.

Vacuum, vent condensers and non-condensables Going deeper

Under vacuum, the condensing temperature drops with the boiling point, the LMTD to cooling water shrinks and the area needed rises fast. Nitrogen bleed and air leaks add non-condensable gas that collects in the condenser and blankets the tubes, cutting U sharply. Keep a vent condenser on chilled water or brine after the main condenser to catch what the main one misses and to cut solvent loss to the vacuum pump. See vacuum systems.

On the plant

If the distillation rate stalls while the jacket still has steam, feel the condenser vapour outlet and check the vent. A hot outlet means the condenser is overloaded or blanketed with nitrogen, and the vapour is going straight to the vacuum pump or the vent.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Counting the total enthalpy of steam instead of the latent heat.
  • Heating with high-pressure steam when low pressure would do.
  • Sizing the condenser for the normal rather than the maximum boil-up.
  • Ignoring non-condensables in vacuum condensers.
  • No vent condenser on low-boiling solvents.

Questions people ask

How much heat does steam give in a jacket?

Its latent heat: about 480–540 kcal/kg depending on pressure.

Why use low-pressure steam for heating?

More latent heat per kg and less energy lost with the condensate.

How do you size a reactor condenser?

Duty from the maximum boil-up and latent heat, then area = duty / (U × LMTD) with margin.
