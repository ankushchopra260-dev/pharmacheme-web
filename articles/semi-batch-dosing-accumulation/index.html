Semi-Batch Reactions: Dosing Time, Accumulation and MTSR

Scale-up · Reaction Safety · October 2026

“Dose the reagent over 4 hours.” Every batch card for an exothermic reaction has a line like this, and on a busy night someone always asks why it can't be 2 hours. The honest answer is not “because the SOP says so”. It is about how much unreacted reagent is sitting in the reactor at any moment, and what that reagent would do if the cooling failed. This article builds that answer from the ground up.

Why we dose instead of mixing everything Basics

If two reactants that release a lot of heat are mixed all at once (a batch reaction), the whole heat of reaction is available from the first minute. If cooling fails, there is nothing you can do: everything is already in the reactor.

In a semi-batch reaction, one reactant is in the reactor and the other is dosed in slowly. Ideally each drop reacts almost as soon as it arrives, so the heat comes out at the rate you dose. That gives you a control handle: if the temperature rises or cooling fails, stop dosing, and the heat release stops too. At least, that is the idea. Whether it is true depends on accumulation.

Accumulation: the reagent waiting to react Basics

Reagent doesn't react instantly. At any moment during dosing, some of what has been added has reacted and some hasn't. The unreacted part is the accumulation. If the reaction is fast compared with the dosing, accumulation stays low and stopping the feed really does stop the heat. If the reaction is slow, reagent piles up, and stopping the feed doesn't help much: the reagent already in the reactor keeps reacting.

Think of filling a bucket with a hole in it. The tap is the dosing rate; the hole is the reaction. The water level in the bucket is the accumulation. Open the tap faster, or make the hole smaller (a colder, slower reaction), and the level rises.

From accumulation to MTSR Working level

The worst case is a total cooling failure at the moment accumulation is highest. All the unreacted reagent then reacts with no cooling, and the batch heats up by its share of the adiabatic temperature rise:

ΔTad = Qreaction / (m × cp)
MTSR = Tprocess + Xacc × ΔTad MTSR: maximum temperature of the synthesis reaction. Xacc: accumulation as a fraction (0 to 1).

Then compare the MTSR with two temperatures that matter: the MTT, the maximum technical temperature (usually the boiling point of the solvent, in an open or vented system), and TD24, the temperature at which a decomposition would reach its maximum rate in 24 hours. Those comparisons give the Stoessel criticality class, from 1 (MTSR below both) to 5.

Worked example

A 3,000 kg batch in toluene with cp 0.43 kcal/kg·°C; the full dose releases 62 kcal per kg of batch. ΔTad = 62 / 0.43 ≈ 144 °C. Toluene boils at 111 °C; the decomposition TD24 is 140 °C. The reaction runs at 60 °C.

  • Dosed over 4 hours: peak accumulation about 14 %, MTSR ≈ 60 + 0.14 × 144 ≈ 80 °C. Below boiling: class 1.
  • Dosed over 1 hour: peak accumulation about 46 %, MTSR ≈ 126 °C. Above boiling but below TD24: class 3, relying on the solvent boiling to stop the temperature.
  • Dosed over 30 minutes: MTSR ≈ 155 °C, above TD24: class 4 or 5, a potential runaway into decomposition.

Can the plant cool it? Working level

Accumulation is about the cooling failure case. There is a second check for normal operation: can the jacket remove the heat as fast as the reaction makes it?

Heat release rate ≈ total reaction heat / dosing time
Cooling capacity = U × A × (Tbatch − Tjacket)

In the example, the total heat is 3,000 × 62 = 1,86,000 kcal. Over 4 hours that is about 46,500 kcal/h; over 1 hour, about 1,86,000 kcal/h. If the jacket can remove about 60,000 kcal/h (U × A of 1,200 kcal/h·°C and 50 °C between batch and cooling water), the 4-hour dosing is controllable and the 1-hour dosing is not: the temperature will rise even with the cooling working perfectly.

This check is why a dosing time that worked in the lab often fails at scale. The lab flask has ten times more cooling area per litre than a plant reactor, so it could absorb a fast addition that the plant cannot. The mixing scale-up article explains why the area per volume falls.

Why colder is not always safer Going deeper

A natural instinct with an exotherm is to run it colder. It feels safer: more margin to the boiling point. But a colder reaction is slower, and a slower reaction accumulates more reagent during dosing.

In the example, dosing over 4 hours at 60 °C gives an MTSR of about 80 °C. The same dosing at 40 °C gives about 126 °C, because the reaction rate roughly quarters and reagent piles up. The batch is cooler while it runs, but much more dangerous if cooling fails. Worse, a reaction that is too cold may stall almost completely; if someone then warms it up, everything accumulated reacts at once.

That is why many dosing steps have a minimum temperature interlock as well as a maximum: no dosing below the temperature at which the reaction is known to keep up.

Protecting a dosing step Going deeper

  • Measure it. Reaction calorimetry (RC1 or similar) gives the heat of reaction, the heat release profile and the accumulation directly. DSC or ARC on the final mixture gives the decomposition onset and TD24. See thermal safety testing.
  • Limit the dosing rate physically: a restriction orifice, a dosing pump that cannot exceed the safe rate, or a dosing vessel that holds only one batch quantity.
  • Interlock the feed: stop dosing on high temperature, low temperature, agitator stopped (rotation, not just motor current) and cooling failure.
  • Never restart blindly after an agitator stop or a long dosing interruption: sample or wait for the accumulated reagent to react first.
  • Size the relief for the runaway case if the class is 3 or above, not only for fire.
On the plant

Agitator stops are the most dangerous moment in a dosing step. Reagent added without mixing can sit as a separate layer. When the agitator restarts, it all mixes and reacts at once. The rule is simple: if the agitator stops, stop dosing, and don't restart the agitator until someone has assessed the accumulation.

Questions people ask

What is accumulation?

The fraction of dosed reagent that has not reacted yet. MTSR = process temperature + accumulation × ΔTad.

Is it safer to run colder?

Not always. Colder means slower, which means more accumulation. In the example, 40 °C gives a higher MTSR than 60 °C for the same dosing time.

How do you choose the dosing time?

Long enough that the heat release stays within the plant's cooling capacity and that the MTSR stays below the boiling point and TD24, confirmed by reaction calorimetry.
