Chemical Bonding Basics

Basic chemistry · Bonds, polarity and intermolecular forces · November 2026

Why does methanol boil at 65 °C when ethane, a molecule of almost the same weight, boils at −89 °C? Why will toluene not mix with water, and why does a reaction that forms nitrogen gas release so much energy? All of these come down to bonds: the strong ones inside molecules and the weak ones between them. This page covers both, with the plant consequences.

Three ways atoms hold together Basics

  • Ionic bonds. One atom gives electrons to another, and the resulting positive and negative ions attract. Sodium chloride is the standard example. Ionic solids have high melting points, dissolve in water, and conduct electricity when molten or dissolved.
  • Covalent bonds. Two atoms share a pair of electrons. Almost all organic molecules, solvents and gases are held together this way. A double bond shares two pairs and a triple bond three.
  • Metallic bonds. Metal atoms pool their outer electrons into a shared “sea”. That is why metals conduct electricity and heat, bend instead of shattering, and why bonding and earthing straps work.

Real bonds sit on a scale between purely covalent and purely ionic. Where a bond sits depends on the electronegativity difference between the two atoms.

Electronegativity differenceBond type (rough guide)Example
below about 0.4Non-polar covalentC–H, C–C, H–H
about 0.4 to 1.7Polar covalentO–H, C–O, C–Cl, N–H
above about 1.7Mostly ionicNa–Cl, K–F

The cut-offs are rules of thumb, not sharp lines. The periodic table article lists the electronegativity values.

Polar bonds and polar molecules Basics

In a polar bond the shared electrons sit closer to the more electronegative atom, which carries a small negative charge (δ−) while the other atom carries a small positive charge (δ+). Whether the whole molecule is polar also depends on its shape.

  • Water is bent, so its two O–H dipoles add up. Water is strongly polar.
  • Carbon dioxide is straight, so its two C=O dipoles cancel. CO2 is non-polar even though each bond is polar.
  • Carbon tetrachloride is symmetrical and non-polar. Chloroform (CHCl3) and dichloromethane have one or two hydrogens in place of chlorine, so they are polar.

Polarity is what makes “like dissolves like” work. Polar solvents dissolve polar and ionic substances; non-polar solvents dissolve non-polar ones. Salts dissolve in water but not in toluene. A hydrocarbon impurity stays in the toluene layer during an aqueous wash.

Forces between molecules Basics

Bonds inside a molecule are strong. The forces between molecules are much weaker, but they decide boiling point, viscosity, solubility and whether two liquids mix. In order of strength:

  • Hydrogen bonds. A hydrogen on oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine is attracted to a lone pair on a neighbouring O, N or F. This is the strongest intermolecular force and the reason water, alcohols, acids and amines boil higher than their weight suggests.
  • Dipole–dipole forces. Polar molecules line up so that δ+ ends face δ− ends. Acetone and acetonitrile are held together mainly this way.
  • Dispersion (London) forces. Every molecule has them, from brief fluctuations in its electron cloud. They grow with molecular size, which is why boiling points rise along a series such as pentane, hexane, heptane.
CompoundMolar massBoiling pointMain force between molecules
Ethane, C2H630−89 °CDispersion only
Methanol, CH3OH3265 °CHydrogen bonding
Dimethyl ether, CH3OCH346−24 °CDipole–dipole
Ethanol, C2H5OH4678 °CHydrogen bonding
Hydrogen sulfide, H2S34−60 °CDipole–dipole, weak
Water, H2O18100 °CStrong hydrogen bonding

Ethanol and dimethyl ether have the same formula, C2H6O. The only difference is whether the oxygen carries a hydrogen, and that alone shifts the boiling point by about 100 °C.

What intermolecular forces mean on the plant Working level

  • Solvent choice and extraction. Water and toluene separate cleanly because toluene cannot hydrogen bond. Methanol, acetone and THF mix with water in all proportions, so they cannot be used as the organic layer in an aqueous wash, and they drag product into the aqueous phase.
  • Azeotropes. Liquids that attract each other differently from how they attract themselves form azeotropes, such as ethanol and water at about 95.6 percent by mass. Simple distillation stops there.
  • Viscosity. Glycols and glycerol are viscous because each molecule forms several hydrogen bonds. That matters for agitation, pumping and heat transfer.
  • Drying. Water bound by hydrogen bonds to a polar product, or held in a crystal as a hydrate, is harder to remove than surface water. Loss on drying can stall well above zero.

Bond energies and the heat of reaction Working level

Breaking a bond takes energy; forming one releases energy. A reaction is exothermic when the bonds formed are stronger in total than the bonds broken. Average bond enthalpies give a quick estimate.

Bonds broken: 4 C–H + 2 O=O = 4(99) + 2(118) = 632 kcal
Bonds formed: 2 C=O + 4 O–H = 2(191) + 4(111) = 826 kcal
ΔH ≈ 632 − 826 = −194 kcal/mol (measured: about −192 kcal/mol) Average bond enthalpies give estimates within roughly 10 percent for simple molecules. Use measured heats of formation or calorimetry for design.

The method is rough but it shows where heat comes from. The thermochemistry article covers the accurate route through heats of formation.

Why nitrogen-releasing compounds are hazardous Going deeper

The N≡N triple bond is one of the strongest bonds known, about 225 kcal/mol. Two consequences follow, and both matter in a plant.

  • Nitrogen gas is very unreactive, so it is the standard inerting gas.
  • Any molecule that can fall apart to N2 releases a great deal of energy when it does. Azides, diazonium salts, diazo compounds and some hydrazines and tetrazoles belong here. They can decompose fast, with heat and a sudden volume of gas.

The same logic applies to peroxides, where a weak O–O bond breaks easily. When a structure contains these groups, screen it by DSC before scale-up. See how to read DSC, ARC and RC1 data.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between ionic and covalent bonds?

In an ionic bond one atom gives electrons to another and the ions attract, as in sodium chloride. In a covalent bond two atoms share electrons, as in water or methane. Most real bonds are somewhere in between, depending on the electronegativity difference.

Why does water have such a high boiling point?

Each water molecule forms strong hydrogen bonds with its neighbours. Breaking these takes a lot of energy, so water boils at 100 °C, while hydrogen sulfide, a heavier molecule without strong hydrogen bonds, boils at about −60 °C.

Why do toluene and water not mix?

Water molecules attract each other strongly through hydrogen bonds. Toluene cannot form hydrogen bonds, so mixing would break water–water attractions without replacing them. The two liquids stay as separate layers.

Can bond energies predict the heat of reaction?

They give a rough estimate, usually within about 10 percent for simple reactions. For design and safety work use heats of formation or measure the heat of reaction by calorimetry.
