Effluent Treatment Basics

Plant Engineering · Environment · October 2026

Pharmaceutical effluent is difficult: high COD, high salt, residual solvents and compounds that bacteria don’t like to eat. Treating it all together usually fails. Treating it well starts long before the ETP, with knowing which stream is which. This article explains the key parameters, the treatment steps, and why segregation is the most important design decision.

The numbers that describe effluent Basics

  • COD (chemical oxygen demand): the oxygen needed to oxidise everything organic chemically, in mg/L. The overall organic load.
  • BOD (biochemical oxygen demand, usually 3 days at 27 °C in India): the oxygen bacteria use to eat the organics. The biodegradable part.
  • BOD/COD ratio: above about 0.4, the effluent is reasonably biodegradable; below about 0.2, biology alone won't do much.
  • TDS (total dissolved solids): salts from neutralisations and work-ups. High TDS, above roughly 1–2 %, inhibits normal biological treatment.
  • pH, TSS, oil and grease, ammoniacal nitrogen and specific toxic compounds.

Typical general discharge standards for inland surface water in India include COD 250 mg/L, BOD 30 mg/L, TSS 100 mg/L and pH 5.5–9, but the consent conditions for each site set the actual limits, and many pharma sites must achieve zero liquid discharge.

Segregation: the first decision Basics

A small volume of mother liquor or aqueous layer can carry most of a site's COD and salt. Mixing it with large volumes of washings dilutes the concentration, but not the load, and turns it into a big stream that is hard to treat. Separate streams at the source:

  • High-COD / high-TDS: aqueous layers, mother liquors, scrubber blowdowns. Send to stripping and evaporation.
  • Low-COD / low-TDS: equipment washings, utility blowdowns, floor washings. Send to biological treatment.

Collect high-strength streams in separate, labelled tanks with an analysis for each batch. The material balance of each process tells you in advance what each stream carries.

High-COD and high-TDS streams Working level

  1. Solvent stripping: a steam stripper removes volatile solvents, recovering them and cutting COD. A few percent of methanol in an aqueous stream can be a large share of its COD.
  2. Neutralisation and pre-treatment: pH adjustment, removal of oil and suspended solids.
  3. Multiple effect evaporator (MEE): evaporates the water using steam several times over; a four-effect MEE typically evaporates around 3–3.5 kg of water per kg of steam. The condensate goes to biological treatment or reuse.
  4. Agitated thin film dryer (ATFD): dries the MEE concentrate to a solid salt, which goes to a hazardous waste facility.

Biological treatment Working level

Low-strength streams and MEE condensate go through a conventional sequence: equalisation (to even out swings in flow and composition), neutralisation, primary clarification, aeration (activated sludge or MBBR) and secondary clarification. Bacteria need steady conditions: sudden slugs of solvent, high salt or toxic compounds kill the biomass, and recovery takes weeks. Equalisation and stream control protect them.

Tertiary treatment and ZLD Going deeper

After biology, tertiary steps polish the water: pressure sand filters, activated carbon, sometimes ozonation, and ultrafiltration plus reverse osmosis for reuse. In a zero liquid discharge scheme, RO permeate is reused as cooling tower or boiler feed make-up, and the RO reject goes back to the MEE, so no liquid leaves the site.

ETP operating cost is dominated by steam for the MEE and ATFD, power for aeration, chemicals and hazardous solid disposal. The cheapest reduction is at source: recover solvents better, reduce salt by choosing reagents and work-ups carefully, and keep high-strength streams small.

On the plant

Track COD load (kg/day), not just concentration. A process change that halves water use while keeping the same load doubles the concentration and looks alarming, but is often good for the ETP. A change that keeps concentration constant but doubles volume is the one to worry about.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Mixing high-strength mother liquors with large washing streams.
  • Sending high-TDS effluent straight to biological treatment.
  • Ignoring solvent content as a source of COD.
  • Shock loading the aeration tank.
  • Watching concentration instead of load.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between COD and BOD?

COD is all oxidisable organics; BOD is the biodegradable part.

What is an MEE?

A multi-stage evaporator that reuses vapour, evaporating several kg of water per kg of steam.

What does zero liquid discharge mean?

No liquid leaves the site; water is reused and salts leave as solids.
