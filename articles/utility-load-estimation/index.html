Utility Load Estimation for a New Block

Plant Departments · Utilities · October 2026

Undersized utilities are one of the most common reasons a new block cannot reach its capacity. Oversized ones waste capital and run inefficiently. A utility load estimate done equipment by equipment, with honest peak and average figures and a sensible diversity, avoids both.

Start with a load list Basics

For every consumer, list the utility, the peak load, the average load over a batch and the hours of use. Sources: the energy balance for heating and cooling, nameplate or calculated power for motors, and process requirements for nitrogen, vacuum and water. A spreadsheet row per equipment per utility is enough.

Steam, cooling water, chilled water and brine Basics

Heating and cooling duties come from the batch energy balance in kcal/h. For steam, kg/h = duty ÷ latent heat at the supply pressure (about 517 kcal/kg at 2 kg/cm² g). For cooling media, flow in m³/h = duty ÷ (density × cp × ΔT ÷ 1000); for water with a 5 °C rise that is duty ÷ 5,000. Chilled water and brine loads convert to TR at 3,024 kcal/h per TR. The steam heating calculator and heat load calculator help with the batch duties.

Peak duty matters: cooling a reaction mass from reflux at the start of a cooldown draws far more than the batch average. Use the peak for headers and control valves and a realistic combined peak for the plant.

Diversity Working level

Not every reactor heats or cools at the same moment. The diversity factor (combined peak ÷ sum of individual peaks) is less than 1. Estimate it from the occupancy chart: line up the batches as they will actually run and add up the loads hour by hour. Without that, factors of 0.6–0.8 are common assumptions for multi-reactor batch blocks, but a block with synchronised batches or one dominant consumer needs the real profile.

Power, nitrogen, air, vacuum and water Working level

  • Power: motor kW × load factor; add HVAC, lighting and the chiller and air compressor power that the new loads create in the utility block.
  • Nitrogen: inerting purges, blanketing, pressure transfers and filter drying; peaks are short but large (inerting and LOC).
  • Compressed air: instruments, pneumatic pumps and tools.
  • Vacuum: distillation and drying loads (vacuum systems).
  • Water: process water, purified water if needed, cooling tower make-up and cleaning.

Check against the utility block Going deeper

Compare the new combined peak with spare capacity in each utility: boiler and steam header, chillers and brine plants, cooling tower, transformer and DG, nitrogen and air plants. Distance matters too: long headers need line sizing for the new flow (line sizing). Add a margin for future products, typically stated explicitly (for example 20 %) so it is not added twice by different people.

On the plant

Ask the utility team for real logged peaks from the existing plant, not nameplate capacities. A chiller rated at 200 TR that delivers 150 TR on a summer afternoon is a 150 TR chiller for planning.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Using batch average duties to size headers.
  • Adding margins at every step so the total is double what is needed.
  • Ignoring the extra load the new block puts on chillers and compressors.
  • Nameplate capacity assumed available in summer.
  • No check of header sizes for the new flows.

Questions people ask

How do I convert a cooling duty to TR?

Divide the duty in kcal/h by 3,024.

What is a diversity factor?

The ratio of the combined actual peak load to the sum of individual peak loads, less than 1 because not all equipment peaks together.

How much steam does a heating duty need?

Duty in kcal/h divided by the latent heat of steam at the supply pressure, about 517 kcal/kg at 2 kg/cm2 g.
