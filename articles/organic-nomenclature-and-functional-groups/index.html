Organic Nomenclature and Functional Groups

Basic chemistry · Names, structures and reactive groups · November 2026

A route sheet from R&D is written in structures and names: 4-methyl-2-pentanone, N,N-dimethylformamide, a tert-butyl carbamate. An engineer who can read them knows straight away which steps will release gas, which streams are flammable, which layer the product sits in and which intermediates need a thermal screen. You do not need to be an organic chemist for that, only to know the naming rules and the main functional groups.

Why carbon chemistry is its own subject Basics

Carbon forms four bonds and bonds strongly to itself, so it builds chains, branches and rings of almost any size. Millions of compounds are possible from a handful of elements: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, halogens, sulfur and phosphorus. The skeleton of carbon and hydrogen carries the bulk of the molecule; the functional groups attached to it decide how it reacts.

Chemists draw structures as skeletal formulas: each corner and line end is a carbon, hydrogens on carbon are not drawn, and every other atom is written in. The molecule structure tool draws one from a name or SMILES string and gives the formula and molar mass.

The ending tells you the bonding: -ane for only single bonds (alkanes), -ene for a C=C double bond (alkenes), -yne for a triple bond (alkynes). A six-membered ring with alternating double bonds is benzene, the parent of the aromatic compounds: toluene is methylbenzene, xylene is dimethylbenzene.

Reading and building an IUPAC name Basics

  1. Find the longest carbon chain that contains the main functional group. It gives the parent name.
  2. Number the chain so the main group gets the lowest number.
  3. Name the side groups (methyl, ethyl, chloro, bromo, nitro) with their positions, in alphabetical order.
  4. Add the ending for the main group: -ol for an alcohol, -one for a ketone, -oic acid for an acid.

Amines and alcohols are further classed as primary, secondary or tertiary by how many carbons are attached to the nitrogen, or to the carbon bearing the OH. It matters: tertiary alcohols dehydrate easily in acid, and primary amines react where tertiary ones cannot.

Groups that need a thermal screen Working level

Some groups store a lot of energy and can decompose fast, with heat and gas. When any of these appear on a route sheet, ask for DSC data before scale-up:

  • Nitro and nitrate esters
  • Azides, diazo compounds and diazonium salts
  • Peroxides and peracids
  • N-oxides and hydroxylamines
  • Acetylenes and metal acetylides
  • Hydrazines and some N–N rich rings such as tetrazoles and triazoles

Isomers and chirality Going deeper

Isomers have the same formula but different structures, and so different properties.

  • Structural isomers have a different connection of atoms. Ethanol and dimethyl ether are both C2H6O; one is a liquid that mixes with water, the other a gas.
  • Positional isomers on a ring are called ortho, meta and para (1,2-, 1,3- and 1,4-). Nitration of toluene gives a mixture, and separating the isomers is often the hardest step.
  • Cis and trans (or E and Z) isomers differ in the arrangement around a double bond.
  • Enantiomers are mirror images, like left and right hands, found when a carbon carries four different groups. They have identical physical properties but can act differently in the body, so many drugs are made and specified as one enantiomer, labelled R or S. Chiral purity is then a release test, and racemisation (loss of one hand) is a process risk.

Questions people ask

How do I name a simple organic compound?

Find the longest carbon chain containing the main functional group, number it so that group gets the lowest number, name the side groups with their positions in alphabetical order, and add the ending for the main group, such as -ol for an alcohol or -one for a ketone.

What is a functional group?

An atom or group of atoms attached to the carbon skeleton that decides how the molecule reacts, for example an OH in alcohols, a C=O in ketones or an NH2 in primary amines. Compounds with the same functional group behave in similar ways.

Which functional groups are hazardous to scale up?

Groups that store a lot of energy, such as nitro, azide, diazo, peroxide, N-oxide and acetylene, and reagents that react violently with water, such as acid chlorides. These need thermal screening and careful quench design before scale-up.

Why do enantiomers matter in pharma?

Mirror-image forms of a molecule have the same physical properties but can have different effects in the body. Many drugs are specified as a single enantiomer, so chiral purity becomes a release test and racemisation becomes a process risk.
