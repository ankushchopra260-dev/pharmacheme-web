Reactor Selection: Batch, Semi-batch, CSTR or Flow

Process Design · Reaction Engineering · October 2026

Most API and fine chemical plants are built around batch reactors, and for good reasons. But the choice of reactor type changes conversion, selectivity, safety and cost, and sometimes a different type solves a problem that no amount of batch optimisation can. This article compares the four basic reactor types and when each one makes sense.

The four basic types Basics

  • Batch: everything charged, reacted, discharged. Flexible, simple, the standard multi-purpose plant reactor.
  • Semi-batch: one reactant dosed into the other. The workhorse for exothermic reactions because dosing controls the heat. See semi-batch dosing.
  • Continuous stirred tank (CSTR): feed in and product out continuously, well mixed, so the whole contents sit at the outlet composition.
  • Plug flow (PFR): a tube where each slice of fluid travels through without mixing with the slices before and after. Every slice behaves like a small batch reactor. Modern flow chemistry reactors approach this. See flow chemistry.

Residence time and conversion Working level

For a simple first-order reaction with rate constant k and residence time τ, the product kτ (the Damköhler number) sets conversion:

Batch or plug flow: X = 1 − e−kτ
One CSTR: X = kτ / (1 + kτ)

To reach 90 % conversion, a batch or plug flow reactor needs kτ = 2.3; a single CSTR needs kτ = 9, almost four times the volume. The reason is that the CSTR runs entirely at the outlet concentration, where the reactant is depleted and the rate is slow. Two CSTRs in series need about 1.9 times the plug flow volume; more tanks in series approach plug flow.

Selectivity: where the reactor type really matters Working level

Conversion only costs volume. Selectivity costs yield and purity, and the reactor type affects it strongly:

  • Consecutive reactions (A → desired B → unwanted C): plug flow or batch, stopped at the right time, gives the most B. A CSTR keeps B in the reactor with fresh conversion going on, so more C forms.
  • Parallel reactions where the side reaction has a higher order in a reagent: keep that reagent dilute. Semi-batch dosing or a CSTR does this naturally.
  • Fast reactions limited by mixing: local excess at the feed point gives impurities. Small, well-mixed flow reactors or good feed-point design help.

Heat removal and safety Working level

A large batch reactor has little cooling area per litre and holds the whole inventory of reactive material. A CSTR at steady state holds mostly converted material, so the energy available for a runaway is smaller. A flow reactor has a tiny volume and enormous area per volume, so it can run reactions that would be unsafe or impossible in batch: very fast, very exothermic, or with unstable intermediates that are made and consumed within seconds.

The trade-off is complexity: continuous plants need steady feeds, accurate pumps, good analytics and a plan for start-up, shut-down and blockages.

Choosing in practice Going deeper

SituationUsually best
Many products, small volumes, frequent changesBatch or semi-batch
Exothermic reaction, moderate rateSemi-batch with controlled dosing
Large single product, liquid phase, slow reactionCSTRs in series
Fast, highly exothermic or hazardous chemistry; unstable intermediatesFlow (plug flow) reactor
Consecutive side reaction you must avoidBatch or plug flow, stopped at the right conversion
On the plant

A batch reactor with a long dosing time is already halfway to a CSTR: the reactor holds mostly converted material with fresh reagent arriving slowly. Thinking of it that way helps explain why some impurities rise when dosing is slowed down, and others fall.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Comparing reactor volumes without checking the conversion each achieves.
  • Choosing a CSTR for a reaction with a consecutive side reaction.
  • Moving to flow chemistry without solving solids handling.
  • Ignoring the inventory of reactive material in safety comparisons.
  • Treating semi-batch as batch in kinetic and safety calculations.

Questions people ask

Why does a CSTR need more volume than a batch reactor?

It runs entirely at the low outlet concentration; for 90 % conversion about 3.9× the volume.

What is the Damkohler number?

Rate constant × residence time; it sets conversion.

When should a flow reactor be used?

For fast, exothermic or hazardous chemistry and short-lived intermediates.
