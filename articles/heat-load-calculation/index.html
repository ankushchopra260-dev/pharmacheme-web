How to Calculate Heat Load & Recovery Time

Chemical Engineering · Heat Transfer · Updated October 2026

“How long will it take to heat the batch?” and “How long to strip the solvent?” are two questions every shift asks. Both come from the same two ideas: how much heat the batch needs, and how fast the jacket can push it through the reactor wall. Get those right and you can predict batch time cycles, spot a fouled jacket, and explain why the last 10 °C of cooling always takes forever.

How much heat does the batch need? Basics

Heat goes into a batch in two ways. Sensible heat raises its temperature; you can feel it, hence the name. Latent heat changes liquid into vapour without changing the temperature; it is hidden, which is what “latent” means. A boiling batch can take heat all day and stay at the same temperature, because every kilocalorie is going into vapour.

Sensible heat Q = M × cp × ΔT   (kcal)
Latent heat Q = mvapour × λ   (kcal)

For a sense of scale: heating 1 kg of water by 1 °C takes 1 kcal, but boiling it off takes 539 kcal. For toluene the numbers are about 0.41 kcal to warm it 1 °C and 86 kcal to boil it. Boiling is where the energy goes.

How fast can the jacket deliver it? Basics

Heat flows from the jacket into the batch through the reactor wall, and the rate depends on three things:

Q (kcal/h) = U × A × ΔT U: overall heat transfer coefficient (kcal/h·m²·°C), how easily heat crosses the wall. A: wetted area (m²), only the part of the wall with liquid behind it. ΔT: jacket temperature minus batch temperature.

That last term is the one people underestimate. Heat flow is proportional to the temperature difference, so as the batch gets close to the jacket temperature, heat flow tails off. That single fact explains most of what follows.

Heat-up and cool-down time Working level

Because the heat flow falls as the batch warms, the temperature does not rise in a straight line. It rises quickly at first and then creeps towards the jacket temperature. With the jacket at a constant temperature Tj (condensing steam is close to this), the time to go from T0 to T is:

t = (M × cp) / (U × A) × ln[(Tj − T0) / (Tj − T)] M × cp / (U × A) is the batch's time constant: the bigger the batch or the worse the heat transfer, the slower it responds.

Worked example

A 5 KL glass-lined reactor with 4,000 kg of batch (cp ≈ 0.48 kcal/kg·°C), 9 m² of wetted area and U ≈ 300 kcal/h·m²·°C. Heat from 30 to 80 °C on 3 barg steam (143.6 °C):

M cp / UA = 4,000 × 0.48 / (300 × 9) ≈ 0.71 h ≈ 42 min
t = 42 × ln[(143.6 − 30) / (143.6 − 80)] = 42 × ln(1.79) ≈ 25 min

Now cool it back from 80 to 40 °C with cooling water at 32 °C:

t = 42 × ln[(80 − 32) / (40 − 32)] = 42 × ln(6) ≈ 76 min

Three times longer for a smaller temperature change. When the batch is at 40 °C, the cooling water has only 8 °C of driving force left. This is why plants switch to chilled water or brine for the last part of a cool-down.

Solvent recovery time Working level

Once the batch is boiling, the temperature stops rising and every kcal from the jacket goes into vapour:

Vapour rate (kg/h) = Heat duty (kcal/h) / λ (kcal/kg)
Recovery time (h) = solvent to recover (kg) / vapour rate (kg/h)

500 kg of solvent with λ = 86 kcal/kg, and a jacket that can deliver 34,400 kcal/h at the boiling temperature:

Vapour rate = 34,400 / 86 = 400 kg/h  →  time = 500 / 400 ≈ 1.25 h

Add the heat-up time before boiling starts, and expect the last part to be slower than the first: the wetted area shrinks and, with mixed solvents, the boiling point rises as the light solvent leaves. The solvent recovery article covers boiling points under vacuum and the condenser side.

Wetted area and reactor occupancy Going deeper

A reactor's jacket area on the datasheet is the full jacket. The area that matters is the part covered by liquid inside. For a vertical reactor with a 2:1 dished bottom head:

  • The bottom head holds a volume of πD³/24 and has an inside area of about 1.08 D².
  • Above the head, each metre of liquid height in the shell adds πD m² of wetted wall.

So the wetted area depends on how full the reactor is. A 5 KL reactor (about 1.75 m diameter) at 80 % occupancy has roughly 9–11 m² wetted, depending on how high the jacket goes; at 30 % (near the end of a solvent strip) only about 5 m². Since Q = U A ΔT, the jacket delivers half the heat just when the operator is waiting for the batch to finish. For recovery time estimates, use the average of the wetted area at the start and end of distillation, which is what the Heat Load calculator does.

On the plant

Low occupancy also hurts agitation: an anchor or a pitched-blade turbine that is barely covered splashes instead of mixing, so U falls as well. If a batch must run at low fill, check the impeller is still submerged at the end of the step.

What sets U, and how it decays Going deeper

U is not one property; it is several resistances in series, like resistors in a circuit:

1/U = 1/hinside + wall thickness / kwall + 1/hjacket + fouling resistances

The biggest one wins. In a glass-lined reactor the glass layer (k ≈ 0.86 kcal/h·m·°C) is a large resistance, so U is typically 215–345 kcal/h·m²·°C. In stainless steel with good agitation and steam, 430–690 is common. Viscous batches, poor agitation and scale in the jacket all pull it down.

The practical check: record the heat-up time of a standard batch when the reactor is clean. If the same batch takes 30 % longer a year later, U has dropped by about 30 %, and the jacket probably needs descaling. Hot water jackets scale faster than steam jackets because of the hard water circulating through them.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Using the full jacket area from the datasheet instead of the wetted area.
  • Using an average ΔT as if the batch temperature were fixed, which underestimates heat-up time near the end.
  • Forgetting the reactor steel and the jacket contents in M cp for short batches; they can add 10–20 %.
  • Taking latent heat at atmospheric pressure for a vacuum recovery (it is a little higher at lower temperatures).
  • Designing a cool-down on cooling water alone when the target is within 10 °C of the cooling water temperature.

Questions people ask

How do you calculate the heat-up time of a jacketed reactor?

t = (M cp / UA) × ln[(Tj − T0) / (Tj − T)]. For the 4,000 kg example on 3 barg steam, about 25 minutes from 30 to 80 °C.

Why does cooling take longer than heating?

The driving force is smaller. Cooling water at 32 °C has only 8 °C of driving force when the batch is at 40 °C, while steam at 144 °C still has over 60 °C when the batch reaches 80 °C.

How do you calculate solvent recovery time?

Vapour rate = heat duty / latent heat; time = solvent mass / vapour rate. 500 kg at 86 kcal/kg and 34,400 kcal/h takes about 1.25 hours of boiling.
