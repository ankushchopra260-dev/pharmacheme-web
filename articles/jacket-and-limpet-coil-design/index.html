Reactor Jackets and Limpet Coils

Process Design · Heat Transfer · October 2026

Heat-up and cool-down times on a plant reactor are often blamed on the vessel size or the glass lining. Sometimes the real culprit is the jacket: cooling water creeping through a wide annulus at a few centimetres per second. This article explains the main jacket types, why velocity on the jacket side matters, and when it stops mattering.

Jacket types Basics

  • Conventional jacket: an outer shell around the vessel, with a gap of typically 50–100 mm. Simple and cheap, good for steam. For liquids, the flow area is huge and the velocity very low unless agitation nozzles or a spiral baffle are fitted.
  • Half-pipe or limpet coil: a pipe cut in half lengthwise and welded in a spiral around the vessel. Small flow area, so high velocity and good heat transfer with liquids. Strong, so it suits high-pressure utilities and thin vessel walls.
  • Dimple jacket: a thin sheet with pressed dimples welded to the vessel. Light and cheap, used for moderate pressures.
  • Internal coils: extra area inside the vessel, at the cost of cleaning difficulty and obstruction to mixing. Not possible in glass-lined vessels.

Why jacket-side velocity matters Basics

Take a 3 m³ vessel, 1.6 m in diameter. A 50 mm conventional jacket has a flow area of about 0.27 m². Twenty m³/h of cooling water flows through it at about 0.02 m/s: barely moving. The jacket-side film coefficient is then only a few hundred W/m²K, set mostly by natural convection.

The same 20 m³/h through an 80 NB half-pipe coil (flow area about 0.0024 m²) runs at over 2 m/s, and the film coefficient rises to around 7,500 W/m²K. Agitation nozzles in a conventional jacket, which inject water tangentially to create swirl, give something in between.

Film coefficients and overall U Working level

1/U = 1/hprocess + twall/kwall + Rfouling + 1/hjacket

The overall coefficient is limited by the worst resistance. With a slow conventional jacket, the jacket side dominates and U is about 220 W/m²K. With agitation nozzles, U roughly doubles. With a half-pipe coil, the jacket side almost disappears from the sum, and U is limited by the process side and the wall: about 550 W/m²K in this example. Going faster on the jacket side beyond this gains little.

For glass-lined vessels, the glass layer (about 1–2 mm, with thermal conductivity around 1 W/m·K) adds a resistance of the same order as the process film, so U is usually 200–400 W/m²K whatever the jacket. Improving the process side, with better agitation, often helps more there.

Steam, water, brine and thermic fluid Working level

  • Steam: condensing film coefficients are very high, so a conventional jacket is fine. Inlet at the top, condensate out at the bottom through a steam trap. Air and non-condensables must be vented or they blanket the surface.
  • Cooling water and chilled water: velocity matters, as above. Inlet at the bottom so the jacket stays full and air is pushed out at the top.
  • Brine and glycol: more viscous at low temperature, so film coefficients are lower; velocity matters even more.
  • Thermic fluid (hot oil): for temperatures above about 180 °C without high-pressure steam. Lower film coefficients than steam; usually in half-pipe coils.

Multi-fluid jackets and practical problems Going deeper

Most multi-purpose reactors switch the same jacket between steam, water and brine. That is convenient but causes trouble if not done carefully:

  • Drain and blow out the previous fluid before switching. Water left in a jacket that receives brine dilutes the brine and can freeze; steam into a jacket full of brine causes water hammer.
  • Thermal shock: switching from steam to cold brine on a glass-lined vessel can crack the glass. Respect the manufacturer's temperature-difference limits. See glass-lined reactor care.
  • Fouling: cooling tower water scales jackets over the years. A periodic acid clean of the jacket, done under a permit, can restore a surprising amount of cooling.
  • Air pockets: a jacket filled from the top traps air and loses area at the top. Fill from the bottom and vent at the top.
On the plant

Measure the cooling water temperature rise across the jacket during a cool-down. A rise of only 1–2 °C with a large temperature difference to the batch means the water isn't picking up heat: low velocity or fouling. A rise of 10 °C or more means the water flow itself is the limit.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Expecting good cooling from water in a conventional jacket with no nozzles.
  • Feeding cooling water from the top and trapping air.
  • Switching utilities without draining the previous fluid.
  • Blaming the glass for slow cooling without checking the jacket side.
  • Never cleaning scaled jackets.

Questions people ask

What is a limpet coil?

A half-pipe spiral welded to the shell: high velocity and strong.

Why is cooling slow in a conventional jacket?

Water moves very slowly in the wide annulus, so the film coefficient is low.

What overall U can a reactor jacket achieve?

About 200–600 W/m²K for stainless, 200–400 for glass-lined.
