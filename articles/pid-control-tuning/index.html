PID Control and Tuning

Plant Engineering · Process Control · October 2026

A badly tuned temperature loop on a reactor shows up as a batch that swings 5 °C around its set point, a jacket valve that never stops moving, or a heat-up that overshoots into an exotherm. Most loops are tuned once at commissioning and never touched again. This article explains what a PID controller actually does and how to tune one sensibly.

Feedback control in one paragraph Basics

A controller compares the measured value (the process variable, PV) with the set point (SP), and moves an output (usually a valve) to reduce the difference, the error. It repeats this many times a second. Everything else is about how much to move the valve for a given error, and how to avoid overreacting.

P, I and D Basics

Output = Kc × [ e + (1/Ti) ∫ e dt + Td × de/dt ]
  • Proportional (Kc): output proportional to the error now. More gain gives a faster response but, beyond a point, oscillation. Proportional action alone leaves a steady offset.
  • Integral (Ti): keeps adding output while any error remains, so it removes the offset. A short integral time (strong integral action) causes overshoot and slow oscillation.
  • Derivative (Td): reacts to how fast the error is changing, damping overshoot on slow processes. It amplifies noise, so it is often left at zero, except on slow temperature loops.

Know the process: gain, lag and dead time Working level

Most plant loops behave roughly like a first-order lag with dead time:

  • Process gain K: how much the PV finally changes for a 1 % change in output.
  • Time constant τ: how long it takes to get about 63 % of the way to its new value.
  • Dead time θ: how long before anything happens at all. Thermowells, long pipes and jacket volumes add dead time.

Dead time is what limits control. The bigger θ is compared with τ, the gentler the tuning must be. Find these three numbers with a simple step test: put the controller in manual, change the output by 5–10 %, and record the PV.

Tuning: a simple, safe method Working level

The IMC (internal model control) rules give robust PI settings from the step test, with one choice: the desired closed-loop speed λ. Choosing λ equal to the dead time is a reasonable start; a larger λ is gentler.

Kc = τ / [K × (λ + θ)]    Ti = τ

For a process with K = 1, τ = 10 min and θ = 2 min, with λ = 2 min: Kc = 2.5 and Ti = 10 min. Compare that with a loop left at Kc = 1 and Ti = 5 min: slower to start, but overshooting because the integral is too strong.

Reactor temperature control in practice Going deeper

  • Cascade control: the batch temperature controller (master) sets the jacket temperature set point; the jacket temperature controller (slave) moves the valves. The fast slave loop corrects utility disturbances before they reach the batch. Tune the slave first.
  • Split range: one output drives cooling for 0–50 % and heating for 50–100 %. Check the overlap so both valves aren't open together, and that there's no dead band where nothing happens.
  • Integral windup: during a long heat-up, the error stays large and the integral term keeps growing; when the batch reaches set point, the stored integral causes a big overshoot. Use anti-windup in the DCS, or switch from ramp to control mode near the set point.
  • Limit the jacket: clamp the master output so the jacket set point stays within the glass-lined vessel's thermal shock limits and the product's wall temperature limit.
  • Exotherms: overshoot in a heat-up can start a reaction. Tune exothermic reactors for little or no overshoot, even at the cost of speed.
On the plant

Before retuning, look at the valve. A sticky valve with a dead band makes every loop oscillate, whatever the tuning. Put the loop in manual, move the output in 1 % steps, and see if the valve follows.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Tuning a loop without knowing the process dead time.
  • Too much integral action, causing slow oscillation.
  • No anti-windup on batch heat-up.
  • Tuning the master loop before the slave loop.
  • Blaming tuning for a sticky valve.

Questions people ask

What does the integral term in PID do?

It removes steady offset; too much causes overshoot and oscillation.

What is cascade control on a reactor?

A batch controller setting a jacket controller, which moves the valves.

How do you tune a PI controller simply?

Step test, then IMC: Kc = τ/[K(λ+θ)], Ti = τ.
