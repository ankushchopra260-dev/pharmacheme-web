Thermochemistry and Heat of Reaction

Basic chemistry · Enthalpy, heat of reaction and temperature rise · November 2026

Every reactor jacket, condenser and chiller on a plant is sized from a heat balance, and the largest term in many of them is the heat of reaction. Thermochemistry is the part of chemistry that puts a number on it. Below, the quantities are in kcal and °C, a heat of reaction is worked out from tables, and then turned into the figure that matters most for safety: how hot the batch would get if cooling failed.

Heat, enthalpy and the sign convention Basics

Enthalpy (H) is the heat content of a system at constant pressure, which is how most plant equipment runs. What we measure and use is the change in enthalpy, ΔH.

  • Exothermic: heat is released, ΔH is negative. Neutralisations, nitrations, hydrogenations, oxidations, Grignard formation.
  • Endothermic: heat is absorbed, ΔH is positive. Evaporation, dissolving many salts, cracking.

Units on this site are kcal, kcal/kg and kcal/mol. 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ; 1 kcal is the heat to warm 1 kg of water by about 1 °C.

Two practical points come straight from this table. Water needs about twice the heat of a typical solvent to warm, and two to seven times as much to boil off, which is why aqueous evaporations are steam hungry. And a refluxing solvent removes heat at a fixed temperature, which is why reflux is used as a temperature limiter in exothermic steps, as long as the condenser can keep up. Detailed values are in the steam tables and the solvent data pages.

Heats of formation and Hess’s law Working level

The standard heat of formation, ΔHf°, is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states at 25 °C and 1 atm. Elements themselves are zero. Values are tabulated for thousands of compounds.

Hess’s law says the heat of a reaction depends only on the start and end states, not the route. So:

ΔHr° = Σ ΔHf°(products) − Σ ΔHf°(reactants) Multiply each value by its coefficient in the balanced equation. Watch the phase: liquid and gaseous water differ by the heat of vaporisation.

Worked example: hydrogenation of nitrobenzene Working level

C6H5NO2(l) + 3 H2(g) → C6H5NH2(l) + 2 H2O(l)
ΔHf°, kcal/mol: nitrobenzene +3.0 · hydrogen 0 · aniline +7.6 · water −68.3
ΔHr° = [7.6 + 2(−68.3)] − [3.0 + 0] = −132 kcal/mol Heats of formation of the liquids from the NIST Chemistry WebBook, rounded. Published values for this reaction are around −130 kcal/mol.

Per kilogram of nitrobenzene (molar mass 123.11), that is 132,000 / 123.11 ≈ 1,070 kcal/kg. For comparison, boiling off a kilogram of methanol absorbs about 263 kcal. This one reaction is among the most exothermic run routinely in batch plants, which is why it is run semi-batch with the hydrogen feed as the control.

Adiabatic temperature rise Working level

The most useful single safety number is the temperature the batch would reach if all the remaining reaction happened with no cooling at all.

ΔTad = (−ΔHr × n) / (m × Cp)
1,000 kg batch with 150 kg nitrobenzene (1.218 kmol), mixture Cp about 0.5 kcal/kg·°C:
ΔTad = (132 × 1,218) / (1,000 × 0.5) ≈ 320 °C n in mol, ΔHr in kcal/mol, m in kg. Illustrative figures; use the measured heat of reaction and the real batch mass for design.

A 320 °C rise from a 60 °C start would boil the solvent and push the mass into the range where nitro compounds decompose. All the nitro compound is in the reactor from the start, so the full rise is available. What limits it is the hydrogen supply: the reaction cannot release heat faster than hydrogen arrives, so on loss of cooling, stopping the hydrogen feed and venting the headspace stops the heat. Watch also for the hydroxylamine intermediate building up, because it is thermally unstable in its own right. A common screening rule (Stoessel) treats an adiabatic rise above about 200 °C as high severity and below about 50 °C as low, provided no gas is formed. See semi-batch dosing and accumulation and hydrogenation safety.

These terms are easy to forget in a heat balance and can be as large as the reaction itself in a work-up. Include them when sizing cooling for quench and neutralisation steps; the heat load calculator can carry them.

Estimated or measured? Going deeper

Tables and Hess’s law give a good first number, but they assume pure compounds in standard states and say nothing about side reactions or decomposition. For anything going to scale:

  • Use reaction calorimetry to measure the heat of the real reaction under real dosing, and how much accumulates.
  • Use DSC or adiabatic (accelerating rate) calorimetry to find decomposition energy and onset temperature of the reaction mixture and isolated intermediates.
  • Compare the maximum temperature the batch could reach (process temperature plus ΔTad of the accumulated material) with the boiling point and the decomposition onset.

The DSC, ARC and RC1 article explains how to read those results.

Questions people ask

What does a negative heat of reaction mean?

The reaction releases heat: it is exothermic. The enthalpy of the products is lower than that of the reactants, and the difference appears as heat that the jacket or condenser has to remove.

How do I calculate a heat of reaction from tables?

Use Hess’s law: add up the heats of formation of the products, each multiplied by its coefficient, and subtract the same sum for the reactants. Make sure the phases match the real conditions, especially for water.

What is adiabatic temperature rise?

The temperature increase the batch would see if the reaction heat stayed in the mass with no cooling. It equals the heat released divided by the batch mass times its specific heat, and it is the first number used to judge the severity of a cooling failure.

Why measure the heat of reaction if it can be calculated?

Calculated values assume pure compounds and ignore side reactions, mixing heats and accumulation. Reaction calorimetry measures the real heat under real dosing, which is what cooling and safety design need.
