Heat Exchanger Design Basics

Process Design · Heat Transfer · October 2026

Every heat exchanger, from a reactor jacket to a shell-and-tube condenser, is sized with one short equation: Q = U × A × ΔT. The skill is in the three pieces: how much heat you must move, what temperature difference you really have, and how well heat crosses the wall once the exchanger has been in service a year. This article walks through each one and finishes with a full sizing example.

Step 1: the heat duty Basics

The duty is the heat that must move from the hot stream to the cold stream per second. With no phase change:

Q = m × cp × (Tin − Tout) Q in kW when m is in kg/s and cp in kJ/kg·K. With condensation or boiling, add m × latent heat.

The duty must balance: what the hot side loses, the cold side gains (minus a few percent lost to the surroundings). Use this to find the flow you need on the other side. If 4,000 kg/h of a solvent stream with cp 2.0 cools from 120 to 60 °C, the duty is (4,000/3,600) × 2.0 × 60 ≈ 133 kW. Cooling water warming from 30 to 40 °C then needs 133 / (4.18 × 10) ≈ 3.2 kg/s, about 11.5 m³/h.

Step 2: the temperature difference (LMTD) Basics

The temperature difference between the two streams is not constant: it is large at one end of the exchanger and smaller at the other. The right average is not the simple arithmetic mean but the log mean:

LMTD = (ΔT1 − ΔT2) / ln(ΔT1 / ΔT2) ΔT1 and ΔT2 are the hot-minus-cold temperature differences at the two ends.

The log mean is always smaller than the arithmetic mean, and the difference grows as the two end differences move apart. When one end difference gets very small, the LMTD falls sharply and the area shoots up: that is the “temperature pinch” designers watch for.

Counter-current vs co-current Working level

In counter-current flow the streams enter at opposite ends; in co-current (parallel) flow they enter at the same end. For our example, hot 120 → 60 °C and cold 30 → 40 °C:

  • Counter-current: ΔT1 = 120 − 40 = 80, ΔT2 = 60 − 30 = 30. LMTD = 50 / ln(2.67) ≈ 51.0 °C.
  • Co-current: ΔT1 = 120 − 30 = 90, ΔT2 = 60 − 40 = 20. LMTD = 70 / ln(4.5) ≈ 46.5 °C.

Counter-current needs about 9 % less area here. The bigger advantage is that it allows the cold outlet to get hotter than the hot outlet (a “temperature cross”), which co-current can never do. Co-current is still used where you want to limit the wall temperature for a heat-sensitive product, because the coldest fluid meets the hottest at the inlet.

Step 3: the U value Working level

U, the overall heat transfer coefficient (W/m²·K), is how easily heat crosses from one fluid to the other. It combines the film on the hot side, the wall, the film on the cold side and any fouling, all as resistances in series:

1/U = 1/hhot + twall/kwall + 1/hcold + Rf

The worst resistance dominates. A gas or a viscous liquid on one side caps U, whatever you do on the other side. Typical overall values for preliminary sizing:

ServiceTypical U (W/m²·K)
Water to water, shell-and-tube800 – 1,500
Organic solvent to cooling water300 – 600
Condensing steam to water1,500 – 4,000
Condensing solvent vapour to cooling water500 – 1,000
Gas to water20 – 200
Glass-lined reactor jacket200 – 400
Plate heat exchanger, water to water3,000 – 6,000

These are starting points. For a final design, calculate the film coefficients from the flows, properties and geometry, or use the vendor's software.

Fouling: designing for the dirty exchanger Working level

A clean exchanger only stays clean for a few weeks. Scale from cooling water, polymer or tar from process streams and corrosion products all add resistance. Designers allow for this with a fouling factor:

1/Udirty = 1/Uclean + Rf

With Uclean = 500 W/m²·K and Rf = 0.0005 m²K/W: 1/U = 0.002 + 0.0005 = 0.0025, so Udirty = 400. The exchanger needs 25 % more area. Typical Rf values: treated cooling tower water 0.0002–0.0004, river or untreated water 0.0005–0.001, clean organic solvents 0.0002, heavy or polymerising streams 0.0005 and up.

Be careful not to stack margins: a generous fouling factor, plus a 20 % area margin, plus a conservative U, can give an exchanger twice the size it needs. An oversized cooler with a control valve throttling the water runs at low velocity, which makes it foul faster.

A full worked example Working level

Cool 4,000 kg/h of process stream (cp 2.0 kJ/kg·K) from 120 to 60 °C with cooling water from 30 to 40 °C, in a shell-and-tube exchanger E-101.

  1. Duty: Q = 1.111 × 2.0 × 60 = 133 kW.
  2. Cooling water: 133 / (4.18 × 10) = 3.2 kg/s, about 11.5 m³/h.
  3. LMTD, counter-current: 51.0 °C.
  4. U: organic to water, clean 500, with Rf 0.0005 gives 400 W/m²·K.
  5. Area: A = Q / (U × LMTD) = 133,000 / (400 × 51.0) ≈ 6.5 m². Co-current would need 133,000 / (400 × 46.5) ≈ 7.2 m².

Specify the exchanger at 6.5 m² plus a modest margin (10–15 %), and check the tube-side velocity: cooling water below about 1 m/s fouls fast.

F factor, pinch and choosing the type Going deeper

The F correction factor. Real shell-and-tube exchangers with more than one tube pass are neither pure counter-current nor co-current. The LMTD is multiplied by a correction factor F (from charts or equations, based on the four temperatures): Q = U × A × F × LMTD. If F falls below about 0.8, the design is inefficient and sensitive; use more shells in series or a true counter-current design.

Approach temperature. The smallest temperature difference anywhere in the exchanger. Below about 5–10 °C for liquids, area grows very fast for little extra heat recovered. That is why a cooling water exchanger rarely cools a process stream below about 38–40 °C: the water arrives at 30–32 °C. Colder targets need chilled water or brine, which costs more per kW. See the chillers article.

Choosing the type. Shell-and-tube for high pressure, fouling services and solvent condensers; plate exchangers for clean liquids and close approaches (U is three to five times higher, but gaskets limit temperature and solvent compatibility); graphite or glass for corrosive acids; jackets and limpet coils for reactors.

On the plant

When a condenser “isn't working”, measure before you blame the design: inlet and outlet water temperatures, water flow, and vapour load. Nine times out of ten the water flow is half of design, the tubes are scaled, or non-condensable nitrogen is blanketing the surface.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Using the arithmetic mean temperature difference instead of the LMTD.
  • Forgetting the latent heat when a solvent vapour condenses.
  • Stacking fouling factor, area margin and conservative U on top of each other.
  • Ignoring non-condensables in vacuum condensers.
  • Taking U from a table for a viscous or gas service where it can be ten times lower.

Questions people ask

What is LMTD?

The effective average temperature difference along an exchanger: (ΔT1 − ΔT2) / ln(ΔT1/ΔT2).

Why is counter-current better?

It gives a higher LMTD (51 against 46.5 °C in the example), so less area, and it allows a temperature cross.

What is a fouling factor?

An extra resistance for deposits: 1/Udirty = 1/Uclean + Rf. An Rf of 0.0005 takes U from 500 to 400.
