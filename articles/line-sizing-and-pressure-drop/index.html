Line Sizing and Pressure Drop

Process Design · Fluid Flow · October 2026

Choosing a pipe size looks like a small decision, but it fixes the pump head, the energy bill, whether the pump cavitates, whether a solvent line charges up with static, and whether the line erodes or blocks. Most of it comes down to one number, velocity, and one equation for friction. This article goes through both, with the rules of thumb engineers actually use.

Start with velocity Basics

v = Q / A = 4Q / (π D²) v in m/s, Q in m³/s, D the pipe's inside diameter in m (not the nominal size).

Typical design velocities for liquids:

ServiceTypical velocityWhy
Pump suction0.6–1.5 m/sLow friction keeps NPSH available high
Pump discharge1.5–3 m/sBalance of pipe cost and pumping energy
Gravity drain0.3–1 m/sLittle head available; avoid running full of vapour
Cooling water headers1.5–2.5 m/sHigh enough to limit fouling
Low-conductivity solvent into a tank≤ 1 m/s until the inlet is submergedStatic charge generation

For 20 m³/h of water: 40 NB gives 4.2 m/s (too fast), 50 NB 2.6 m/s (good for a discharge), 80 NB 1.2 m/s (good for a suction).

Reynolds number: laminar or turbulent Basics

Re = ρ v D / μ

Below about 2,300 the flow is laminar: smooth layers, friction proportional to velocity. Above about 4,000 it is turbulent: eddies, friction roughly proportional to velocity squared. Water at 2.6 m/s in a 50 NB line has Re ≈ 135,000, well into turbulence. A 100 cP oil at the same velocity has Re ≈ 1,200, which is laminar, and behaves very differently.

Friction loss: the Darcy equation Working level

ΔP = f × (L / D) × ρ v² / 2 f is the Darcy friction factor: 64/Re for laminar flow; for turbulent flow from the Moody chart or the Colebrook or Swamee-Jain equations, depending on Re and the pipe roughness (about 0.045 mm for commercial steel).

For 20 m³/h of water in 50 NB steel, f ≈ 0.02 and the loss is about 1.3 bar per 100 m. In 80 NB it falls to about 0.18 bar per 100 m. That is the trade-off: friction falls steeply with diameter (roughly as D−5 at constant flow), while pipe cost rises with diameter. Velocities of 1.5–3 m/s for pumped liquids sit near the economic optimum for typical plant lengths.

Fittings, valves and equivalent length Working level

Elbows, tees, valves, strainers and entrances add loss. Two ways to include it:

  • K values: ΔP = K × ρ v²/2 for each fitting. Typical K: standard 90° elbow 0.3–0.75, open gate valve 0.15, open globe valve 6–10, swing check 2, tank entrance 0.5, exit 1.
  • Equivalent length: each fitting adds the friction of a straight length, for example about 30 pipe diameters for an elbow.

In compact plant piping, fittings often account for more loss than the straight pipe. A Y-strainer that is half blocked can account for more than everything else together; that's why strainers get differential pressure gauges.

Special cases: solvents, slurries, viscous liquids, gases Going deeper

  • Solvents and static: keep low-conductivity solvents at 1 m/s or less into tanks until the fill pipe is submerged, and at most about 7 m/s anywhere. See static electricity.
  • Slurries: keep velocity above the settling velocity of the solids, often 1.5–2.5 m/s, and avoid dead legs and low points where solids collect.
  • Viscous liquids: flow is often laminar and loss rises with viscosity; heat tracing or a larger line can save a lot of pump power. Use a positive displacement pump.
  • Gases and vapours: much higher velocities (10–30 m/s for process gas, lower for vacuum lines where every millibar matters). Vacuum lines from a condenser to the pump should be short and generously sized; see vacuum systems.
  • Steam and condensate: steam 20–35 m/s; condensate return lines carry flash steam and must be sized for the two-phase flow.

For a full network with several branches, use the line sizing design tool.

On the plant

When a pump “has become weak”, check the strainer and the line before the pump. Scale, a half-closed valve or an extra length of hose added for a temporary job can take more head than the pump has to give.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Using the nominal size instead of the inside diameter.
  • Sizing pump suction lines like discharge lines.
  • Ignoring fittings and strainers in compact piping.
  • Filling solvent tanks at high velocity through a top nozzle.
  • Forgetting that viscous liquids may be laminar.

Questions people ask

What velocity should a pump discharge line have?

1.5–3 m/s; suction lines 0.6–1.5 m/s.

How do you calculate pressure drop in a pipe?

Darcy: ΔP = f × (L/D) × ρv²/2, plus fittings.

What is the friction factor for laminar flow?

64/Re below Re ≈ 2,300.
