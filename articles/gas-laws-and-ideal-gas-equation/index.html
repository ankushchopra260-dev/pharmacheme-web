Gas Laws and the Ideal Gas Equation

Basic chemistry · PV = nRT and plant gas calculations · November 2026

Nitrogen for inerting, CO2 from a quench, hydrogen in a hydrogenator, vapour in a tank vent: gases are handled on every plant, and almost every gas calculation is the same equation, PV = nRT, with the units sorted out. Most errors are not in the physics but in gauge versus absolute pressure, °C versus kelvin, and Nm³ versus actual m³. This page covers all three with worked examples.

The simple gas laws Basics

Early experiments found how a fixed amount of gas behaves when one property changes:

  • Boyle’s law: at constant temperature, P1V1 = P2V2. Halve the volume and the pressure doubles.
  • Charles’s law: at constant pressure, V1/T1 = V2/T2. Gas expands as it warms.
  • Gay-Lussac’s law: at constant volume, P1/T1 = P2/T2. A closed vessel’s pressure rises as it warms.
  • Avogadro’s law: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of moles.

All four are parts of one equation.

The ideal gas equation Basics

PV = nRT P absolute pressure, V volume, n moles, T absolute temperature in kelvin (K = °C + 273.15).
Value of RUnits to use with it
0.08314bar·m3 / (kmol·K)
8.314kPa·m3 / (kmol·K), or J / (mol·K)
0.08206atm·L / (mol·K), or atm·m3 / (kmol·K)
1.987cal / (mol·K), or kcal / (kmol·K)

Two rules prevent most mistakes:

  • Temperature in kelvin. Using °C gives nonsense, and division by zero at 0 °C.
  • Pressure absolute. Plant gauges read gauge pressure. Absolute = gauge + atmospheric, about 1.013 bar at sea level. A vessel at 2 bar(g) is at 3.013 bar(a). Vacuum gauges need the same care: 700 mmHg vacuum is 60 mmHg absolute.

Molar volume, Nm³ and actual m³ Basics

One kilomole of any ideal gas occupies:

  • 22.41 m3 at 0 °C and 1.013 bar (the usual “normal” conditions, Nm3)
  • 23.64 m3 at 15 °C and 1.013 bar (a common “standard” condition, Sm3)
  • 24.47 m3 at 25 °C and 1.013 bar

Normal and standard conditions are not defined the same way by every supplier or code. Always state the reference temperature and pressure next to a gas flow.

Actual flow = Normal flow × (T / 273.15) × (1.013 / Pabs)
100 Nm3/h of nitrogen at 40 °C and 0.5 bar(g): 100 × (313.15 / 273.15) × (1.013 / 1.513) = 76.8 m3/h actual Line sizing, velocity and flowmeter selection use the actual flow. Purchase, consumption and mass balance use the normal flow.

Worked example: nitrogen for a pressure purge Working level

A reactor has 5 m3 of headspace at 25 °C and atmospheric pressure. It is pressurised with nitrogen to 3 bar(g). How much nitrogen does one cycle use?

n before = P V / R T = 1.013 × 5 / (0.08314 × 298.15) = 0.204 kmol
n after = 4.013 × 5 / (0.08314 × 298.15) = 0.810 kmol
Nitrogen added = 0.605 kmol = 16.9 kg = 13.6 Nm3 If the vessel is then vented to atmosphere, the oxygen fraction falls by the pressure ratio, 1.013 / 4.013, about a quarter per cycle.

Three cycles take air at 20.9 percent oxygen down to about 0.3 percent, using about 41 Nm3 of nitrogen. The inerting and limiting oxygen concentration article covers the target and the methods in detail.

Gas mixtures and partial pressure Working level

Dalton’s law: in a gas mixture each component behaves as if it were alone, and the total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures. For an ideal mixture:

pi = yi × Ptotal yi is the mole fraction, which for ideal gases also equals the volume fraction. Air at 1.013 bar has an oxygen partial pressure of 0.209 × 1.013 = 0.212 bar.

Partial pressure is what drives gas solubility and reaction rate. Hydrogen at 5 bar(g) in a reactor whose headspace also holds solvent vapour has a hydrogen partial pressure lower than the gauge suggests. It is also why a vessel containing a volatile solvent shows a higher pressure than the gas alone would give: the solvent’s vapour pressure adds to it.

Gas density and heavier-than-air vapours Working level

ρ = P M / R T
Air at 25 °C, 1.013 bar: 1.013 × 28.96 / (0.08314 × 298.15) = 1.18 kg/m3 M is the molar mass in kg/kmol. Doubling the pressure doubles the density; heating from 25 to 100 °C lowers it by about a fifth.

The ratio of a vapour’s molar mass to 28.96 tells you how it moves when released. Dichloromethane vapour (M = 84.9) is about three times as heavy as air, and toluene vapour (92.1) a little heavier still. These vapours flow along floors and collect in pits, trenches and drains, which is why gas detectors for them are mounted low and confined-space entry needs testing near the floor.

Closed vessels and temperature Working level

A closed receiver holds air at 1.013 bar(a) and 25 °C. In the sun it reaches 55 °C.

P2 = 1.013 × 328.15 / 298.15 = 1.115 bar(a), a rise of about 0.10 bar

The gas alone gives only a small rise. If the vessel also contains a volatile solvent, the solvent’s vapour pressure rises much faster with temperature and usually dominates. That is the reason tanks breathe and closed drums bulge; see storage tank breathing losses.

When the ideal gas equation is not good enough Going deeper

Real molecules have size and attract each other. The ideal gas equation works well for nitrogen, oxygen, hydrogen, argon and air at the pressures in most batch plants (up to roughly 10 bar) and ambient or higher temperatures. It becomes less accurate:

  • at high pressure, such as gas cylinders and high-pressure hydrogenation;
  • near the condensation point, for vapours and for gases that liquefy easily such as ammonia, chlorine, CO2 and propane;
  • at low temperature.

The correction is the compressibility factor, PV = ZnRT, where Z = 1 for an ideal gas. Z comes from charts, an equation of state or property tables. For a hydrogen cylinder at about 200 bar, Z is roughly 1.1, so the ideal gas equation overestimates the contents by about 10 percent. For condensable gases, use property tables or vapour pressure data rather than PV = nRT.

Questions people ask

Why must temperature be in kelvin in PV = nRT?

The gas laws are proportional to absolute temperature, which starts at zero kelvin. Using °C gives wrong ratios, and at 0 °C would divide by zero. Add 273.15 to the Celsius value.

What is the difference between Nm³ and m³?

Nm³ is the volume the gas would occupy at normal conditions, usually 0 °C and 1.013 bar, and measures an amount of gas. Actual m³ is the volume at the real line temperature and pressure, and is what you use for line sizing and velocity.

How much nitrogen does a pressure purge use?

Calculate the moles in the headspace before and after pressurising with PV = nRT, using absolute pressures. For 5 m³ taken from atmospheric to 3 bar(g) at 25 °C, one cycle uses about 0.6 kmol, or 13.6 Nm³.

When is the ideal gas equation not accurate?

At high pressure, near the condensation point and at low temperature. For cylinders, high-pressure hydrogen or easily liquefied gases such as ammonia and chlorine, use a compressibility factor or property tables.
