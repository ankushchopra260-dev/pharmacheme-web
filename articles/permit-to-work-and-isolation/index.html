Permit to Work and Isolation

Process Safety · Safe Work · October 2026

Most serious accidents during maintenance happen at the boundary between operations and maintenance: a line opened that was still connected to pressure, a pump started that someone was working on, a permit that didn’t survive a shift change. A permit to work is the system that manages that boundary. This article explains how it works, and how to isolate equipment properly.

What a permit to work is for Basics

A permit to work is a written agreement between the person in control of the plant and the person doing the work. It records what will be done, where, what hazards exist, how the equipment has been made safe, what precautions are needed, and who has checked each one. Its value is not the paper but the conversation and checks it forces: the plant is handed over in a defined state, and handed back only when the work is complete and the equipment ready.

Types of permit Basics

  • Hot work: welding, grinding, cutting, anything that makes sparks or flames. Needs gas testing and fire watch.
  • Cold work / line break: opening process lines and equipment.
  • Confined space entry: vessels, tanks, pits. Needs isolation, ventilation, atmosphere testing for oxygen, flammables and toxics, a standby person and a rescue plan.
  • Electrical work and work at height, often as certificates attached to a main permit.

Where several jobs touch the same equipment, they must be cross-referenced so one permit isn't closed while another depends on the same isolation.

The isolation hierarchy Working level

Valves leak and get opened by mistake. The more severe the hazard, the more secure the isolation must be:

  1. Single closed valve: only for low-hazard fluids and short, simple jobs.
  2. Double block: two closed valves in series.
  3. Double block and bleed: two closed valves with a vent or drain open between them, so any leak through the first valve goes to the bleed, not to the work.
  4. Positive isolation: a spade (blind) or spectacle blind inserted, or a spool removed and the open ends blanked.

Toxic, flammable, high-pressure or hot fluids, and any confined space entry, generally need positive isolation. The isolation points must be marked on a P&ID copy attached to the permit. See how to read a P&ID.

Lockout, tagout and verification Working level

Every energy source that could hurt someone must be isolated and locked: electrical supply, pneumatic and hydraulic power, stored pressure, springs, gravity (raised parts), and process chemicals. Each person working puts their own lock on the isolation (a multi-lock hasp or lock box) and only they remove it.

Then verify: try to start the motor locally; check the pressure gauge reads zero and open the bleed; test the atmosphere before entry. An isolation that hasn't been tested is an assumption.

Handover: the Piper Alpha lesson Going deeper

On 6 July 1988, on the Piper Alpha platform in the North Sea, a pressure safety valve had been removed from a condensate pump for maintenance and replaced with a blind flange that was not leak-tight. The permit for that work was not effectively communicated at the evening shift handover. When the other pump tripped, the night shift started the pump with the missing valve. Condensate leaked, ignited and exploded, and the fires that followed destroyed the platform. 167 men died.

The inquiry found weaknesses in the permit to work system, especially in shift handover and in cross-referencing permits. The lesson for every plant: a permit is only as good as the handover. Active permits should be visible on a board in the control room, discussed at every handover, and suspended permits must leave the equipment in a clearly safe and labelled state.

On the plant

Walk the job with the permit in hand before signing it. Most permit failures are found in the field: a blind in the wrong place, a valve that isn't the one on the drawing, a drain that is blocked.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Signing permits in the office without visiting the job.
  • Treating a stop button or a closed valve as an isolation.
  • Not verifying the isolation before starting work.
  • Permits not discussed at shift handover.
  • Closing a permit while another job depends on the same isolation.

Questions people ask

What is double block and bleed?

Two closed valves with an open bleed between them, so leakage goes to the bleed, not the work.

When is positive isolation needed?

For toxic, flammable, high-energy fluids, long jobs and any confined space entry.

What does LOTO mean?

Isolating, locking and tagging every energy source, then verifying it before work.
