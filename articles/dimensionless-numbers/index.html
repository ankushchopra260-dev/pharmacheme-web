Dimensionless Numbers in Chemical Engineering

Fundamentals · Flow, heat, mass, mixing and reaction · November 2026

A dimensionless number is a ratio of two competing effects with all the units cancelled out: inertia against viscosity, convection against conduction, reaction against diffusion. Because it has no units, the same value means the same behaviour in a lab flask and a plant reactor. That is why correlations, regime maps and scale-up rules are all written in dimensionless numbers. This guide covers 34 numbers used every day in chemical and pharmaceutical plants, grouped by subject. For each one you get what it means, the values that matter, where it is used, and a calculator that works it out and explains your result.

How to read a dimensionless number Basics

Almost every dimensionless number is a ratio: this effect ÷ that effect. Read it that way and the value explains itself. A Reynolds number of 75,000 means inertia beats viscosity by a wide margin, so the flow is turbulent. A Biot number of 0.02 means heat crosses the inside of a solid fifty times more easily than its surface, so the solid heats evenly.

  • Far from 1: one effect dominates, and you can usually ignore the other.
  • Close to 1: both effects matter, and simple rules break down.
  • Regime limits (such as Re = 2,100 in a pipe) come from experiments, and are approximate.
  • Correlations such as Nu = 0.023·Re0.8·Pr0.4 link dimensionless numbers, so one equation covers every fluid, size and speed in its range.

Units must be consistent inside each number. The calculators below take plant units (kcal, °C, cP, mm) and convert them for you.

Fluid flow

Re Reynolds number

Re = ρ·v·D / μ

Ratio of: Inertial forces ÷ viscous forces

The single most used number in chemical engineering. It tells you whether a flow is smooth and layered (laminar) or chaotic and mixing (turbulent), and so which friction factor, heat transfer and mass transfer correlations apply.

ValueWhat it means
below 2,100Laminar in a pipe: smooth layers, parabolic velocity profile, poor radial mixing
2,100 to 4,000Transition: unstable, avoid designing here
above 4,000Turbulent: flat profile, good mixing, friction depends on roughness

Where you use it

  • Pipe and line sizing, and choosing the friction factor
  • Heat exchanger and jacket correlations
  • Flowmeter selection: orifice and vortex meters need turbulent flow
  • Viscous products: a thick slurry at low velocity is often laminar

Try it on a real case: Pipe pressure drop calculator

f Friction factor (Darcy and Fanning)

ΔP = fD·(L/D)·ρv²/2, with fD = 4·fF

Ratio of: Wall shear stress ÷ kinetic energy of the flow

Turns a pipe length into a pressure drop. Two conventions exist, and mixing them is a classic error: the Darcy factor is four times the Fanning factor. Laminar flow gives fD = 64/Re; in turbulent flow it depends on Re and the relative roughness ε/D.

ValueWhat it means
laminarfD = 64/Re, roughness does not matter
turbulent, smooth pipefD about 0.015 to 0.025 for usual plant flows
fully roughfD depends only on ε/D; old or corroded pipe can double it

Where you use it

  • Pressure drop in lines and headers
  • Pump head and NPSH checks
  • Relief valve inlet and outlet losses

Try it on a real case: Pipe pressure drop calculator

Fr Froude number

Fr = v / √(g·L) (sometimes defined as v²/(g·L))

Ratio of: Inertial forces ÷ gravity forces

Governs flows with a free surface, where gravity shapes the liquid: open channels, partly full pipes, waves, and vortices in stirred tanks. Check which definition a correlation uses: one is the square of the other.

ValueWhat it means
below 0.31 (v/√gD)Gravity drain line runs self-venting: gas escapes back up the line, no gulping or surging
0.31 to 1Drain line may surge and carry gas down; size the line larger
above 1 (open channel, hydraulic depth basis)Supercritical flow in channels; jets and hydraulic jumps

Where you use it

  • Sizing gravity drain and overflow lines from vessels and columns
  • Vortex formation in unbaffled agitated tanks
  • Hydraulic models of channels and spillways

Eu Euler number

Eu = ΔP / (ρ·v²)

Ratio of: Pressure forces ÷ inertial forces

A pressure loss expressed in velocity heads. Fitting loss coefficients (K values), orifice and valve coefficients are Euler numbers in disguise: K = 2·Eu.

ValueWhat it means
constantIn turbulent flow through fittings and orifices the Euler number is nearly constant, so ΔP rises with v²

Where you use it

  • Loss coefficients for valves, bends, tees and strainers
  • Orifice and nozzle sizing
  • Scaling pressure drop with flow: double the flow, four times the ΔP

Try it on a real case: Control valve sizing

We Weber number

We = ρ·v²·L / σ

Ratio of: Inertial forces ÷ surface tension forces

Decides whether drops and bubbles break up. High We means the flow can tear a drop apart; low We means surface tension holds it together. In stirred vessels the drop size in a liquid–liquid dispersion falls as the impeller Weber number rises, roughly d/D ∝ We−0.6.

ValueWhat it means
below about 1Surface tension dominates: drops and bubbles stay whole
about 12Critical value for a drop breaking up in a gas stream
well above 12Atomisation: sprays, mist formation

Where you use it

  • Liquid–liquid extraction and dispersion in reactors
  • Spray nozzles, scrubbers and atomisers
  • Mist carry-over from vessels

Ca Capillary number

Ca = μ·v / σ

Ratio of: Viscous forces ÷ surface tension forces

Matters where liquids flow through narrow gaps or pores, or coat surfaces. At low Ca surface tension controls: liquid is held in pores and films stay thin.

ValueWhat it means
below about 10−5Capillary forces dominate: liquid trapped in pores and cake
10−5 to 10−3Mixed
above 10−3Viscous forces start to move trapped liquid

Where you use it

  • Draining and deliquoring filter cakes
  • Wetting and coating
  • Flow in packed beds and porous media

Try it on a real case: Filtration calculator

Bo Bond number (Eötvös number)

Bo = Δρ·g·r² / σ (r = drop or tube radius)

Ratio of: Gravity forces ÷ surface tension forces

Decides the shape of drops and bubbles and whether gravity or surface tension wins. With the radius as the length, a vertical tube holds a liquid plug when Bo is below about 0.84; larger drops and bigger tubes let gravity win.

ValueWhat it means
below about 0.84Surface tension wins: round drops, a liquid plug stays in a vertical tube
above about 0.84Gravity wins: drops flatten, liquid drains from the tube

Where you use it

  • Drop and bubble size in settlers and sparged reactors
  • Why small-bore lines hold liquid slugs
  • Liquid hold-up in packing

Ma Mach number

Ma = v / c, with c = √(γ·R·T / M)

Ratio of: Flow velocity ÷ speed of sound

Tells you whether gas compressibility matters. Below about 0.3 a gas can be treated as incompressible. Near Ma = 1 the flow chokes: no more mass can pass however low the downstream pressure.

ValueWhat it means
below 0.3Compressibility negligible: liquid-style pressure drop methods are fine
0.3 to about 0.7Use compressible-flow methods; many flare and vent headers are limited to this range
approaching 1Choked flow: noise, vibration and high back pressure

Where you use it

  • Relief valve tailpipes and flare headers
  • High-velocity gas and steam lines
  • Vent and blowdown sizing

Try it on a real case: Relief valve sizing

Particles and separation

Rep Particle Reynolds number

Rep = ρf·v·d / μ

Ratio of: Inertial ÷ viscous forces around a particle

The Reynolds number based on particle size and the velocity of the particle relative to the fluid. It sets the drag law, and so how fast particles settle or how easily they are carried.

ValueWhat it means
below 0.3Stokes regime: drag coefficient CD = 24/Rep, settling velocity ∝ d²
0.3 to 500Intermediate regime
500 to 2×105Newton regime: CD ≈ 0.44, settling velocity ∝ √d

Where you use it

  • Settling in decanters, thickeners and knock-out pots
  • Fluidised beds and pneumatic conveying
  • Crystal suspension in agitated vessels

Try it on a real case: Knock-out pot sizing

Ar Archimedes number

Ar = g·d³·ρf·(ρp − ρf) / μ²

Ratio of: Gravity (buoyancy-corrected) ÷ viscous forces

Uses only fluid and particle properties, not the velocity, so it gives the settling velocity directly without trial and error. Also used for the minimum fluidisation velocity and bubble rise.

ValueWhat it means
below about 35Stokes regime: Rep = Ar/18
35 to 9×104Intermediate: Rep ≈ 0.153·Ar0.714
above 9×104Newton regime: Rep ≈ 1.74·Ar0.5 (Rep above about 500)

Where you use it

  • Terminal settling velocity of crystals, catalyst and droplets
  • Minimum fluidisation velocity
  • Checking whether a decanter or thickener can separate a solid

Try it on a real case: Knock-out pot sizing

Stk Stokes number

Stk = ρp·d²·v / (18·μ·L)

Ratio of: Particle stopping distance ÷ obstacle size

Tells you whether a particle or droplet follows the gas around an obstacle or crashes into it. That is how demisters, cyclones and impactors catch droplets and dust.

ValueWhat it means
below 0.1Particles follow the flow: poor capture
about 1Partial capture
above 1Particles hit the obstacle: good capture by impaction

Where you use it

  • Wire-mesh and vane demisters
  • Cyclones and impingement separators
  • Dust and mist sampling

Try it on a real case: Knock-out pot sizing

Heat transfer

Nu Nusselt number

Nu = h·L / k

Ratio of: Convective heat transfer ÷ conductive heat transfer across the fluid

The dimensionless heat transfer coefficient. Correlations give Nu from Re and Pr, and you get h back as h = Nu·k/L. For turbulent flow in tubes, Dittus–Boelter gives Nu = 0.023·Re0.8·Pr0.4 (heating).

ValueWhat it means
3.66Laminar, fully developed flow in a tube with constant wall temperature
tens to hundredsTurbulent flow in exchangers and jackets
Nu = 0.023·Re0.8·PrnDittus–Boelter: Re above 10,000, Pr 0.7 to 160; n = 0.4 heating, 0.3 cooling

Where you use it

  • Film coefficients in exchangers, jackets and coils
  • Comparing heat transfer at lab and plant scale
  • Checking a vendor U value

Try it on a real case: Jacketed vessel heat transfer

Pr Prandtl number

Pr = cp·μ / k

Ratio of: Momentum diffusivity ÷ thermal diffusivity

A property of the fluid alone. It compares how fast momentum and heat spread. High Pr fluids (oils, thick organics) have a thin thermal boundary layer buried in the velocity layer, so their heat transfer is poor and depends strongly on viscosity.

ValueWhat it means
about 0.7Gases
about 7 at 20 °C, 2 at 90 °CWater
100 to 10,000Oils, glycols and viscous organics
below 0.05Liquid metals

Where you use it

  • Every forced and natural convection correlation
  • Explaining why a viscous batch heats so slowly
  • Thermal fluid (hot oil) system design

Try it on a real case: Overall heat transfer coefficient

Gr Grashof number

Gr = g·β·ΔT·L³ / ν²

Ratio of: Buoyancy forces ÷ viscous forces

The natural-convection counterpart of Reynolds. Warm fluid rises because it is lighter; Gr measures how strongly that drives flow. Comparing Gr with Re² tells you whether natural or forced convection controls.

ValueWhat it means
Gr/Re² well below 1Forced convection controls
Gr/Re² about 1Mixed convection
Gr/Re² well above 1Natural convection controls

Where you use it

  • Heat loss from tanks, pipes and vessels without stirring
  • Cooling of a batch with the agitator stopped
  • Storage tank stratification

Try it on a real case: Insulation heat loss

Ra Rayleigh number

Ra = Gr·Pr = g·β·ΔT·L³ / (ν·α)

Ratio of: Buoyancy ÷ (viscous × thermal diffusion)

Sets the natural convection regime and the Nusselt number for free convection. For a vertical surface the boundary layer turns turbulent near Ra = 109.

ValueWhat it means
below 109 (vertical plate)Laminar natural convection: Nu ≈ 0.59·Ra1/4
above 109Turbulent natural convection: Nu ≈ 0.10·Ra1/3

Where you use it

  • Heat loss from vessel walls and hot pipes
  • Cooling of an unstirred batch or a drum
  • Air-side heat transfer on insulated surfaces

Try it on a real case: Insulation heat loss

Bi Biot number

Bi = h·L / ksolid, L = volume ÷ surface area

Ratio of: Resistance inside the solid ÷ resistance at its surface

Tells you whether a solid heats evenly or with a big temperature difference between surface and centre. Below 0.1 the inside is nearly uniform and simple lumped calculations work.

ValueWhat it means
below 0.1Lumped: the solid is at one temperature throughout
0.1 to 10Both resistances matter
above 10Inside conduction controls: the surface reaches the fluid temperature quickly, the centre lags

Where you use it

  • Heating and cooling drums, bags and blocks of product
  • Thawing frozen material
  • Fire exposure of vessel walls and structural steel

Fo Fourier number

Fo = α·t / L², α = k / (ρ·cp)

Ratio of: Heat conducted in time t ÷ heat stored

Dimensionless time for transient conduction. Heat penetrates about √(α·t), so Fo compares that depth with the size of the object. Charts of centre temperature against Fo and Bi solve most heating and cooling-of-solids problems.

ValueWhat it means
below 0.05Heat has barely reached the centre
about 0.2 and aboveThe centre is responding; one-term series solutions are accurate
above 1Close to steady state

Where you use it

  • Time to heat or cool a drum, block or tray of product
  • Dryer and freezer design
  • How long a vessel wall takes to warm in a fire

Pe Péclet number (heat)

Pe = v·L / α = Re·Pr

Ratio of: Heat carried by flow ÷ heat conducted

When Pe is large, heat moves with the fluid and conduction along the flow can be ignored. In laminar flow in tubes this sets the length of the thermal entrance region.

ValueWhat it means
below 1Conduction controls
1 to 100Both matter
above 100Heat is carried by the flow; axial conduction negligible

Where you use it

  • Laminar heat exchangers and long thin channels
  • Micro-reactors and flow chemistry
  • Packed and porous beds

Gz Graetz number

Gz = Re·Pr·D / L

Ratio of: Heat carried along the tube ÷ heat conducted to the wall

Used for laminar flow in tubes. A high Graetz number means a short tube compared with the thermal entrance length: the boundary layer is still thin, so heat transfer is higher than the fully developed value.

ValueWhat it means
below about 10Thermally developed: Nu tends to 3.66
above about 10Entrance effects: Sieder–Tate Nu = 1.86·Gz1/3·(μ/μw)0.14

Where you use it

  • Laminar heating of viscous products in tubes and coils
  • Hot oil and glycol exchangers
  • Scale-up of continuous flow reactors

St Stanton number and Colburn j-factor

St = h / (ρ·v·cp) = Nu / (Re·Pr); jH = St·Pr2/3

Ratio of: Heat transferred to the wall ÷ heat carried by the flow

Links heat transfer to friction. The Colburn analogy, jH ≈ fFanning/2, lets you estimate a heat transfer coefficient from a measured pressure drop, and explains why better heat transfer usually costs pressure drop.

ValueWhat it means
jH ≈ fF/2Colburn analogy, turbulent flow, no form drag

Where you use it

  • Compact and plate exchanger data, which are given as j and f curves
  • Estimating h from pressure drop
  • Trade-off between heat transfer and pumping power

Mass transfer

Sh Sherwood number

Sh = kc·L / D

Ratio of: Convective mass transfer ÷ diffusive mass transfer

The mass transfer twin of Nusselt. Correlations give Sh from Re and Sc, in the same form as Nu from Re and Pr. That is why heat and mass transfer correlations can often be swapped by replacing Nu with Sh and Pr with Sc.

ValueWhat it means
2A single sphere in still fluid: the diffusion limit
Sh = 2 + 0.6·Re1/2·Sc1/3Ranz–Marshall for spheres, drops and bubbles

Where you use it

  • Dissolution of solids and crystals
  • Mass transfer to drops and bubbles
  • Catalyst particles in slurry reactors

Try it on a real case: Gas–liquid mass transfer and kLa

Sc Schmidt number

Sc = μ / (ρ·D)

Ratio of: Momentum diffusivity ÷ mass diffusivity

The mass transfer twin of Prandtl, and a property of the fluid and solute only. Liquids have Sc around 1,000 because molecules diffuse far more slowly than momentum spreads, which is why liquid-side mass transfer is so often the bottleneck.

ValueWhat it means
0.5 to 2Gases
500 to 2,000Small molecules in water and thin solvents
above 10,000Viscous liquids, large molecules

Where you use it

  • Mass transfer correlations
  • Explaining why gas absorption into a liquid is liquid-film controlled
  • Scaling dissolution and extraction

Try it on a real case: Gas–liquid mass transfer and kLa

Pem Péclet number (mass)

Pe = v·L / D = Re·Sc

Ratio of: Mass carried by flow ÷ mass diffusing

Large Péclet numbers mean diffusion cannot even out concentration along the flow. In narrow channels and micro-reactors it tells you whether two streams will mix by diffusion before they leave.

ValueWhat it means
below 1Diffusion evens out concentration
well above 1Concentration differences are carried with the flow

Where you use it

  • Micro-mixers and flow chemistry
  • Chromatography and packed beds
  • Membrane processes

Try it on a real case: Continuous flow chemistry

Le Lewis number

Le = α / D = Sc / Pr

Ratio of: Thermal diffusivity ÷ mass diffusivity

Matters when heat and mass transfer happen together, as in drying, humidification and cooling towers. For air and water vapour Le is about 0.85, close enough to 1 that the wet-bulb temperature is nearly the adiabatic saturation temperature, which makes psychrometric charts simple.

ValueWhat it means
about 0.85Air and water vapour
much greater than 1Organic vapours in air: wet-bulb and adiabatic saturation temperatures differ

Where you use it

  • Dryer design and wet-bulb temperature
  • Cooling towers and humidifiers
  • Evaporation of solvents into gas streams

Ha Hatta number

Ha = √(k1·D) / kL (first-order reaction)

Ratio of: Reaction in the liquid film ÷ diffusion through the film

Decides where a gas–liquid reaction happens. A slow reaction takes place in the bulk liquid, so you need liquid volume. A fast reaction takes place in the film at the interface, so you need interfacial area, and the reaction speeds up absorption by an enhancement factor about equal to Ha.

ValueWhat it means
below 0.3Slow: reaction in the bulk; the bulk volume and kLa matter
0.3 to 3Intermediate
above 3Fast: reaction in the film; interfacial area matters, absorption enhanced about Ha times

Where you use it

  • Gas scrubbing with reactive solutions
  • Hydrogenation, oxidation and chlorination
  • Choosing between a bubble column, packed column and stirred tank

Try it on a real case: Packed scrubber calculator

Mixing and agitation

Rei Impeller Reynolds number

Re = ρ·N·D² / μ (N in rev/s, D = impeller diameter)

Ratio of: Inertial ÷ viscous forces at the impeller

Sets the mixing regime in a stirred vessel. In turbulent flow the power number is constant and baffles matter; in laminar flow the power draw is proportional to viscosity and close-clearance impellers are needed.

ValueWhat it means
below 10Laminar: use anchors, helical ribbons; power ∝ μ·N²·D³
10 to 10,000Transitional
above 10,000Turbulent: constant power number, baffles needed

Where you use it

  • Choosing the impeller type
  • Power and blend time correlations
  • Scale-up of reactors and crystallisers

Try it on a real case: Mixing scale-up calculator

Np Power number

Np = P / (ρ·N³·D⁵)

Ratio of: Drag on the impeller ÷ inertial forces

Each impeller type has its own power number, nearly constant in turbulent flow. Shaft power then follows from P = Np·ρ·N³·D⁵: double the speed and the power rises eight times. Working backwards from a measured power tells you what the impeller is really doing, which is useful when the drawing does not match the vessel.

ValueWhat it means
about 5Rushton disc turbine
about 1.3Pitched blade turbine, 45°
about 0.3Hydrofoil
about 0.35Retreat curve (glass-lined, one baffle)

Where you use it

  • Motor and gearbox sizing
  • Power per volume for scale-up
  • Comparing impellers

Try it on a real case: Agitator simulator

Nq Flow (pumping) number

Nq = Q / (N·D³)

Ratio of: Liquid pumped by the impeller ÷ impeller swept volume per revolution

How much liquid the impeller pushes out per revolution. With the vessel volume it gives the turnover time, a quick measure of how often the whole batch passes through the impeller.

ValueWhat it means
about 0.5 to 0.8Turbines and hydrofoils in turbulent flow

Where you use it

  • Heat transfer and feed dispersion checks

Fri Impeller Froude number

Fr = N²·D / g

Ratio of: Inertial (centrifugal) forces ÷ gravity at the impeller

Controls the surface vortex. In an unbaffled vessel the vortex deepens as Fr rises and can reach the impeller, drawing in gas. Baffles break the swirl, so in baffled vessels Fr matters mainly for surface aeration and for drawing down floating solids.

ValueWhat it means
lowFlat surface
risingDeeper vortex in unbaffled vessels, surface aeration in baffled ones

Where you use it

  • Unbaffled and glass-lined vessels with one baffle
  • Drawing down floating powders
  • Avoiding air entrainment

Nθ Blend time number

N·θ95 = 5.2 / (Np1/3·(D/T)²) (turbulent, H = T)

Ratio of: Blend time ÷ time for one impeller revolution

In turbulent flow the number of impeller revolutions needed to blend to 95 % uniformity is constant for a given geometry. That gives the blend time directly, and shows why blend time rises on scale-up at equal power per volume.

Reaction engineering

Da Damköhler number

Da = k·τ (first order; τ = residence or batch time)

Ratio of: Reaction rate ÷ transport (flow) rate

Compares how fast the reaction goes with how long the material stays. Small Da means the material leaves before reacting; large Da means conversion is complete. For a first-order reaction, a CSTR gives X = Da/(1 + Da) and a plug flow reactor gives X = 1 − e−Da.

ValueWhat it means
below 0.1Little conversion
about 1Partial conversion; reactor type matters
above 5Near-complete conversion in a plug flow reactor

Where you use it

  • Sizing CSTRs and tubular reactors
  • Flow chemistry residence time
  • Comparing reaction and mixing or heat removal times

φ Thiele modulus

φ = L·√(k / Deff); effectiveness η = tanh φ / φ (slab)

Ratio of: Reaction rate inside a catalyst pellet ÷ diffusion into it

Tells you whether the inside of a catalyst particle is being used. At large φ the reactant is consumed near the outer surface and the centre does nothing; smaller particles or more open pores help.

ValueWhat it means
below 0.4No diffusion limitation: η above about 0.95
0.4 to 3Partial limitation
above 3Strong limitation: η ≈ 1/φ, only the outer shell works

Where you use it

  • Choosing catalyst particle size
  • Hydrogenation catalyst (Pd/C, sponge nickel) loading
  • Explaining why a finer catalyst grade runs faster

Bod Bodenstein number (axial Péclet)

Bo = v·L / Dax

Ratio of: Transport by flow ÷ back-mixing (axial dispersion)

Measures how close a flow reactor is to plug flow. It comes from a tracer test. Large values mean little back-mixing, close to plug flow; small values mean the reactor behaves like a stirred tank. A rough equivalent number of tanks in series is Bo/2.

ValueWhat it means
below 1Close to a single stirred tank
1 to 10Strong back-mixing
10 to 100Intermediate
above 100Close to plug flow

Where you use it

  • Tubular and flow reactors
  • Packed beds and columns
  • Residence time distribution tests

γ Arrhenius number

γ = Ea / (R·T)

Ratio of: Activation energy ÷ thermal energy

Measures how sensitive a reaction is to temperature. High γ means a small temperature rise speeds the reaction a lot. That is good for control by temperature, and dangerous when cooling is lost, because the heat release accelerates.

ValueWhat it means
10 to 20Mild temperature sensitivity
about 20 to 40Typical organic reactions: the rate roughly doubles every 10 °C
above 40Very temperature-sensitive: small excursions matter

Where you use it

  • Thermal runaway screening
  • Choosing the reaction temperature
  • Scaling batch times between temperatures

Dimensionless numbers and scale-up Going deeper

Two systems behave the same if they are geometrically similar (same shape) and their important dimensionless numbers are equal. This is the principle of similarity, and it is the basis of every scale-up rule. The difficulty is that you rarely can keep them all equal at once:

  • In a stirred vessel scaled up by a factor of 10 in diameter, keeping the impeller Reynolds number constant would cut the speed 100 times. Keeping the Froude number constant would raise the power per volume about 3 times (√10). You cannot keep both.
  • Keeping power per volume constant, the usual choice, raises the Reynolds number, leaves the power number unchanged in turbulent flow, and makes the blend time longer.
  • Keeping heat transfer similar is harder still, because the wall area per volume falls as the vessel grows, whatever the agitation.

So you identify the number that controls the step that matters, keep that one constant, and check what happens to the others. The mixing scale-up calculator and the scale-up training module work through this.

Check yourself: quick quiz

Questions people ask

What is a dimensionless number?

A combination of quantities in which all the units cancel, so the result is a pure number. Most are ratios of two effects, such as inertia to viscosity (Reynolds) or convection to conduction (Nusselt). Because they have no units, the same value means the same behaviour at any size, which is why they are used for correlations and scale-up.

Why are dimensionless numbers important in chemical engineering?

They let one correlation cover many fluids, sizes and conditions; they decide which flow or transfer regime applies; and they are the basis of scale-up by similarity. Keeping the right dimensionless number constant between lab and plant keeps the behaviour that matters the same.

What is the difference between the Nusselt and Biot numbers?

Both are h·L/k, but Nusselt uses the conductivity of the fluid and describes convection at a surface, while Biot uses the conductivity of the solid and compares the resistance inside a solid with that at its surface.

Which dimensionless numbers are used in heat transfer?

Mainly Nusselt (the dimensionless heat transfer coefficient), Reynolds and Prandtl for forced convection, Grashof and Rayleigh for natural convection, Biot and Fourier for heating and cooling of solids, and Graetz, Péclet and Stanton for special cases.

Which dimensionless numbers matter for agitated vessels?

The impeller Reynolds number sets the regime, the power number gives the shaft power, the flow number the pumping rate, the Froude number the vortex, and the blend time number the mixing time. In gas–liquid and liquid–liquid systems the Weber number also matters.

Can you keep all dimensionless numbers the same on scale-up?

No. Geometric similarity fixes the shape, but you cannot keep Reynolds, Froude, power per volume and blend time all constant at a different size. You choose the number that controls the step that matters most and accept changes in the others.
