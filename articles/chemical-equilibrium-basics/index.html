Chemical Equilibrium Basics

Basic chemistry · K, Le Chatelier and temperature · November 2026

A batch that stops short of completion and will not move, however long it is held, is often not a fault at all. It has reached equilibrium. Knowing that is worth a great deal of steam and reactor time, because the fix is never to wait longer.

What equilibrium is Basics

Many reactions run in both directions at once. At the start the forward reaction is fast because there is plenty of reactant. As product builds up the reverse reaction speeds up, until the two rates match. From then on the composition stops changing, although molecules are still reacting both ways. This is dynamic equilibrium, shown with a double arrow: A + B ⇌ C + D.

Two points that are often confused:

  • Equilibrium is not the same as completion. It can lie far towards products (the reaction looks complete), far towards reactants (hardly anything happens), or anywhere between.
  • A catalyst does not move it. A catalyst speeds up both directions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner, but at the same composition.

The equilibrium constant K Basics

For a reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the ratio below has a fixed value at a given temperature, whatever amounts you start with.

K = [C]c [D]d / ( [A]a [B]b ) Concentrations at equilibrium. For gases partial pressures are used instead, giving Kp. Pure solids and pure liquids such as a solid catalyst or a solvent in large excess are left out.
  • K much larger than 1 (say above 100): products dominate, the reaction goes essentially to completion.
  • K much smaller than 1 (say below 0.01): reactants dominate; little product forms unless you remove it.
  • K between about 0.01 and 100: a real mixture at the end, and the plant has to work for its conversion. The exact conversion also depends on the stoichiometry and the feed ratio.

Worked example: how far will it go? Working level

Take an isomerisation A ⇌ B with K = 3 at the reaction temperature. Start with 1 mol of A and let x mol convert.

K = x / (1 − x) = 3 → x = 0.75 The batch stops at 75 percent conversion whatever the hold time or catalyst load.

For a reaction with two reactants and two products, A + B ⇌ C + D, the answer depends on the feed ratio, so an excess of one reactant pushes conversion of the other higher. The esterification article works through this case with real numbers: about 67 percent at a 1 : 1 feed with K near 4, about 90 percent with a threefold excess.

Le Chatelier’s principle on the plant Basics

If you disturb a system at equilibrium, it shifts to oppose the disturbance. In practice there are four levers.

ChangeWhat the equilibrium doesPlant example
Add more reactantShifts towards productsExcess alcohol in an esterification
Remove a productShifts towards productsDean-Stark water removal, distilling off a low-boiling product, venting a gas
Raise the pressure (gas reactions)Shifts towards the side with fewer gas molesAmmonia synthesis (in liquid-phase hydrogenations, pressure is used for rate and hydrogen solubility rather than equilibrium)
Raise the temperatureShifts in the endothermic directionAn exothermic reaction gives less product at equilibrium when hot

Adding an inert solvent or nitrogen does not change K, but it can change concentrations or partial pressures and so the position of a reaction where the number of moles changes.

Temperature changes K itself Working level

Concentration and pressure move the composition but leave K the same. Temperature changes K. The van ’t Hoff equation gives the size of the change.

ln(K2/K1) = −(ΔH / R) × (1/T2 − 1/T1)
Example: ΔH = −10 kcal/mol, K = 100 at 25 °C. At 85 °C:
ln(K2/100) = (10,000 / 1.987) × (1/358.15 − 1/298.15) = −2.83 → K2 ≈ 5.9 R = 1.987 cal/mol·K, temperatures in kelvin. Assumes ΔH roughly constant over the range.

This is the central trade-off for exothermic reversible reactions. Heating makes the reaction faster but lowers the conversion it can reach. A common answer is to run hot at the start for rate, then cool towards the end to collect the better equilibrium, or to remove product continuously so that equilibrium is never reached.

Equilibrium beyond reactions: solubility and phases Working level

The same idea governs solubility, vapour–liquid behaviour and partition between layers. A saturated solution is an equilibrium between the solid and the dissolved form. For a sparingly soluble salt this is written as a solubility product, Ksp.

CaSO4(s) ⇌ Ca2+ + SO42−    Ksp = [Ca2+][SO42−] ≈ 4.9 × 10−5 at 25 °C
Simple estimate: solubility = √Ksp = 7.0 × 10−3 mol/L ≈ 1.0 g/L The measured solubility of gypsum is close to 2 g/L, because ions in solution pair up and interact. Use measured data for design and the Ksp estimate for direction.

The useful consequence is the common ion effect. Adding sulfate or calcium from another source lowers the solubility further, which is why calcium sulfate scale forms in cooling towers and evaporators as the water concentrates. The same principle is used deliberately when a product is salted out with sodium chloride. For crystallisation, see solubility curves and crystallisation yield.

Equilibrium or just slow? How to tell Going deeper

Both look the same on a trend: conversion that creeps and then stops. They need opposite fixes, so it is worth separating them.

  • Check the end point against a calculation. If it matches the equilibrium conversion for the feed ratio and temperature, the reaction is doing what thermodynamics allows.
  • Try the reverse. In the lab, start from the products. If the mixture moves back to the same composition, that is equilibrium.
  • Spike test. Add a little extra of a product. At equilibrium, conversion falls back. If the reaction was merely slow, it carries on unchanged.
  • Add catalyst or raise the temperature briefly. A slow reaction speeds up and goes further. An equilibrium speeds up but stops at the same place, or lower if the reaction is exothermic.

If it is equilibrium, the levers are excess reagent, product removal and temperature. If it is slow, they are catalyst, temperature, mixing and concentration. The conversion and yield article has a full troubleshooting table.

Questions people ask

Does a catalyst change the equilibrium?

No. A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster but at the same composition. Only temperature changes the equilibrium constant itself.

Why does heating reduce the yield of some reactions?

For an exothermic reversible reaction, the equilibrium constant falls as temperature rises, so less product is present at equilibrium. The reaction is faster when hot but stops earlier.

What is the equilibrium constant used for?

It tells you how far a reaction can go at a given temperature and feed ratio. From K you can calculate the maximum conversion, and how much excess reagent or product removal is needed to reach a target.

How do I know if my batch has stalled at equilibrium?

Compare the final conversion with the calculated equilibrium value, approach it from the product side in the lab, or spike in a little product. If conversion falls back, the batch is at equilibrium.
