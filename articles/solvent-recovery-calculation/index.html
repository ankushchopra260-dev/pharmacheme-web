Solvent Recovery Calculation: From Boiling Point to Batch Time

Chemical Engineering · Distillation · Updated October 2026

Almost every batch in a pharma or agrochemical plant ends the same way: the reaction is done, and now the solvent has to come off. How hot will the batch get? How long will it take? Will the condenser keep up, or will solvent end up in the vacuum pump oil? This article answers those questions in order, from the basic idea to the checks that decide whether a recovery runs smoothly on the plant.

Why we recover under vacuum Basics

A liquid boils when its vapour pressure reaches the pressure above it. At sea level that is about 1,013 mbar, so toluene boils at 110.6 °C. Lower the pressure in the reactor and the liquid needs less heat to reach that point, so it boils cooler.

Cooler boiling helps in three ways. The product is less likely to degrade. Hot water or low-pressure steam is enough to drive the distillation. And the gap between the jacket and the batch is bigger, so heat flows in faster.

Boiling point at any pressure: the Antoine equation Basics

The relation between vapour pressure and temperature is curved: each 10 °C of temperature roughly doubles a solvent's vapour pressure near its boiling point. The Antoine equation describes that curve with three constants per solvent:

log₁₀(P) = A − B / (T + C)   ⇒   Tb = B / (A − log₁₀P) − C P in mmHg and T in °C for the constants used here. Some tables use bar and kelvin, so always check the units of the set you use.

For toluene, A = 6.95087, B = 1342.31, C = 219.187. At 100 mmHg (133 mbar):

Tb = 1342.31 / (6.95087 − 2.000) − 219.187 ≈ 271.1 − 219.2 ≈ 52 °C

A quick sanity check for any Antoine set: put P = 760 mmHg in and you should get the normal boiling point (110.6 °C for toluene). If you don't, the constants or units are wrong.

Heat load and recovery time Working level

Once the batch is boiling, almost all the heat goes into turning liquid into vapour. So the boiling duty is simply:

Heat duty (kcal/h) = vapour rate (kg/h) × latent heat (kcal/kg)
Recovery time (h) = solvent to recover (kg) / vapour rate (kg/h)

Worked example

2,000 kg of toluene to strip at 100 mmHg, at a target 500 kg/h. Toluene's latent heat is about 86 kcal/kg (a little higher at 52 °C than at 110 °C, which is a small extra margin).

Vapour rate = 500 kg/h
Boiling duty = 500 × 86 ≈ 43,000 kcal/h
Time at that rate = 2,000 / 500 = 4 h

Before boiling starts, the batch has to be heated to 52 °C. Heating 2,000 kg of toluene (cp ≈ 0.41 kcal/kg·°C) from 30 °C takes 2,000 × 0.41 × 22 ≈ 18,000 kcal, roughly 25 minutes at 43,000 kcal/h. Add the reactor steel and the product mass if they are significant, plus 5–10 % for losses.

Can the jacket supply it? Working level

The 43,000 kcal/h has to get through the reactor wall. The jacket can only deliver Q = U × A × ΔT, where U is the overall heat transfer coefficient, A the wetted jacket area and ΔT the difference between jacket medium and batch.

Hot water at 80 °C, batch boiling at 52 °C: ΔT = 28 °C
With U ≈ 260 kcal/h·m²·°C (glass-lined, typical) and 6 m² wetted area:
Q = 260 × 6 × 28 ≈ 43,700 kcal/h  →  just enough

At atmospheric pressure the same toluene boils at 110.6 °C; hot water can't do it at all, and even 3 barg steam (144 °C) gives only about 33 °C of driving force. Vacuum is often what makes a recovery possible with the utilities the plant has.

On the plant

The wetted area shrinks as the solvent comes off. A reactor that starts 80 % full may be 30 % full at the end, and the jacket loses most of its useful area just when you want to finish. That is why the last 20 % of a recovery always seems slow. The Heat Load calculator averages the wetted area over the distillation for exactly this reason.

The condenser decides how deep you can go Going deeper

It is tempting to think deeper vacuum is always better. It isn't. Every kilogram that boils off must be condensed, and a condenser can only condense vapour that is warmer than its coolant. A practical rule: keep the vapour at least 8–10 °C above the coolant outlet temperature.

  • Toluene at 133 mbar boils at 52 °C: cooling water at 32–37 °C condenses it comfortably.
  • Methanol at 50 mbar boils at about 3 °C: cooling water cannot condense it. The vapour passes through the condenser and into the vacuum pump, where it dilutes the oil, or goes straight out of the vent.

For the toluene example, the condenser must remove the same 43,000 kcal/h. With cooling water 32 → 37 °C and vapour condensing at 52 °C:

LMTD = (20 − 15) / ln(20/15) ≈ 17.4 °C
Area = 43,000 / (345 × 17.4) ≈ 7.2 m² (U ≈ 345 kcal/h·m²·°C for a shell-and-tube condenser under vacuum)
Cooling water = 43,000 / (1 × 5) ≈ 8,600 kg/h ≈ 8.6 m³/h

Under vacuum, air leaking into the system blankets the condenser tubes and cuts U sharply. A small leak can halve the condenser's capacity, which you see as a rising vapour temperature at the condenser outlet and solvent in the vacuum pump. A secondary vent condenser on chilled water or brine, after the main condenser, is cheap insurance for low-boiling solvents.

Mixed solvents and water Going deeper

The Antoine equation is for one pure liquid. A mixture boils at a temperature between its components, and that temperature rises as the lighter one leaves. For an ideal mixture each component contributes its share of vapour pressure (Raoult's law: partial pressure = mole fraction × pure vapour pressure), so a toluene–methanol mix starts boiling much cooler than pure toluene and finishes near toluene's boiling point.

Many common pairs are not ideal and form azeotropes, mixtures that boil at a fixed composition and cannot be separated by simple distillation: ethanol–water at about 95.6 % ethanol, IPA–water at about 87.7 % IPA. Toluene and water don't mix at all and boil together at about 84 °C at atmospheric pressure, below either pure boiling point, which is useful for drying a toluene batch by azeotropic distillation. For anything beyond a rough estimate, use proper VLE data.

What goes wrong on the plant Going deeper

  • Bumping and foaming when vacuum is pulled too fast on a warm batch: the whole batch flashes at once and carries product into the vapour line. Pull vacuum in steps and keep the agitator running.
  • Solvent in the vacuum pump: condenser too warm, fouled, or blanketed by air leaks. Check the vent temperature.
  • Receiver flashing back: a receiver at the same vacuum but warmer than the condensate boils it again.
  • Slow last stage: wetted area falling and the boiling point rising as the mixture gets heavier; plan the end point, don't just wait.
  • Units: mbar entered into an Antoine set fitted in mmHg. 1 mmHg = 1.333 mbar, and the error is several degrees.

Questions people ask

How do you calculate the boiling point of a solvent under vacuum?

Rearrange the Antoine equation: Tb = B / (A − log₁₀P) − C, with P in mmHg and the solvent's A, B, C constants. Toluene at 100 mmHg boils at about 52 °C.

How do you calculate heat load for solvent recovery?

Heat duty (kcal/h) = vapour rate (kg/h) × latent heat (kcal/kg). 500 kg/h of toluene needs about 43,000 kcal/h, plus sensible heat to warm the batch and losses.

Is mmHg the same as mbar?

No. 1 mmHg ≈ 1.333 mbar. Check which unit your Antoine constants were fitted in.

Why can't I pull deeper vacuum to recover faster?

Because the condenser must be colder than the vapour. Methanol at 50 mbar boils at about 3 °C, so cooling water cannot condense it and it ends up in the vacuum pump.
