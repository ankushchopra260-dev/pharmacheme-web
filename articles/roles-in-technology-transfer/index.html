Roles in Technology Transfer: Who Does What

Scale-up · Technology transfer · November 2026

A technology transfer is a team sport played across eight or more functions. This article sets out what each function owns, where the handover breaks, how to run the meetings and who owns the transfer package, with a RACI table and a critical path example.

Why roles decide the outcome Basics

Technology transfer means moving a process from the team that developed it, the sending unit, to the plant that will run it routinely, the receiving unit. WHO guidance on technology transfer uses exactly these terms. It says the transfer should end with documented evidence that the receiving unit can reproduce the product or process against agreed specifications.

That evidence is not made by one department. R&D knows why the process works. Production knows what the plant can do. QC and QA decide what counts as proof. Engineering knows which equipment can be changed and how long it takes. If any of these voices joins late, the plan changes late, and late changes cost weeks.

The same WHO text asks for responsibilities to be written down before work starts, for both sites and for any sponsor or marketing authorisation holder. Treat that as a rule of thumb even for an internal transfer between two buildings: if it is not written, it is assumed, and assumptions are where transfers go wrong.

What each function owns Basics

  • R&D (process chemistry): the process description, critical parameters and ranges, yield and impurity history, known hazards, and the reasons behind each step. R&D also supports the first batches on site. The failure to avoid is handing over a recipe without the reasons.
  • Analytical development and QC: methods, their validation, reference standards, specifications, and the method transfer to the receiving laboratory. QC then tests the engineering and validation batches. WHO guidance expects analytical procedures to be in place at the receiving laboratory before it tests validation samples.
  • QA: the quality agreement, the protocol and acceptance criteria, deviation and change control, batch record approval and release. QA should join the gap analysis, not just review the final report. See QA and GMP: deviation and CAPA.
  • Production: the people who will actually run the process. They review the process for practicality, write or confirm operating steps, get trained, and execute the batches. Operators often spot a problem, such as a transfer line that cannot drain, long before a document does.
  • Technology transfer or process engineering group: the glue. It leads the gap analysis, runs the plan, owns the transfer package and the open-issues list, and does the scale-up calculations. See Lab to plant scale-up checklist.
  • Engineering and maintenance: equipment fit, modifications, utilities, instrumentation, qualification and spares. Their lead times are often among the longest in the plan.
  • EHS: process safety review, calorimetry and hazard data, effluent and emission impact, permits, and the HAZOP. See HAZOP made practical.
  • Regulatory affairs: decides whether the new site, scale, equipment or supplier needs a filing, a variation or a notification, and manages timelines. See Regulatory affairs: DMF and CEP.
  • Procurement and supply chain: raw material sources, vendor qualification, packaging and lead times, and cost. A new supplier for a key reagent can change the impurity profile.
  • Project management: the schedule, budget and escalation path. In a small plant, the technology transfer lead often plays this role as well.

Worked example: critical path and readiness Working level

Eleven activities, each owned by one or more functions, have durations and dependencies. Added up one after the other they take 62 weeks. Run in parallel where the dependencies allow, the plan finishes in 32 weeks. Now test a slip. If equipment qualification takes 4 weeks longer, the plan ends at week 36. If analytical method transfer takes 4 weeks longer, nothing changes, because it has 11 weeks of float. If vendor approval takes 4 weeks longer, nothing changes either, because it has 6 weeks of float. The message for roles is simple: engineering and QA on equipment must be in the room from day one, while a delay in QC does not move the date until its float runs out.

Float is a planning aid, not permission to relax. Vendor approval has 6 weeks of float, but a 12-week slip would push the end date.

How to read this

A single overall percentage hides the problem. Production and engineering are at 50%, so the gate is a no-go for those two functions even though R&D looks ready. Set a rule such as "no function below 90% on items that affect the batch" and list the open items by name.

Handover friction points and fixes Working level

  • Tacit knowledge stays with R&D. Fix: have chemists write the "why" next to each step, and have a chemist and an operator walk through the process together at the plant before the first batch.
  • Equipment differences found late. Fix: do the side-by-side comparison of sending and receiving equipment in planning, as WHO guidance suggests, and give engineering the first draft of the process description.
  • Method behaves differently in the receiving laboratory. Fix: transfer methods before process batches, use a protocol with acceptance criteria and share known problem areas such as unstable samples.
  • Specifications set by R&D without plant data. Fix: QA and QC review the limits against capability data before they are frozen.
  • Regulatory lead time discovered late. Fix: ask regulatory affairs in week one whether any change needs approval, and put that activity on the plan.
  • Everyone assumes someone else owns a task. Fix: one accountable name per row, reviewed at every meeting.

Meeting cadence and package ownership Working level

A common pattern is a kickoff meeting, a weekly core team meeting of 30 minutes, a monthly steering review with management, and a formal gate review before each major step such as the engineering batch and the validation batches. Adapt the rhythm to the size of the project; a small transfer may need only the weekly meeting and one gate.

  • Kickoff: scope, goals, RACI, schedule, acceptance criteria, escalation route.
  • Weekly core team: open issues list, actions from last week, slipped dates. Keep it to named owners and dates.
  • Gate review: readiness score by function, risks, go or no-go decision recorded in writing.
  • Close-out: transfer report, lessons learned, handover to routine production.

The transfer package should have one owner, usually the technology transfer lead, with R&D responsible for content on the chemistry and QC on the analytical sections. Use revision control: every document carries a version, and changes after approval go through change control. See Technology transfer documents for the contents and Management of change for the change process.

Common mistakes Going deeper

  • Starting with R&D and technology transfer only, and bringing in QA, regulatory and engineering at validation.
  • Having no single accountable owner for the package, so each function keeps its own version.
  • Treating the plant team as recipients of instructions rather than as reviewers of the process.
  • Judging readiness by documents issued instead of items that are actually ready for use.
  • Letting RACI sit in a slide deck that nobody updates after the kickoff.
  • Ignoring float, so the team works hard on items that cannot move the end date and neglects those that can.

What this approach cannot do Going deeper

A RACI table clarifies who acts. It does not make the process robust, and it does not replace technical review. The critical path example uses fixed durations, but real activities vary, and equipment lead times in particular can slip by months. The readiness score treats all items as equal, while one missing safety document can matter more than ten small items. Use these tools to focus attention, then read the open items one by one.

Checklist Going deeper

  • Sending and receiving units named, with written responsibilities.
  • One accountable owner per activity, recorded in the protocol.
  • QA, QC, production, engineering, EHS and regulatory in the gap analysis.
  • Acceptance criteria agreed before the first batch.
  • Critical path identified and reviewed monthly.
  • Transfer package with version control and a single owner.
  • Gate reviews with readiness by function and recorded decisions.
  • Lessons learned captured at close-out.

Questions people ask

Who leads a technology transfer?

One named owner should lead it, usually a technology transfer or process engineering lead at the receiving site, working with a counterpart in R&D. Leading means running the plan, the meetings and the gap list. It does not mean doing every task. Without a single owner, items fall between functions.

What is the difference between the sending unit and the receiving unit?

The sending unit is the site or team that holds the process knowledge, usually R&D or an existing plant. The receiving unit is the plant that will make the product routinely. WHO guidance uses these terms and expects both sides to share duties in writing before the transfer starts.

Why do transfers fail even when the chemistry is sound?

Most failures are handover failures: undocumented tacit knowledge, equipment differences found late, analytical methods that behave differently in the receiving lab, or raw material sources that were never qualified. Chemistry rarely fails alone; the missing detail around it does.

When should QA and regulatory affairs get involved?

At the start, not at validation. QA helps set acceptance criteria and review the gap analysis. Regulatory affairs checks whether the site, batch size, equipment or supplier changes need a filing or notification, because approval lead times are often among the longest in the plan.

What should be in the transfer package?

The process description with critical parameters and ranges, the impurity and yield history, safety data, analytical methods and validation, raw material specifications and approved sources, equipment information and cleaning information. The package needs one owner and a version history so that everyone works from the same revision.
