Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table

Basic chemistry · Atoms, elements and trends · November 2026

Every mass balance on a plant starts with a molar mass, and every molar mass starts with the periodic table. The table also explains a lot that engineers meet without naming it: why sodium metal is stored under oil, why chlorine ends up as HCl in the scrubber, why nickel catalysts work and why chloride pits stainless steel.

What an atom is made of Basics

An atom has a small, heavy nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. Protons carry a positive charge, electrons an equal negative charge, and neutrons none. Almost all the mass sits in the nucleus; almost all the chemistry happens in the electrons.

  • Atomic number (Z) is the number of protons. It fixes the element: every atom with 17 protons is chlorine.
  • Mass number (A) is protons plus neutrons. Chlorine-35 has 17 protons and 18 neutrons.
  • A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. An ion has gained or lost electrons: Na+ has lost one, Cl− has gained one.

Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are isotopes. They react almost identically, which is why chemistry ignores them most of the time. Pharma is one place they matter: deuterium (hydrogen with one neutron) is used in NMR solvents such as CDCl3 and in a few deuterated drugs, where the slightly stronger C–D bond slows down metabolism.

Why atomic masses are not whole numbers Basics

The atomic mass printed on the table is the average over the natural mix of isotopes. Chlorine is about 75.8 percent chlorine-35 and 24.2 percent chlorine-37, so:

Average atomic mass of Cl = 0.7576 × 34.969 + 0.2424 × 36.966 = 35.45 Isotope masses and abundances from the IUPAC (CIAAW) tables. This is the 35.45 g/mol you use in every calculation.

The same number is grams per mole, or kilograms per kilomole. On a plant the kilomole is the natural unit: 1 kmol of chlorine atoms weighs 35.45 kg. The mole concept article takes this further.

Electrons, shells and valence Basics

Electrons fill shells around the nucleus. Only the outermost shell, the valence shell, takes part in bonding, and the number of valence electrons decides how an element behaves.

  • Atoms tend to gain, lose or share electrons until they have a full outer shell, usually eight electrons (the octet rule). Hydrogen is satisfied with two.
  • Sodium has one valence electron and gives it up easily, so it forms Na+ and reacts violently with water.
  • Chlorine has seven and takes one more, so it forms Cl− or shares an electron in a covalent bond.
  • Carbon has four and shares all of them, which is why it forms four bonds and long chains.
  • Helium, neon and argon already have full shells and almost never react.
On the plant

Nitrogen is not a noble gas, it is just very slow to react because of its strong triple bond. Lithium metal does react with nitrogen at room temperature, and some hot metals and organometallics do too. For those, the inert gas is argon.

Reading the periodic table Basics

The table is arranged by atomic number. Rows (periods) show how many electron shells an atom has. Columns (groups) collect elements with the same number of valence electrons, which is why elements in a group behave alike.

Trends across the table, and what they mean in practice Working level

Three properties change in a regular way across the table. You do not need the numbers by heart, only the direction.

  • Electronegativity, the pull an atom has on shared electrons, rises from left to right and from bottom to top. Fluorine is the highest. It decides whether a bond is polar, and so whether a molecule mixes with water.
  • Atomic size grows down a group and shrinks across a period. Big atoms such as iodine and bromine hold their outer electrons loosely, so C–I and C–Br bonds break more easily than C–Cl. That is why alkyl iodides and bromides are better alkylating agents, and also more toxic.
  • Metallic character, the ease of losing electrons, rises down and to the left. Potassium is more reactive than sodium, which is more reactive than lithium.

The difference between two atoms tells you how polar the bond between them is. O–H (difference 1.24) is very polar, C–H (0.35) is close to non-polar, and Na–Cl (2.23) is ionic. The chemical bonding article builds on this.

Worked example: chlorine from a solvent ends up in the scrubber Working level

A site sends 100 kg of dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) waste to an incinerator. How much HCl reaches the scrubber, and how much caustic does it take?

Molar mass CH2Cl2 = 12.011 + 2(1.008) + 2(35.45) = 84.93 kg/kmol
Chlorine content = 70.90 / 84.93 = 83.5 percent by mass
CH2Cl2 + O2 → CO2 + 2 HCl
100 / 84.93 = 1.177 kmol solvent → 2.355 kmol HCl × 36.46 = 85.9 kg HCl
NaOH needed = 2.355 kmol × 40.00 = 94.2 kg (as 100 percent NaOH) Ideal complete combustion, no other chlorine sources. Real scrubbers are sized with a margin on top.

The point is that the chlorine atoms do not disappear. Whatever happens to the solvent, each chlorine atom ends up somewhere: as HCl, as chloride in the effluent, or as a chlorinated by-product. An element balance is the quickest check on any process that handles halogens or sulfur.

The periodic table and corrosion Going deeper

Corrosion is metal atoms losing electrons. Metals that give up electrons easily (further left and lower in the table, and further down the galvanic series) corrode first, unless they form a tight protective oxide, as aluminium, titanium and chromium do. Zinc protects steel in galvanising because zinc loses electrons more readily than iron, so the zinc corrodes instead.

Stainless steel resists corrosion because its chromium forms a very thin, tight oxide film. Chloride ions attack that film at weak spots and stop it re-forming, which is why chloride causes pitting and stress corrosion cracking in stainless steel, and why the chloride content of cooling water and process streams matters. Questions people ask

Why is the atomic mass of chlorine 35.45 and not a whole number?

Because natural chlorine is a mix of two isotopes, chlorine-35 (about 76 percent) and chlorine-37 (about 24 percent). The 35.45 is the weighted average, and it is the value to use in all calculations.

What decides how an element reacts?

Mainly the number of electrons in its outer shell. Elements in the same column of the periodic table have the same number of valence electrons, so they react in similar ways, for example sodium and potassium both react with water.

What is electronegativity used for in practice?

It tells you how polar a bond is. A large difference between two atoms gives a polar or ionic bond, which affects solubility in water, boiling point and which part of a molecule reacts.

Is nitrogen an inert gas?

Not strictly. It reacts very slowly because of its strong triple bond, so it is used for inerting. Lithium metal and some hot metals react with nitrogen, and for those argon is used instead.
