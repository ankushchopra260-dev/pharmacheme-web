Centrifuge Cake Weight & G-Force: How a Basket Centrifuge Works in Numbers

Chemical Engineering · Solid–Liquid Separation · Updated October 2026

A basket centrifuge is a filter that uses spin instead of vacuum. Planning a batch on one comes down to a few numbers: how hard it spins the cake (G-force), how much cake fits in one load, and how much liquid it can squeeze out. This article builds them up from the basics, then covers the limits that matter on the plant: the lip, the load rating and the residual moisture.

How a basket centrifuge separates Basics

The basket is a perforated drum lined with a filter cloth. Slurry is fed in while it spins. The spin flings everything outwards: solids pack against the cloth as a cake, and the liquid is pushed through the cake and the cloth and out through the holes. Once the feed stops, the basket keeps spinning to spin the cake dry, then the cake is washed (if needed), spun again and discharged, by a scraper (peeler) or by hand through the bottom.

It is the same physics as a Nutsche filter, with one big difference: instead of 0.5–1 bar of vacuum or gas pressure pushing the liquid, the spinning liquid itself presses on the cake, and at normal speeds that pressure is several times higher. That is why centrifuge cakes usually come out drier.

G-force Basics

G-force tells you how many times stronger than gravity the push is. It comes from the centripetal acceleration ω²r divided by g:

G = ω² r / g = (2πN/60)² r / 9.81
G = 1.118 × 10−5 × r (cm) × N²   =   1.12 × r (mm) × (N/1000)²

Two things to remember. G grows with the square of speed, so a basket at 1,400 rpm works the cake about twice as hard as at 1,000 rpm. And G is proportional to radius, so it is not the same everywhere: it is highest at the basket wall and lower at the inner surface of the cake.

Cake volume and weight per load Working level

The cake forms a hollow cylinder (an annulus) against the basket wall. Its volume is the basket's volume minus the empty core:

Cake volume = H × (π/4) × [D² − (D − 2t)²]
Cake weight = cake volume × bulk density of the wet cake D basket diameter, H basket height, t cake thickness. Use the bulk density of the actual discharged wet cake of your product, not a handbook value.

The cake cannot be thicker than the lip allows. The lip is the ring at the top of the basket; its opening diameter sets the maximum cake thickness, (D − Dlip) / 2. Feed beyond that and slurry spills over the lip into the filtrate side.

Worked example Working level

A 1,200 mm (48”) basket, 600 mm high, lip opening 900 mm, running at 1,000 rpm. Planned cake 120 mm thick, wet cake bulk density 700 kg/m³.

G at the wall = 1.12 × 600 × (1,000/1,000)² ≈ 670 G
G at the cake surface (r = 480 mm) ≈ 540 G
Lip limit = (1,200 − 900) / 2 = 150 mm  → 120 mm is fine
Cake volume = 0.6 × π/4 × (1.2² − 0.96²) ≈ 0.244 m³
Cake weight = 0.244 × 700 ≈ 171 kg per load

If the batch produces 900 kg of wet cake, that is about five and a half loads, so plan six. Each load has feed, spin-dry, wash, spin-dry and discharge steps, so the number of loads, not the spin itself, usually decides the batch time.

The pressure that drives liquid out Going deeper

During the feed, a layer of liquid sits on top of the cake. Spinning, that liquid presses outwards like a column of water pressing on the bottom of a tank, except the “gravity” is hundreds of G. The pressure across the cake and cloth is:

ΔP = ρliquid × ω² × (rwall² − rliquid surface²) / 2

In the example, with a 30 mm liquid layer on the cake (liquid surface at 450 mm radius) and liquid of 900 kg/m³, ΔP = 900 × 104.7² × (0.6² − 0.45²) / 2 ≈ 7.8 bar. A vacuum Nutsche can never exceed about 0.9 bar. That is the real reason a centrifuge filters and dewaters faster: the same cake resistance equation applies, with a much bigger pressure.

Once the liquid layer has drained, the remaining liquid is held in the pores by capillary forces. Spinning longer or faster removes more of it, but with diminishing returns: the residual moisture falls steeply in the first minutes and then levels off. If the cake is still too wet after a reasonable spin, the answer is usually in the crystals (fines blocking pores) or the wash (a viscous wash solvent), not more spin time. Load, speed and safety limits Going deeper

  • Maximum load and speed are linked. The basket's stress depends on load × speed². The nameplate gives a maximum load at maximum speed; a denser product or a heavier load means a lower allowed speed. Never exceed both.
  • Imbalance. Uneven feeding, or a cake that slumps on one side, makes the basket wobble. Feed at low speed and evenly, and respect the vibration trip.
  • Flammable solvents. A spinning basket full of solvent-wet cake, with fast-moving liquid and plastic cloth, is a static and flammable-vapour hazard. Centrifuges handling solvents should be nitrogen-inerted with oxygen monitoring and properly earthed. The nitrogen purging calculator helps plan the inerting.
  • Peeler discharge leaves a heel of cake on the cloth that blinds over many loads; plan heel washes.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Quoting one G-force for the whole cake; the inner surface sees less.
  • Using a handbook bulk density instead of the real wet cake, which can be off by 30 %.
  • Planning cake thickness without checking the lip limit.
  • Running a heavier, denser cake at the nameplate maximum speed.

Questions people ask

How do you calculate G-force in a centrifuge?

G = 1.118 × 10−5 × r (cm) × N², or 1.12 × r (mm) × (N/1000)². A 600 mm radius at 1,000 rpm gives about 670 G.

How do you calculate cake weight in a basket centrifuge?

Cake volume = H × π/4 × [D² − (D − 2t)²], times the wet cake bulk density. The example basket holds about 171 kg per load.

Why does a centrifuge dewater better than a Nutsche filter?

The spinning liquid presses on the cake with several bar of pressure (about 7.8 bar in the example), against at most about 1 bar on a vacuum filter.
