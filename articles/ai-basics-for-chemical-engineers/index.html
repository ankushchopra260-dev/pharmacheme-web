AI Basics for Chemical Engineers: Start Here

Plant Engineering · Digitalization · October 2026

Never used AI at work? This page is for you. It explains the different kinds of AI in plain words, gives you five things to try this week, builds your first prompts for you, and shows which tools engineers in chemical and pharma plants actually use. What “AI” means in a plant

“AI” is used for very different tools. Knowing which one you are talking about decides the data you need, how you validate it and who can build it.

TypeWhat it doesPlant example
Machine learning (ML)Learns a relationship from historical numbers: inputs to an outputPredict the impurity at end of batch from temperature, dosing rate and stirrer power
Anomaly detectionLearns what “normal” looks like and flags departuresA pump’s vibration and bearing temperature drift weeks before failure
Computer visionRecognises patterns in images or videoLabel and packaging defects; PPE checks at a charging area
Optimisation (incl. Bayesian optimisation)Searches for the best settings with the fewest trialsChoosing the next lab experiment for a new route
Generative AI / large language models (LLMs)Reads and writes text, code and summariesSearch SOPs and past deviations in plain language; draft a first version of a report
Digital twinA model of the process (first-principles, data-driven or both) kept in step with live dataTry a new temperature profile on the model before the reactor

Most of the value reported by plants so far comes from the first three, built on historian and lab data. Generative AI is newer, faster to try, and under the most regulatory caution in GMP areas.

Your first week with AI

You do not need to code or buy anything to start. A general AI assistant works like a very well-read colleague who answers instantly, writes fast and is sometimes confidently wrong. Use it for words and ideas; check every number yourself.

Five things to try this week

  1. Explain something. “Explain NPSH to a new production officer in five lines, with one plant example.”
  2. Summarise a manual. Paste a non-confidential section of a pump or dryer manual and ask for the start-up checks as a list.
  3. Draft a document. “Draft a one-page toolbox talk on static electricity when charging solvents.” Then correct it.
  4. Spreadsheet help. “Write an Excel formula that flags batches where yield is below 92% and cycle time above 30 h.” Test it on a few rows you know.
  5. Prepare questions. “Give me 15 what-if questions for a HAZOP of a solvent recovery column.” Use them as prompts for your team, not as the HAZOP.
Three rules from day one

Use only the AI tools your company has approved, and never paste confidential process data, recipes or customer names into public tools. Treat every number, regulation or reference as unverified until you have checked it. You remain responsible for anything you sign or send.

Prompt builder

Good answers come from good prompts: say who you are, what you need, the context and the format. Pick options and copy the prompt into your company-approved AI assistant.

Generative AI at work: do and don’t

Chat assistants are the AI most engineers touch first. NIST’s generative AI risk profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024) names “confabulation” as a core risk: the production of confidently stated but false content. In a plant, a confident wrong number is worse than no number.

Good uses (with review)

  • Searching your own SOPs, batch records and past deviations in plain language, with links back to the source document
  • A first draft of a report, training material or email that an expert then corrects
  • Summarising long documents: vendor manuals, regulations, literature
  • Writing and explaining spreadsheet formulas or small analysis scripts, then testing them on known data
  • Brainstorming what-if questions for a HAZOP pre-read

Keep it out of

  • Batch release, OOS conclusions and any decision a qualified person signs
  • Relief, safety-interlock and equipment sizing numbers without independent calculation
  • Reaction hazard conclusions: use calorimetry data (see reading DSC, ARC and RC1)
  • Confidential process data pasted into public tools: use company-approved, access-controlled systems
  • Citations you have not opened yourself

Questions people ask

How can a chemical engineer start using AI?

Start with a general AI assistant approved by your company for everyday text work: explaining a concept, summarising a manual, drafting a report or writing a spreadsheet formula. Give it context, ask it to show its working and list assumptions, and check every number against a calculation or source.

Which AI tools are used in the chemical industry?

General assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot) for text work; process-data analytics on top of historians (for example Seeq and TrendMiner); predictive maintenance software (for example Aspen Mtell); retrosynthesis tools for R&D (CAS SciFinder, Reaxys, AiZynthFinder); and Python, KNIME, JMP or Power BI for data analysis.

Is it safe to paste plant data into ChatGPT?

Only into tools your company has approved for that data. Do not paste confidential process data, recipes, batch records or customer names into public AI tools, and check every number the tool gives you.

Is AI the same as machine learning?

Machine learning is one part of AI: models that learn a relationship from data. AI also includes computer vision, optimisation and generative AI such as chat assistants that read and write text.
