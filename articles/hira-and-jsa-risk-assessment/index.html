Risk Assessment: HIRA, JSA and the Risk Matrix

Plant Departments · Risk Assessment · October 2026

HAZOP looks at the process design. Most injuries, though, happen during ordinary activities: charging powders, sampling, changing a filter cloth, working at height. HIRA and JSA are the tools for those. Done honestly, they are short, practical and make work safer; done as paperwork, they are neither.

HIRA: hazard identification and risk assessment Basics

A HIRA covers each area or activity: list the routine and non-routine tasks, identify the hazards in each (chemical exposure, fire, pressure, moving machinery, manual handling, height, electricity, heat), estimate the risk with existing controls, and decide whether more control is needed. It is reviewed periodically and after any change or incident.

The risk matrix Basics

Risk combines severity (from first aid to fatality or major release) and likelihood (from very unlikely to frequent), usually each on a 1–5 scale. Risk score = severity × likelihood, and bands such as low, medium, high and unacceptable decide the action. Example: a solvent drum transfer without earthing, severity 4 (fire with possible serious injury), likelihood 3: score 12, high. With bonding, earthing and a dip pipe, likelihood drops to 1: score 4, low. The method is simple; the value is in the honest discussion behind the numbers.

JSA: breaking a job into steps Working level

A job safety analysis takes one specific job, for example opening a filter dryer to discharge wet cake, and breaks it into steps. For each step: the hazard, the control, and who is responsible. It is best done with the people who do the job, at the place where it is done. The JSA then becomes the basis of the SOP, the training and the permit.

Hierarchy of controls Working level

  1. Elimination: remove the hazard (a closed charging system instead of manual tipping).
  2. Substitution: a less hazardous material or form (a solution or paste instead of a dusty powder).
  3. Engineering controls: local exhaust, containment, interlocks, guards, earthing.
  4. Administrative controls: procedures, permits, training, signs, job rotation.
  5. Personal protective equipment: the last line, not the first.

A risk assessment whose only actions are “use PPE” and “be careful” has not been finished.

Where HIRA and JSA fit Going deeper

HIRA and JSA cover activities and occupational hazards. Process hazards, deviations of flow, temperature and pressure, are covered by HAZOP, with LOPA where the consequence is severe, and by thermal safety studies for reactions. Chemical exposure assessment compares expected exposure with occupational exposure limits and decides containment, especially for potent APIs. Together they form the site’s risk register, reviewed when anything changes through management of change.

On the plant

Do the JSA for a non-routine job at the job location, with the people who will do it, on the day. A desk JSA written last year rarely knows that the platform rail was removed for the new line.

Mistakes I see most often

  • HIRA copied from another area and never reviewed.
  • Scores adjusted to avoid actions.
  • JSA written without the people who do the job.
  • Relying on PPE instead of engineering controls.
  • Not reviewing the assessment after an incident or change.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between HIRA and JSA?

HIRA assesses the hazards of all activities in an area. A JSA breaks one specific job into steps and lists the hazard and control for each.

How is a risk score calculated in a risk matrix?

Severity times likelihood, each usually on a 1 to 5 scale, with bands deciding what action is needed.

What is the hierarchy of controls?

Elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls and PPE, in order of effectiveness.
