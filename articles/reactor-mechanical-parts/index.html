Reactor Mechanical Parts

Equipment · Reactors · November 2026

Most freshers learn a reactor as a box with a temperature and a volume. The plant teaches it differently: as thirty-odd mechanical parts, any one of which can hold up a batch. The seal weeps, the bottom valve passes, a gasket blows on the manhole, the gearbox breather is choked. This is a part-by-part tour of a jacketed batch reactor, what each part is for, and what goes wrong with it.

The pressure envelope Basics

The shell, the two dished heads and the jacket together hold the pressure. The heads are usually torispherical, sometimes ellipsoidal where the design pressure is higher; a hemispherical head is the strongest and the most expensive. The wall thickness comes from the design pressure, the diameter, the material’s allowable stress, the joint efficiency and a corrosion allowance, typically 1.5 to 3 mm on carbon steel.

Two numbers matter more than the rest, and they are different numbers for the shell and for the jacket:

  • Design pressure. The jacket is usually rated for the steam supply; the shell for the process. They are not interchangeable, and a jacket is not a place to put process material.
  • Full vacuum. Any vessel that will ever see a vacuum distillation must be rated for full vacuum, not just internal pressure. Collapse under vacuum is a buckling failure and happens at a far lower differential than a burst.

Both numbers are on the nameplate, with the hydrotest pressure, the design temperature, the maker, the serial number and the year. If you cannot read the nameplate, treat that as a finding, not a nuisance.

For how the metal or the lining is chosen against the chemistry, see materials of construction and glass-lined reactor care. For the jacket itself, including why a limpet coil gives a better coefficient than a plain jacket, see jacket and limpet coil design.

Nozzles and what goes in them Basics

Almost everything that goes wrong on a reactor goes wrong at a nozzle, because every nozzle is a joint, and most are also a discontinuity in the lining.

NozzleWhat it carriesWhat goes wrong
ManholeAccess, solid chargingGasket blows; cover cracked by over-torque; davit arm seized
ChargingLiquids, reagents, dip pipeDip pipe corroded or snapped; dosing above the surface when it should be below
VentVapour to condenser, vacuumChoked with solids; the biggest single cause of a lost vacuum
ReliefSafety valve, rupture discIsolated, fouled, or a burst disc nobody noticed had burst
ThermowellTemperature sensorSensor not touching the bottom of the well, so the reading lags badly
Sight and light glassLooking inFogged, or cracked by thermal shock when cold liquid hits hot glass

A thermowell lets the sensor be changed without opening the vessel, at the cost of a slower response. If a batch temperature looks sluggish against the jacket, check that the element is seated at the bottom of the well before you suspect the control loop. More on this in instrumentation basics.

On the relief nozzle: a rupture disc below a safety valve keeps the process off the valve seat, which suits fouling or corrosive service. It also means a burst disc can sit unnoticed unless the gap between disc and valve is monitored. Sizing is its own subject — see relief valve sizing.

The agitator assembly Working level

From the top down: motor, gearbox, coupling, shaft, impellers, and on longer shafts a steady bearing at the base.

The gearbox is the part most often neglected. It has an oil level, a breather and a drain, and the breather chokes with dust until the casing cannot breathe and the seals start to weep oil. Oil on the top platform under a gearbox is a finding, not housekeeping.

The shaft has to be stiff enough that its running deflection stays small and its critical speed sits clear of the operating speed. This is why the same motor power can come on a much heavier shaft in a taller vessel. A shaft that has been bent — by a seized bottom bush, or by being used as a ladder rung during a shutdown — will chew out a seal in weeks.

The steady bearing deserves a decision rather than a habit. It supports the shaft bottom and allows a longer, slimmer shaft, but it runs submerged in the batch, wears, and puts its wear debris into the product. In pharmaceutical service that is a contamination route, so many reactors now use a shorter, stiffer cantilever shaft with no bottom bearing at all.

In a glass-lined reactor the baffle cannot be welded to the wall, so it is a single beaver-tail baffle hung through its own nozzle. One baffle does less than the four wall baffles of a stainless vessel, which is part of why glass-lined mixing numbers do not transfer directly from steel. Impeller choice, power number and baffling are covered in agitator design basics.

The shaft seal Working level

This is the part that fails most, and the one that puts solvent vapour into the building when it does.

Gland packing in a stuffing box is the old arrangement: rings of packing compressed around the shaft by a gland follower, often with a lantern ring part-way down for a flush. It is cheap and forgiving, it always leaks a little by design, and that is why it has been pushed out of solvent and toxic service.

A mechanical seal runs two flat faces against each other, one turning with the shaft and one static, with a thin film between them. A single seal has process on one side and atmosphere on the other. A double seal has two sets of faces with a barrier fluid between them, held above process pressure so that any leak is barrier fluid inward, not process outward. Any reactor handling a flammable or toxic solvent should be on a double seal with a monitored barrier system.

Seals fail for a short list of reasons:

  • Dry running. Barrier fluid lost or its pressure fallen below process pressure. The faces overheat in minutes.
  • Solids at the faces. Crystallising batches and slurries grind the faces. A flush is there to keep them clear.
  • Run-out and misalignment. A bent shaft or a badly aligned coupling loads one part of the face.
  • Thermal shock. A sudden jacket swing moves the faces apart.

The useful habit is to treat the barrier pot as an instrument. Its level and pressure trend downward before a seal fails, and that trend is the warning you get. A seal that is weeping already had a story earlier in the week.

Gaskets, clamps and bolt torque Working level

On a glass-lined reactor the joints are made with PTFE envelope gaskets held by clamps or bolts onto glass-lined faces. Glass takes compression well and bending badly. Two rules follow, and both are broken regularly:

  1. Tighten in a star pattern, in stages, never one bolt at a time around the circle. Uneven pull bends the flange.
  2. Tighten to the vendor’s torque figure with a calibrated wrench, and stop there. “A bit more” is how nozzle flanges crack.

A gasket is a consumable. Re-using one because the joint “looked fine” is a half-hour saved against a shift lost. Keep the right size and material in stores before the shutdown, not during it.

The bottom outlet valve Working level

The bottom outlet is where a reactor is emptied, and in a batch plant it is also where cross-contamination between products is most likely. A flush-bottom valve puts the seat level with the inside of the dished head, so there is no pocket above it holding a heel of the last batch. An ordinary valve mounted below a short stub leaves exactly such a pocket.

What to watch: a valve that passes while you are still reacting sends batch into the line below, and the first sign is often a warm discharge line. Seat damage from solids, and a stem seal that leaks when the valve is cold, are the other two common findings. Any work on it is isolation work — see permit to work and isolation.

Supports, earthing and the nameplate Going deeper

Support lugs carry the full weight of vessel, batch and agitator into the structure, and they have to let the shell grow as it heats. Seized or badly shimmed supports load the shell in a way the designer never intended; cracked grout and sheared anchor bolts under a lug are worth a close look during any shutdown.

Earthing is a process safety item, not an electrical formality. A glass-lined vessel is an insulator, so charge generated by pouring or by a solvent transfer has to be taken away through earthed metal parts, and the earthing boss and strap are part of that path. Continuity is testable, and should be tested. See static electricity and solvent fires.

The nameplate ties the vessel to its documentation: the design and test pressures, the design temperature, the material, the serial number and the year of manufacture. It is what a periodic statutory inspection works from. Rules differ by country and by state, so follow the inspection interval and the competent-person requirement that applies to your site rather than a number from a book. The same documentation trail is what a qualification exercise checks: equipment qualification.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Treating the barrier pot as a tank to be topped up rather than an instrument that is warning you.
  • Tightening manhole or nozzle bolts round the circle instead of in a star pattern, and past the vendor’s torque.
  • Pulling vacuum on a vessel rated only for internal pressure.
  • Assuming the jacket design pressure is the same as the shell’s.
  • Blaming the control loop for a lagging temperature before checking that the sensor is seated in the thermowell.
  • Keeping a steady bearing in a pharmaceutical reactor without asking where its wear debris goes.
  • Re-using a gasket to save a shutdown half-hour.

Questions people ask

What are the main mechanical parts of a chemical reactor?

The pressure envelope (shell, dished heads, jacket or limpet coil), the nozzles on the top cover, the agitator assembly (motor, gearbox, coupling, shaft, impellers, steady bearing), the shaft seal, the baffle, the support lugs and the bottom outlet valve.

Why do reactor mechanical seals fail?

Usually dry running after barrier fluid is lost or its pressure drops below process pressure, solids reaching the faces, or a bent shaft and misalignment wearing the faces unevenly.

What is a steady bearing in a reactor?

A bush at the base that supports the bottom of a long agitator shaft. It runs in the batch and wears, so its debris is a contamination route; cantilever shafts avoid it entirely.

Why does the bolt tightening sequence matter on a glass-lined reactor?

Glass takes compression but not bending. Tightening round the circle, or past the specified torque, bends the flange and cracks the lining.
