Mixing Scale-up: From the Lab Flask to the Plant Reactor

Chemical Engineering · Mixing · Updated October 2026

A reaction that worked perfectly in a 2-litre flask at 300 rpm is moving to a 20 KL reactor. What speed should the plant agitator run at? There is no single right answer, because you cannot keep everything the same when you make a vessel a thousand times bigger. You have to choose what matters most for your process, and accept that other things will change. This article explains the choice.

What an agitator actually does Basics

An impeller does two jobs at once. It pumps: it moves the whole batch around the vessel so every part passes through the impeller zone regularly. And it shears: right at the blade tips the liquid is torn apart into small eddies, which breaks up droplets, disperses gas and, unfortunately, can break crystals.

Different impellers split their effort differently. A Rushton (flat-blade) turbine throws liquid outwards and makes a lot of shear: good for gas dispersion. A pitched-blade turbine or hydrofoil pushes liquid down and around: good for blending and keeping solids suspended with less shear. An anchor scrapes the wall at low speed: good for viscous batches and heat transfer near the wall.

The four numbers that describe mixing Basics

Reynolds number Re = ρ N D² / μ    turbulent above about 10,000; laminar below about 10
Tip speed = π D N    a measure of the shear at the blade tips
Power P = Np ρ N³ D⁵    Np is the impeller's power number
Power per volume P/V    how hard the whole batch is being worked

N is speed in revolutions per second and D the impeller diameter. Two things in the power equation are worth noticing: power rises with the cube of speed and the fifth power of diameter. Double the impeller diameter at the same speed and you need 32 times the power. That is why plant agitators run so much slower than lab stirrers.

The power number Np depends on the impeller type and is roughly constant in turbulent flow: about 5 for a six-blade Rushton turbine, about 1.3–1.5 for a 45° pitched-blade turbine, and 0.3 or less for a hydrofoil.

The scale-up rules Working level

If the plant vessel is a scaled copy of the lab one (same ratio of impeller to tank diameter, same impeller type, same baffles, same fill height ratio), every common rule reduces to one formula:

N2 = N1 × (D1 / D2)n
Keep this the samenWhat it protects
Tip speed1Shear: crystals, flocs, fragile solids
Power per volume (P/V)2/3Blending, heat transfer, gas or liquid dispersion
Reynolds number2The flow regime; rarely practical

Worked example: 0.1 m to 1.0 m impeller Working level

A lab vessel (0.3 m diameter) with a 0.1 m Rushton turbine at 300 rpm, water-like liquid. The plant vessel is ten times bigger in every dimension: a 3 m tank with a 1.0 m impeller, about a thousand times the volume.

Constant P/V: N2 = 300 × (0.1/1.0)2/3 ≈ 65 rpm
Constant tip speed: N2 = 300 × (0.1/1.0) = 30 rpm
Constant Re: N2 = 300 × (0.1/1.0)² = 3 rpm

The same lab result gives 65, 30 or 3 rpm. At 65 rpm the plant agitator draws about 6 kW (a thousand times the lab's 6 W). At 30 rpm it draws only 0.6 kW, but the batch is worked ten times less hard per cubic metre. At 3 rpm, nothing is really mixing.

Which rule for which process Working level

  • Crystallisation and fragile solids: constant tip speed. Crystal breakage happens at the blade tips. Keep the tip speed the same as the lab (or below about 3–5 m/s) and accept slower blending.
  • Blending, heat transfer, reactions with dosing, liquid–liquid or gas–liquid dispersion: constant P/V. These depend on how much energy goes into the whole batch.
  • Keeping solids suspended: the just-suspended speed (Zwietering) scales as roughly D−0.85, between the two rules. Constant P/V usually keeps solids off the bottom; constant tip speed may not. Check it.
  • Viscous batches (laminar flow): scale-up is about keeping the whole batch moving, usually with close-clearance impellers (anchors, helical ribbons); P/V rules don't apply.
On the plant

Most plant reactors are not scaled copies of the lab flask: a round-bottom flask with a magnetic stirrer is nothing like a baffled reactor with an anchor. When the geometry is different, use the rules only as a starting point, and confirm with a pilot run or at least a check of blend time and suspension at the chosen plant speed.

What changes no matter which rule you pick Going deeper

Blend time gets longer. In turbulent flow, the time to blend to 95 % uniformity is inversely proportional to speed for the same geometry. In the example, the lab blends in about 5 s; the plant takes about 25 s at constant P/V and about 55 s at constant tip speed. If your chemistry is fast compared with that (a quick neutralisation, a fast addition of a reactive reagent), the plant will see local hot spots or over-concentrated zones the lab never did. Slow the addition, or add near the impeller where mixing is most intense.

Heat transfer area per volume falls. The vessel's surface area grows with diameter squared, its volume with diameter cubed. Ten times the diameter means one tenth the jacket area per cubic metre of batch. A reaction that was easy to cool in the lab can run hot in the plant even at the same P/V. This is the single most important reason why exothermic reactions need a proper heat balance before scale-up, not just a mixing calculation. See the heat load article and the cooling failure and runaway guide.

Shear distribution widens. At constant P/V the plant has higher tip speed (here 3.4 m/s against 1.6 m/s) but slower average circulation. Crystals see brief, more violent passes through the impeller zone.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Using one rule by habit without asking what the process actually needs.
  • Comparing speeds between different impeller types as if their power numbers were the same.
  • Scaling mixing correctly and forgetting that heat removal per volume fell tenfold.
  • Ignoring the fill level: a reactor run at 30 % fill has the impeller near the surface and very different flow.

Questions people ask

What are the main mixing scale-up rules?

For geometrically similar vessels, N2 = N1 × (D1/D2)n, with n = 1 for constant tip speed, n = 2/3 for constant P/V (turbulent) and n = 2 for constant Reynolds number.

Which scale-up rule should I use?

Constant tip speed when shear damages the product, constant P/V when blending, heat transfer or mass transfer control the result. If you are unsure, calculate both and check what each does to the property you care about.

Why does blend time increase on scale-up?

Blend time in turbulent flow is inversely proportional to speed. Plant agitators turn much slower, so blending takes longer: about five times longer at constant P/V for a tenfold increase in impeller diameter.
