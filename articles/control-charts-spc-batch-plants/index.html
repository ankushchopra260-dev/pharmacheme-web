Control Charts for Batch Plants: SPC for Yield and Assay

Process monitoring · Statistical process control · November 2026

A batch result is never exactly the same twice. A control chart helps you tell normal batch-to-batch scatter from a real change, such as a new raw material lot or a worn probe. This article covers individuals and X-bar/R charts, run rules and Cp/Cpk, with made-up yield and assay data.

What a control chart does Basics

Every batch process varies. Yield moves by a percent or two, assay moves in the second decimal, cycle time moves by minutes. Most of this movement is common-cause variation: many small effects that are always present. A control chart plots results in time order and draws a centre line and two limits calculated from the data itself.

As long as points stay inside the limits and show no pattern, the process is behaving as it usually does. Leave it alone. A point outside the limits, or a clear pattern, suggests a special cause: something changed. Then you look for it.

This matters because the two wrong reactions are both expensive. If you react to every up and down, you adjust a stable process and make it worse. If you ignore a real shift, you carry on making off-trend batches until one fails.

Control limits are not specification limits

Control limits describe what the process does. Specification limits describe what is allowed. They are calculated differently, they answer different questions, and they should never be drawn as if one were the other.

Which chart for which data Basics

The choice depends on how many results you have per time point.

  • Individuals and moving range (I-MR): one result per batch, such as batch yield, final assay or cycle time. This is the most common chart in batch plants because batches are made one at a time.
  • X-bar and R: a small group of results taken together, for example five assay results from different locations of a blend, or five samples from one drum. The chart tracks the group average and the group range.
  • Attribute charts: for counts or fractions, such as rejected containers per lot. These are outside this article.

Put the points in the order they were made. A chart of sorted or averaged data hides exactly the time pattern you are looking for.

How the limits are calculated Working level

For an individuals chart, the spread is estimated from the average moving range, which is the absolute difference between each batch and the one before it. This keeps slow drift from inflating the limits.

Individuals chart

MRi = |xi − xi−1|, centre line = mean of x

UCL, LCL = mean ± 2.66 × MR̄  (2.66 = 3 / 1.128)

Moving range chart: UCL = 3.267 × MR̄, no lower limit

Estimated sigma = MR̄ / 1.128

For X-bar and R with subgroups of five, the limits use the average range R̄ and constants from standard tables.

X-bar and R chart, subgroup size n = 5

X-bar chart: grand mean ± 0.577 × R̄

R chart: UCL = 2.114 × R̄, LCL = 0 (for n = 5)

Within-subgroup sigma = R̄ / 2.326

Calculate the limits from a period you know was stable, usually 20 to 25 points for an individuals chart or 20 to 25 subgroups for X-bar and R. Then freeze the limits and plot new data against them. Recalculate only after a real, understood change such as a new method or a process improvement, and record why.

Worked example: batch yield and assay Working level

Batch yield on an individuals chart

The first 20 batches of an intermediate gave a mean yield of 85.98 percent and an average moving range of 1.27. The limits and the estimated sigma follow.

UCL = 85.98 + 2.66 × 1.27 = 89.37 %

LCL = 85.98 − 2.66 × 1.27 = 82.59 %

Sigma = 1.27 / 1.128 = 1.13 %, moving range UCL = 3.267 × 1.27 = 4.16

Run rules: reading the pattern Working level

A point beyond three sigma is one signal. Patterns inside the limits can also show a change. These are the rules most plants use. They come from the Western Electric and Nelson sets, and your quality system should state which ones you apply.

  • One point beyond the three-sigma limit.
  • Two of three points in a row beyond two sigma on the same side.
  • Four of five points in a row beyond one sigma on the same side.
  • Eight points in a row on the same side of the centre line.
  • Six points in a row steadily rising or steadily falling (a trend).

Each extra rule makes the chart more sensitive and also raises the false alarm rate. A single three-sigma rule gives about 0.27 % false alarms per point for normal data. Over 20 points that is about a 5 % chance of at least one false signal. Adding rules raises this further. Choose a small set, apply it consistently and write it down.

Individuals charts of data with drift or serial correlation can show runs even when nothing has gone wrong, because neighbouring points are related. If a run appears but you can find no cause, check whether the data are autocorrelated before you chase it.

Cp and Cpk: control versus capability Working level

A control chart tells you whether the process is stable. Cp and Cpk tell you whether a stable process fits inside the specification. Only calculate them after the chart shows stability.

Cp = (USL − LSL) / (6 × sigma)

Cpk = min( USL − mean , mean − LSL ) / (3 × sigma)

Take an assay specification of 98.0 to 102.0 % (illustrative). From subgroups 1 to 12 the within-group sigma is 1.135 / 2.326 = 0.488, and the mean is 99.86.

Cp = 4.0 / (6 × 0.488) = 1.37

Cpk = (102.0 − 99.86) / (3 × 0.488) = 1.46 for the upper side and (99.86 − 98.0) / (3 × 0.488) = 1.27 for the lower side, so Cpk = 1.27

If the process then runs at the shifted mean of 100.55, with the same sigma, Cpk falls to 0.99. Nothing went out of specification, yet the margin shrank by a fifth. This is what a control chart gives you: the warning comes before the failure.

Many sites look for Cpk of at least 1.33 on critical quality attributes, but this is a company target, not a law. Cp and Cpk also assume roughly normal data and a stable process. With only a few points the estimate is uncertain, so treat the number as a guide.

Common mistakes Going deeper

  • Using specification limits as control limits. The process can be drifting badly inside the specification, and you will not see it.
  • Calculating limits from data that includes shifts or outliers. This inflates the limits. Use a stable period, or remove points with a known assigned cause and record the reason.
  • Recalculating the limits every week. The limits then follow the drift and never signal. Freeze them.
  • Mixing products, grades or equipment on one chart. One chart should hold one process. Different trains or recipes need separate charts, or the limits become meaningless.
  • Reacting to every point. Adjusting after a normal high or low point adds variation. Act on signals, not on single movements.
  • Rounded or poorly resolved data. If results are rounded so coarsely that most moving ranges are zero or one step, the limits collapse. Record enough decimals.

Checklist before you start Going deeper

  • One quality attribute, one process, one chart.
  • Data in time order, with batch numbers and the lot of key raw materials noted.
  • At least 20 stable points for the baseline; trial limits if fewer.
  • Chart type chosen: I-MR for one value, X-bar and R for subgroups.
  • Run rules chosen and written in the procedure.
  • A defined response to a signal: who checks, what is checked, how it is recorded.
  • Cpk calculated only after the chart shows stability, and read with its uncertainty.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between control limits and specification limits?

Control limits come from the process data and show what the process does when it is stable. Specification limits come from the customer, the pharmacopoeia or the registered file and show what is acceptable. A process can be in control and still fail the specification, and the other way round.

How many batches do I need to set control limits?

A common rule of thumb is at least 20 to 25 points from a period when the process was stable. With fewer points the limits are uncertain, so treat them as trial limits and review them as data comes in.

Which chart should I use for batch yield?

Use an individuals and moving range chart when there is one value per batch. Use an X-bar and R chart when you take several results per batch or per sampling round, for example five assay results from one blend.

Do control limits replace in-process limits or alert and action limits?

No. They work alongside them. Release and in-process limits protect quality. Control limits warn you early that the process is drifting, often well before a limit is breached. Your quality system decides how a control chart signal is handled.

Can I use a control chart if my data is not normal?

Charts are fairly robust for mild departures, but strongly skewed data, such as impurities near zero or counts, can give false alarms. Look at a histogram first, consider a transformation, and do not read Cpk literally when the data is badly skewed.
