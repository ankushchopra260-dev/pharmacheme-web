Generative AI for SOPs and Deviations

Quality teams spend much of their week writing, searching and reviewing text: SOPs, deviations, CAPAs, change controls. Generative AI is good at exactly that, and also good at sounding right when it is wrong. This article shows where it helps in a GMP plant, how answers from your own documents (RAG) work, what the regulators say, and gives a prompt builder for drafting deviation reports safely.

Where generative AI fits in GMP documents Basics

Large language models are good at reading, sorting and drafting text, and SOPs, batch records, deviations and CAPAs are mostly text. They are also capable of “confabulation”, which NIST defines as the production of confidently stated but erroneous or false content. The rule that follows: let the model draft, search and summarise; let qualified people decide and sign.

TaskGenerative AI roleWho decides
Finding the right SOP clauseSearch in plain language, with a link to the source paragraphThe user reads the source
Drafting or harmonising an SOPFirst draft from a template, old SOPs and the change requestAuthor, reviewer and QA approve as usual
Writing up a deviationStructures the facts the investigator gives into a clear descriptionInvestigator and QA
Root-cause investigationSuggests possible causes and the evidence to checkThe investigation team, on evidence
Trending deviations and complaintsGroups similar records across products and yearsQA reviews the clusters
TrainingQuestions and summaries from approved SOPsTrainer checks before use

FDA’s 2023 discussion paper on AI in drug manufacturing lists trend monitoring of complaints and deviation reports as an AI application. The EU draft GMP Annex 22 (July 2025) keeps generative AI out of critical GMP decisions, but allows non-critical use where a qualified person stays responsible for the output; EMA was reconsidering that boundary in 2026.

Answers from your own documents: RAG Basics

A general chatbot answers from what it learned in training, which knows nothing about your SOP-PRD-045 revision 7. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), described by Lewis and co-workers in 2020, combines a language model with a searchable store of documents: the system first finds the relevant passages in your approved documents, then writes an answer from those passages, with links back to them.

  1. Approved, current documents are loaded from the document management system (superseded versions excluded).
  2. A question comes in: “What is the maximum hold time for wet cake of product X?”
  3. The system retrieves the paragraphs most likely to answer it.
  4. The model answers only from those paragraphs, quotes them and links to them, or says it did not find the answer.
On the plant

The single most important test of a document assistant: ask it questions whose answer is not in your documents. A good system says “not found”; a bad one makes up a plausible hold time.

Fill in the facts; the tool builds a prompt for your company-approved assistant that drafts a clear deviation description and an investigation plan, without inventing facts or concluding the root cause. Do not enter confidential product names or batch numbers into public tools; use codes.

Before the draft goes to QA

  • Every fact in the draft is one you supplied or can show evidence for; delete anything the model added.
  • Times, values and limits match the batch record and the trend.
  • The root cause is still open until the evidence is in.
  • The record states that an AI tool helped draft it, if your quality system requires it.

Setting it up properly Going deeper

  • Approved tool, controlled data. An enterprise or on-premises system with access control, so a user only retrieves documents they are allowed to read.
  • Only current, approved documents in the store, refreshed from the document management system on every revision.
  • A test set. Fifty or more real questions with known answers, plus questions it must refuse; rerun after every change of model, prompt or documents.
  • Citations required. No answer without a link to the source paragraph.
  • Intended use written down. What it may and may not be used for, who reviews its output, and how it is monitored, in line with your computerised-system procedure and the ISPE GAMP AI guide (October 2025).

Mistakes I see most often

  • Pasting batch records and recipes into a public chatbot.
  • Letting the model write the root cause before the investigation.
  • Loading superseded SOPs, so the assistant quotes old limits.
  • No test with “unanswerable” questions.

Questions people ask

Can ChatGPT be used to write SOPs in a GMP plant?

It can produce a first draft, but only in a company-approved tool, without confidential data in public systems, and the SOP still goes through normal authoring, review and QA approval. The approved document, not the AI draft, is what counts.

Can generative AI decide the root cause of a deviation?

No. It can suggest possible causes and the evidence to check. The investigation team decides the root cause on evidence, and QA approves the conclusion. The EU draft Annex 22 keeps generative AI out of critical GMP decisions.

What is RAG and why does it matter for SOP search?

Retrieval-augmented generation first retrieves passages from your own approved documents and then answers from them, with links. It is far less likely to invent answers than a chatbot answering from memory, and you can check the source.
