Soft Sensors in Chemical Plants

Your lab tells you the impurity four hours after the sample was taken. By then the batch has moved on. A soft sensor fills the gap with an estimate every minute, built from measurements you already log. This article explains what soft sensors can estimate, how to build one, how to keep it honest, and includes a free tool to fit and test one on your own data.

What a soft sensor is Basics

A soft sensor (also called a virtual sensor or inferential) estimates a quantity that is slow, expensive or impossible to measure online, using measurements the plant already logs. The lab may report an impurity every four hours; the soft sensor gives a best estimate every minute from temperatures, pressures, flows and power.

What you want to knowFast inputs that often carry the information
Reaction conversion or residual starting materialHeat released (jacket flow × ΔT), dosing integral, reaction time, temperature profile
Distillate or bottoms purityTray temperatures, reflux ratio, pressure, feed rate
Moisture or residual solvent during dryingProduct and jacket temperature, vacuum level, condensate rate, drying time
Solids content or density of a slurryAgitator power, pump current, mass-flow meter density
Fouling of a heat exchangerCalculated U-value from flows and four temperatures

Kadlec, Gabrys and Strandt’s review of data-driven soft sensors in the process industry describes them as an alternative to traditional ways of acquiring critical process variables, used for monitoring and control. In pharma, the same idea sits inside FDA’s 2004 PAT framework, whose tools include multivariate tools for design, data acquisition and analysis, process analysers, process control tools, and continuous improvement and knowledge management tools.

Three ways to build one Basics

  • First-principles: a balance equation. U-value from heat duty and log-mean temperature difference is a soft sensor for fouling. Most robust outside past data, needs good instruments.
  • Data-driven: regression or machine learning from past data. Fast to build where the physics is messy; only valid inside the range it was trained on.
  • Hybrid: a physics model with a data-driven correction for what the physics misses. Often the best choice for reactors and dryers.

Build your own soft sensor (regression tool) Working level

Paste data exported from your historian and lab: one row per lab sample, up to three input columns, and the lab result in the last column, oldest row first. The tool fits a linear model on the first 70% of rows and tests it on the last 30%, the way a soft sensor would be tested on batches it has not seen. Your data stays in your browser.

Soft sensor builder

Columns separated by tabs, commas or spaces. Header row optional. At least 10 rows.

How to read it: a test R² close to the training R² means the model generalises; a much lower test R² means it learned noise or the process changed. The test error (RMSE) should be well inside the specification width before anyone uses the estimate.

Keeping it honest: drift and recalibration Working level

  • Keep taking lab samples. They are the reference that tells you when the soft sensor has drifted. Fewer samples, not none.
  • Set a limit in advance for the difference between estimate and lab; when it is crossed, stop using the estimate for decisions and investigate.
  • Watch the inputs. A soft sensor is only valid inside the range of its training data. Flag inputs outside it: new grade, new scale, new raw material supplier.
  • Recalibrate under change control, with the new model tested on data it has not seen. Try the drift demo in AI in the chemical industry.

Soft sensors under GMP Going deeper

If a soft sensor only helps operators, treat it as an advisory tool with ordinary engineering change control. If its output is used for a GMP decision, such as an end point, an in-process control or release, it becomes part of the control strategy: document the intended use, validate on independent data, freeze the model version and manage changes formally. The EU draft GMP Annex 22 (July 2025) sets out these expectations for AI models in critical GMP use.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Testing on random rows, so samples from the same batch sit in both training and test data and the model looks better than it is.
  • Lab results matched to the wrong time: the sample time, not the time the result was entered.
  • Using inputs that are set points rather than measurements.
  • Stopping lab sampling once the soft sensor “works”.

Questions people ask

What is a soft sensor in a chemical plant?

A model that estimates a slow or expensive measurement, such as an impurity, conversion or moisture, from fast measurements the plant already logs, such as temperatures, pressures, flows and power.

How much data do I need to build a soft sensor?

Enough lab results, matched to process data at the sample time, to cover the normal operating range: often a few dozen to a few hundred samples. Always test on the most recent data, not on random rows.

Can a soft sensor replace lab testing?

Not entirely. Lab results remain the reference that shows when the model has drifted. Many plants reduce sampling frequency once the soft sensor is proven, and keep a check sample on a fixed schedule.
