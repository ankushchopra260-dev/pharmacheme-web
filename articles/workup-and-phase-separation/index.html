Work-up and Phase Separation at Scale

Scale-up · Work-up · October 2026

In the lab, you shake the separating funnel, wait a minute and run off the lower layer. In the plant, the same work-up can take hours to settle, leave a cloudy rag layer at the interface, and lose product down the drain with the aqueous layer. This article explains why, and how to make a work-up that separates cleanly at scale.

Why two liquids separate Basics

Water and an immiscible solvent such as toluene, ethyl acetate or MDC form two phases because they don't dissolve in each other (much). Mixing breaks one phase into droplets dispersed in the other. When mixing stops, droplets fall or rise according to density, meet, merge (coalesce) and form a continuous layer. Separation needs both steps: settling to bring droplets together, and coalescence to merge them.

Settling: the Stokes law picture Basics

v = g × d² × Δρ / (18 μ) v settling velocity, d droplet diameter, Δρ density difference between phases, μ viscosity of the continuous phase.

Droplet size counts most, because it is squared. With a toluene-water density difference of about 130 kg/m³ and a water-like viscosity:

  • 100 µm droplets settle at about 0.7 mm/s: 2–3 minutes across a 10 cm separating funnel, but about 35 minutes across a 1.5 m plant reactor.
  • 30 µm droplets take about 26 minutes in the funnel and over 6 hours in the plant.

That is the scale-up problem: settling distance grows 15-fold, and plant agitators often make smaller droplets than a hand-shaken funnel.

Emulsions and rag layers Working level

An emulsion forms when droplets are stabilised against coalescing. A rag layer is a band of emulsion, often with fine solids, that sits at the interface and won't resolve. Common causes:

  • Fine solids (catalyst fines, inorganic salts, polymeric by-products) collecting at the interface.
  • Surface-active species: amphiphilic intermediates, soaps formed at high pH, residual surfactant.
  • Small density difference: MDC with heavy brine, or solvents close to water's density.
  • Over-mixing: high-speed agitation before settling makes very fine droplets.
  • A pH near the pKa of the product, where it partitions into both phases.

Breaking an emulsion Working level

  • Add salt (brine): increases the density difference and reduces the solubility of organics in water.
  • Change pH away from the problem range, or adjust temperature (viscosity falls and solubilities change).
  • Filter the mixture through a filter aid to remove interface solids, often the real cause.
  • Add more solvent or a different co-solvent to change density and viscosity.
  • Wait, gently: slow agitation helps droplets meet; no agitation at all can leave them suspended.

Test these in the lab on a plant sample before the campaign, and write the chosen remedy into the batch record.

Running separations in the plant Going deeper

  • Mix enough, not more: extraction needs contact, but not violent mixing. Lower the agitator speed for extractions, and reduce it further before stopping.
  • Specify settling time from plant experience, not from the lab funnel.
  • See the interface: a sight glass on the bottom outlet line, conductivity probes (water conducts, most solvents don't) or a capacitance probe. Run the lower layer slowly as the interface approaches.
  • Decide where the rag goes: keep it with the product layer, send it to a separate drum, or recycle it to the next batch. Decide in advance, not at 3 a.m.
  • Check losses: analyse the aqueous layer for product. A few percent of product in a large aqueous volume is a big yield loss.
On the plant

Take a sample from the reactor at the end of mixing and watch it settle in a measuring cylinder. If it takes 10 minutes in a 30 cm cylinder, you can expect roughly 50 minutes or more in a 1.5 m reactor, and you know before you start draining.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Copying the lab settling time into the plant batch record.
  • Mixing extractions at full agitator speed.
  • Ignoring interface solids as the cause of a rag layer.
  • Draining the lower layer fast as the interface approaches.
  • Not checking the aqueous layer for product loss.

Questions people ask

Why does phase separation take longer in the plant?

Longer settling distances and often smaller droplets; settling velocity goes with d².

How do you break an emulsion?

Brine, pH or temperature change, filtering out solids, more solvent, gentle agitation.

What is a rag layer?

A stable emulsion band at the interface, often stabilised by fine solids.
