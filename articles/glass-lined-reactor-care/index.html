Glass-Lined Reactor Care

Plant Engineering · Equipment Care · October 2026

A glass-lined reactor costs a lot, takes months to replace, and can be ruined in seconds by a dropped spanner or a blast of steam into a cold jacket. Yet it will last decades if a handful of rules are followed. This article explains how the lining works, what damages it, and how to inspect and repair it.

How a glass lining works Basics

A glass-lined reactor is a carbon steel vessel with a layer of special glass, typically about 1–2 mm thick, fused onto the inside at high temperature. The glass is applied so that it is held in compression by the steel as they cool; glass is very strong in compression and weak in tension. Anything that puts the glass in tension, such as a sudden temperature change, an impact or flexing of the steel, can crack or chip it. Once the glass is breached, the process liquid reaches the steel and corrodes it quickly.

Thermal shock Basics

Glass and steel expand at different rates. A sudden temperature difference between the glass surface and the steel behind it creates stress in the glass. Manufacturers publish charts of the allowable temperature difference, which depend on the vessel temperature and on whether you are heating or cooling. Typical dangerous situations:

  • Charging cold liquid into a hot empty vessel, or hot liquid into a cold one.
  • Switching the jacket from steam straight to brine, or brine to steam.
  • Spraying cold wash water onto a hot wall.

Ramp the jacket temperature in steps, clamp the jacket set point within the allowed difference in the DCS, and drain the jacket between utilities. See jacket design.

Mechanical and chemical damage Working level

  • Impact: dropped tools, sample thieves, scrapers, solids charged from height. Use protective covers on the manhole and non-metallic tools.
  • Agitator problems: a bent shaft or worn bearing lets the impeller strike the wall or baffle. Check runout after maintenance.
  • External damage: welding on the outside of the jacket or nozzles destroys the glass behind it. Never weld on a glass-lined vessel.
  • Chemicals: glass resists almost all acids, but is attacked by hydrofluoric acid and fluorides, hot concentrated phosphoric acid, and alkali, increasingly so with temperature and pH. Prolonged boiling water also slowly attacks glass.

Electrostatic damage Working level

Glass is an insulator. When non-conductive liquids such as toluene or heptane are stirred, filtered or sprayed into a glass-lined vessel, charge can build up on the glass surface until it discharges through the lining to the steel, leaving a pinhole. It is also an ignition risk. Remedies include conductive glass, earthed tantalum or PTFE-coated probes in contact with the liquid, slower filling through dip pipes, and adding conductive solvents where the process allows. See static electricity.

Inspection and repair Going deeper

  • Visual inspection at every opportunity: chips, dull or rough patches (chemical attack), rust stains (breach).
  • Spark (holiday) testing: a high-voltage brush passed over the glass shows sparks where there is a pinhole or crack to the steel. Use the voltage recommended by the manufacturer for the glass thickness.
  • Thickness measurement of the glass with a magnetic gauge shows gradual chemical wear.
  • Repairs: small defects are sealed with tantalum plugs and screws or tantalum patches, with PTFE gaskets; larger areas with repair cements or sleeves. Repairs are compatible with fewer chemicals than the original glass, so note them in the vessel history and check every new product against them.
On the plant

Keep a simple logbook for each glass-lined vessel: spark test results, repairs and their locations, and any thermal or mechanical incident. When a new product is planned for that reactor, the logbook tells you whether its chemistry will attack the old repairs.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Steam straight into the jacket of a cold vessel, or brine into a hot one.
  • Metal tools and sample thieves inside the vessel.
  • Hot caustic cleaning.
  • Welding on the jacket or nozzles.
  • Ignoring static with non-conductive solvents.

Questions people ask

What causes thermal shock in glass-lined reactors?

A sudden temperature difference between glass and steel, which puts the glass into tension.

What chemicals damage glass lining?

HF and fluorides, hot concentrated phosphoric acid, and hot alkali.

What is spark testing?

A high-voltage test that sparks through pinholes to show defects.
