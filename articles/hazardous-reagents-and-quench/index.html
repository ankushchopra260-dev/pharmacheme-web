Hazardous Reagents and Quench Design

Process Safety · Hazardous Operations · October 2026

A small group of reagents causes a large share of the incidents in API plants. They are not exotic: sodium hydride, lithium aluminium hydride, thionyl chloride, sodium azide. What they share is that their hazard is greatest at the end of the reaction, when the excess has to be destroyed. This article covers the main ones and the principles of a safe quench.

Why the quench is the dangerous step Basics

During the reaction, the reagent is consumed under conditions somebody designed carefully. The quench is different: it destroys whatever is left, often an uncertain amount, with water or an alcohol, releasing heat and gas. It is often written in the batch record as a single line (“quench with water”) and done near the end of a long shift. Many incidents follow that pattern.

The usual suspects Basics

ReagentMain hazardsKey precautions
Sodium hydride (usually 60 % in mineral oil)Hydrogen with water, alcohols and acids; known thermal runaways in DMF, DMSO and DMAcAvoid NaH with DMF/DMSO at elevated temperature unless tested; quench slowly with IPA before water; inerted vessel and safe H2 vent
Lithium aluminium hydrideViolent reaction with water, hydrogen; ether solventsControlled quench (for example the Fieser method: water, 15 % NaOH, water in a 1:1:3 ratio by weight of LAH); keep cold; vent H2
Thionyl chlorideReleases SO2 and HCl; violent with waterDistil off the excess before quench; route vents to a caustic scrubber sized for the gas
Sodium azide and organic azidesToxic and explosive HN3 with acid; explosive heavy-metal azides (copper, lead) in drains; diazidomethane with dichloromethaneNever acidify azide streams; no DCM with azide; destroy residual azide with nitrite under controlled conditions; check drains and metals
Organolithiums (n-BuLi)Pyrophoric; reacts violently with waterClosed transfer under nitrogen; cold, controlled quench

This is a starting list, not a complete one. Peroxides, nitration mixtures, diazo compounds and hydroxylamine all deserve the same level of care.

Designing a safe quench Working level

  • Know how much is left. Use the stoichiometry and an in-process check, not “excess”. The amount of reagent left decides the heat and gas.
  • Control by addition rate. The quench should be dose-controlled: heat and gas come out as you add, and stop when you stop. That needs the quench reagent to react quickly at the quench temperature. Too cold and it accumulates, then reacts all at once.
  • Choose the direction. Adding quench to the reaction (normal quench) or the reaction to the quench (reverse quench) gives very different local concentrations. Reverse quench into a large volume of cold quench solution often gives better control of heat and gas.
  • Use a mild quench first. For hydrides, IPA or ethyl acetate before water or acid destroys the bulk more gently.
  • Agitation on, interlocked. If the agitator stops, quench reagent pools on top and reacts violently when mixing restarts.

Gas evolution and venting Working level

Every mole of NaH gives a mole of hydrogen. Six kilograms of NaH (250 mol) releases about 5.6 Nm³ of hydrogen. Over 2 hours that averages 2.8 Nm³/h, but the peak rate is often higher. The vent line, flame arrester and scrubber must handle the peak without pressurising the vessel, and the headspace must stay inerted so the hydrogen has no oxygen to burn with inside the vessel.

The same logic applies to CO2 from bicarbonate quenches (foaming), SO2 and HCl from thionyl chloride, and nitrogen from azide destruction.

Before scale-up Going deeper

  • Run the quench in the reaction calorimeter at the plant addition rate, measuring heat and gas. See how to read RC1 data.
  • DSC the reaction mixture before quench, especially reagent/solvent combinations with known incidents.
  • Calculate peak gas rate and check the vent and scrubber capacity. See scrubber design.
  • Write the quench into the batch record as a controlled step with rate, temperature limits and stop conditions, not a single line.
On the plant

If a quench “isn't doing anything”, stop adding. A quench that seems not to react is the most dangerous kind: the reagent is accumulating, and when it starts, it will all go at once.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Quenching an unknown excess.
  • Quenching too cold, so the quench reagent accumulates.
  • Adding the quench faster because nothing seems to happen.
  • Vent lines sized for normal breathing, not for quench gas.
  • Using DMF or DMSO with NaH at elevated temperature without testing.

Questions people ask

Why is NaH in DMF dangerous?

The combination can decompose exothermically at moderate temperature; several runaways have been reported.

How much hydrogen does sodium hydride give on quench?

One mole of H2 per mole of NaH, about 0.93 Nm³ per kg of pure NaH.

What is a reverse quench?

Adding the reaction mixture into the quench, keeping the reactive species dilute.
