Gas Scrubbing Agents: Which Liquid for Which Gas

Process Safety · Emissions · October 2026

A scrubber is only as good as the liquid in it. Caustic soda handles most acid gases, but HF, NOx, HCN, SO3 and mercaptans each need something more, and some gases cannot be scrubbed at all. This table gives the usual agent for each gas, the reaction, how much agent each kg of gas uses, and the traps.

Scrubbing agent table Basics

Consumption is the stoichiometric amount of 100 % agent per kg of gas. Charge more than this: keep a reserve so the liquor never runs out during a peak.

Hydrogen, CO, CO2 and butane: standard practice Working level

These gases neither dissolve nor react in scrubbing liquors, so a scrubber does nothing for them. Routing them through one gives false comfort, and for flammable gases it can add an ignition source (the fan). Standard practice is to keep them out of the acid-gas scrubber and handle them by dilution, safe venting, recovery or combustion.

GasMain hazardStandard practice
H2 (hydrogen)Flammable from 4 to 75 % in air; very low ignition energy; rises quicklyDedicated hydrogen vent, nitrogen dilution, flame arrester, safe discharge point; flare or catalytic oxidation for large continuous flows

CO2 (carbon dioxide)Asphyxiant; heavier than airVent to a safe place outdoors; keep it off the caustic scrubber or size the caustic for it

Hydrogen

Hydrogen comes from hydrogenations, hydride and metal quenches (NaH, LiAlH4, NaBH4, sodium), and metals in acid.

  • Dilute: sweep the vent with nitrogen so the gas stays below 25 % of the LEL, that is about 1 % hydrogen. Size the nitrogen flow for the peak hydrogen rate, for example the first minutes of a hydride quench.
  • Separate vent: do not route hydrogen into the acid-gas scrubber header or through its fan unless they are designed and zoned for flammable gas. Keep hydrogen vents away from oxidiser and air vents.
  • Discharge safely: vent at a safe height above the roof, away from air intakes, lights and other ignition sources. Fit a flame arrester rated for hydrogen (gas group IIC) at the outlet, and earth the vent line.
  • Large flows: continuous hydrogen streams go to a flare or a catalytic recombiner.
  • Before opening: purge hydrogenation reactors with vacuum–nitrogen cycles until the hydrogen and oxygen readings are within the permit limits.
  • Detect: fixed hydrogen detectors near the reactor and the vent outlet.

Carbon monoxide

CO comes from oxalyl chloride reactions, formic acid decomposition, carbonylations and incomplete combustion.

  • Destroy it where flows are significant: a thermal oxidiser (typically 800 to 1,000 °C with about 1 s residence time), a catalytic oxidiser, or a flare oxidises it to CO2 (2CO + O2 → 2CO2).
  • Small flows: dilute and discharge through a stack high enough for the ground-level concentration to stay well below the exposure limit; check this with a dispersion estimate.
  • Never indoors: no CO vent may discharge inside a building or near an air intake.
  • Detect: fixed CO detectors, alarming below 25 ppm, in the reactor area and near the discharge. CO has no smell.

Butane and other light hydrocarbons

Butane comes from LPG handling, aerosol filling and as a by-product (for example from n-butyllithium, or tert-butyl group removal giving isobutylene).

  • Recover first: a refrigerated condenser recovers liquid where the quantity justifies it.
  • Then destroy: send what remains to a thermal oxidiser or flare.
  • Small vents: discharge through a flame arrester at a safe point. A flame arrester only stops flashback into the vent line; it does not treat the gas.
  • Header design: nitrogen-swept header with an oxygen analyser, so the mixture stays outside the flammable range.
  • Low points: butane is twice as heavy as air, so check pits, drains and trenches, and classify the area under the zone (ATEX) rules.

Carbon dioxide

CO2 comes from carbonate neutralisations, Boc deprotections and phosgene or chloroformate chemistry. Vent it to a safe place outdoors. In a caustic scrubber it uses caustic (CO2 + 2NaOH → Na2CO3 + H2O, 1.82 kg NaOH per kg), and sodium carbonate crystallises above about 17 %. So either keep CO2-rich vents off the caustic scrubber or include the CO2 in the caustic sizing (the packed scrubber calculator can do this). It is an asphyxiant and heavier than air: check enclosed spaces and pits.

Working out agent consumption Working level

Take the reaction and the molar masses. For chlorine: Cl2 + 2NaOH, so 2 × 40.0 kg NaOH per 70.9 kg Cl2, or 1.13 kg NaOH per kg Cl2.

Example: a step releases 45 kg of SO2 per batch into a 2,500 kg charge of 10 % NaOH (250 kg NaOH).

  • Each batch uses 45 × 1.25 = 56 kg NaOH.
  • Keeping a 2 % free-caustic reserve (50 kg NaOH) leaves 200 kg usable, enough for 3 batches, not 4.

Remember that other gases in the same vent use caustic too: HCl from the same step, and any CO2.

Rules for every scrubber Going deeper

  • One liquor, compatible vents: hypochlorite from a chlorine scrubber meeting acid gives chlorine, and meeting ammonia gives chloramines. Sulphide liquor meeting acid gives H2S. Check vent combinations with the chemical mixing hazard checker.
  • Size for the peak: the first minutes of a thionyl chloride distillation or a quench, not the batch average.
  • Watch the heat: neutralisation releases about 13.7 kcal per mol. A hot liquor absorbs worse and can drive gases such as NH3 and SO2 back out.
  • Measure, don’t smell: change the liquor at a set free-caustic or pH value, and put outlet detectors on toxic gases (HCN, phosgene, Cl2).
  • Validate special chemistry: oxidative treatments (cyanide, mercaptans, NOx) need tested pH, dose and contact time before use.

Questions people ask

Why is HF scrubbed with KOH and not NaOH?

Sodium fluoride has low solubility (about 4 g per 100 mL water) and crystallises on packing and nozzles. Potassium fluoride is very soluble, so KOH keeps the scrubber clean.

Can a caustic scrubber remove carbon monoxide or hydrogen?

No. CO, hydrogen and light hydrocarbons such as butane are hardly soluble and do not react with scrubbing liquors. They need dilution and safe venting, a flare or a thermal oxidiser.

How should hydrogen from a reaction be vented?

Through a dedicated nitrogen-swept vent, kept below 25 % of the LEL (about 1 % hydrogen), with a hydrogen-rated flame arrester at a safe discharge point. Large continuous flows go to a flare or catalytic recombiner. It is never sent through the acid-gas scrubber fan.

How much NaOH does it take to scrub chlorine?

About 1.13 kg of NaOH per kg of chlorine, plus excess so the liquor stays alkaline.
