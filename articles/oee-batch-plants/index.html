OEE was born on car-parts lines, where a machine makes one part every few seconds. A reactor makes one batch every day or two, sits idle waiting for QC, and spends hours being cleaned. OEE still works there, if you define it properly. This article explains availability, performance and quality for batch equipment, gives a calculator that shows where your hours go, and how to use the result to find capacity.

OEE in one line Basics

Overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) = availability × performance × quality. It was first used by Seiichi Nakajima, the founder of total productive maintenance (TPM), and is now standardised among the manufacturing KPIs of ISO 22400-2. It answers one question: of the time this equipment was meant to produce, how much produced good product at the ideal rate?

FactorBatch-plant meaningTypical losses
AvailabilityTime the reactor or train was actually running batches, out of planned production timeBreakdowns, waiting for materials, QC results or utilities, cleaning and changeover
PerformanceIdeal batch time × batches made, out of running timeSlow heating and cooling, long dosing, extra distillation, waiting between steps (see cycle-time debottlenecking)
QualityGood batches out of all batchesRejected, reprocessed or reworked batches

Batch OEE calculator

Planned downtime (no orders, planned shutdown) is excluded from OEE. Cleaning and changeover count as availability loss.

Use the best demonstrated batch time as the ideal, not the BMR’s standard time, which usually contains historical slack. Otherwise performance looks better than it is and the biggest loss stays hidden.

What is a good OEE? Working level

The commonly quoted “world-class” targets are availability above 90%, performance above 95%, quality above 99% and OEE above 85% (Emerson’s introduction to OEE). These come from discrete and continuous manufacturing; multi-product batch plants with long cleaning and QC waits rarely reach them. The useful comparison is with yourself: the same equipment month on month, and with the best month you have achieved.

Using OEE to find the money Working level

  • Measure the bottleneck, not every vessel: plant output is set by the slowest step.
  • Split the losses. The value of OEE is the breakdown into availability, performance and quality hours, each with an owner.
  • Automate the data. Batch start and end times from the MES or DCS batch engine, and downtime reasons from a simple pick-list, beat end-of-month spreadsheets (see process historian basics).
  • Turn hours into money. One extra batch a month is the contribution margin of that batch; see plant KPI dashboard and digital project business case.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Using the BMR standard time as the ideal, which hides slow steps.
  • Counting cleaning as planned downtime, so changeover losses disappear.
  • One OEE for the whole plant, which averages away the bottleneck.
  • OEE reported but no one owning each loss.

Questions people ask

How is OEE calculated for a batch process?

OEE = availability x performance x quality. Availability is running time over planned production time; performance is batches made times the ideal batch time, over running time; quality is good batches over all batches.

What is a good OEE for a batch chemical plant?

The common world-class targets (OEE above 85%) come from discrete and continuous manufacturing. Multi-product batch plants usually score lower because of cleaning, changeover and QC waits; compare each bottleneck with its own best month.

Should cleaning time count in OEE?

Yes, as an availability loss. Cleaning and changeover are real losses that can be reduced through campaign planning and faster cleaning; counting them as planned downtime hides them.
