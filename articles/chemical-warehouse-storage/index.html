Chemical Warehouse Management

Plant Departments · Warehouse · October 2026

A chemical warehouse is not just a store. It holds flammable, corrosive, toxic and reactive materials side by side, keeps GMP status for every container, and feeds the plant with exactly weighed charges. Most of its rules exist because something went wrong somewhere.

Every container has a status Basics

Each container shows its material, lot number and status: Quarantine, Approved, Rejected or Under test. Rejected material is kept in a locked, separate area until it is returned or destroyed. In a warehouse management system the status is controlled in software, but a physical label still matters for anyone walking the floor.

Segregation of incompatible materials Basics

Store materials by hazard, not by alphabet. The classic separations:

  • Oxidisers (nitrates, peroxides, hypochlorite, permanganate) away from flammables and organics.
  • Acids away from bases, and acids away from cyanides and sulphides, which release toxic gas.
  • Water-reactive materials (sodium metal, hydrides, acid chlorides, thionyl chloride) in dry areas, away from sprinklers where possible and from aqueous materials.
  • Pyrophoric catalysts kept wet and in closed containers.
  • Toxic and highly potent materials in a restricted area.

The safety data sheet (section 7, handling and storage, and section 10, stability and reactivity) is the starting point. A simple compatibility chart at the entrance helps everyone. The materials compatibility tool covers what containers and gaskets can hold.

Flammable solvents Working level

Solvents in drums and tanks bring the most risk. In India, petroleum products and many flammable liquids are covered by the Petroleum Rules and licensing by PESO, with limits on quantity and separation distances; the site’s licence states what may be stored where (see PESO licensing). Good practice:

  • Drum stores with ventilation, flameproof electrics, bunding and spill kits.
  • Earthing and bonding for drum transfer; see static electricity and solvent fires.
  • Tank farms with dykes, nitrogen blanketing where needed, and breather valves sized for breathing losses.
  • No storage of solvents in production areas beyond the shift requirement.

FIFO, FEFO and stock accuracy Working level

FIFO (first in, first out) issues the oldest lot first. FEFO (first expiry, first out) issues the lot that expires or reaches retest first, which matters when lots arrive with different shelf lives. Bin cards or system locations, regular cycle counts and immediate recording of every movement keep the stock accurate; an inaccurate stock is how a plant runs short of a material the system says it has.

Dispensing and reconciliation Going deeper

Dispensing turns approved stock into weighed charges for a specific batch. It is done in a dispensing booth with extraction (often a downflow booth for powders), on calibrated balances checked daily, with each charge labelled for the batch, double-checked and recorded. Potent materials need containment. Afterwards, issued, used and returned quantities are reconciled; an unexplained difference is a deviation.

Finished API is stored according to its label conditions (for example below 25 °C, protected from light), with temperature and humidity monitored and mapped across the store so the worst spot is known.

On the plant

Walk the store once a week with the SDS sections 7 and 10 of the five most hazardous materials in hand. Misplaced pallets and leaking drums are found by walking, not by the system.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Storing by supplier or alphabet instead of hazard.
  • Rejected material left in the approved area.
  • Drum transfers without earthing.
  • Issuing the newest lot because it is easiest to reach.
  • Balances not checked before dispensing.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between FIFO and FEFO?

FIFO issues the oldest received stock first; FEFO issues the stock with the earliest expiry or retest date first. FEFO is preferred where shelf lives differ.

Which materials should never be stored together?

Oxidisers with flammables, acids with bases, acids with cyanides or sulphides, and water-reactive materials with aqueous materials. Check section 10 of the SDS for each material.

Why is warehouse temperature mapping done?

To find the warmest and coldest spots so that materials with storage conditions are kept where those conditions are actually met.
