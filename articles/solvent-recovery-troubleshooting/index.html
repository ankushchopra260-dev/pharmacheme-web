Why Is Solvent Recovery Taking Longer Than Expected?

Plant Engineering · Solvent Recovery · Troubleshooting

A slow solvent recovery almost always comes down to one of three limits: not enough heat reaching the batch, a boiling point higher than planned (usually poor vacuum), or a condenser that can't keep up. A few readings taken during the distillation tell you which one, and the fix follows from that.

The three limits

Distillation rate = heat delivered to the batch ÷ latent heat of the solvent. The heat delivered is U × A × ΔT across the jacket, and none of it counts unless the condenser can turn the vapour back into liquid. So the batch runs at the pace of the weakest of these:

LimitWhat sets itTypical sign
Heat supplyHot water temperature and flow, steam pressure, jacket U and wetted area AJacket outlet close to inlet temperature but low distillate; or batch never reaches a full boil
Driving forceBoiling point of the batch, set by the pressure in the reactor and the batch compositionVapour temperature higher than planned for the solvent
CondensingCondenser area, coolant temperature and flow, non-condensable gasPressure climbs as heating increases; warm vent; solvent at the vacuum pump

Readings to take first

Take these in the middle of the distillation, once the batch is boiling steadily:

  1. Distillate rate: receiver level change over 15 to 30 minutes, converted to kg/h.
  2. Pressure at the reactor, not at the vacuum pump. A big difference between the two points to a restriction or an overloaded condenser.
  3. Vapour temperature at the reactor top and the batch temperature.
  4. Jacket inlet and outlet temperatures and the hot water flow, or the steam pressure at the jacket (not at the header).
  5. Condenser coolant inlet and outlet temperatures, and whether the vent line after the condenser is warm.
  6. Batch level against the top of the jacket.

Compare the vapour temperature with the boiling point of the solvent at the measured pressure. The boiling point under vacuum calculator or the solvent data pages give it. If they match, the gauge is right and the batch is boiling as expected for that pressure.

Worked example: 3.4 hours planned, 7 hours actual

A batch distils 3,000 L of toluene (2,601 kg) from 30 °C under vacuum, with 80 °C hot water, an average wetted area of 8 m² and a jacket U of 250 kcal/h·m²·°C. Toluene latent heat is about 84 kcal/kg and cp 0.41 kcal/kg·°C.

Planned at 100 mbar: toluene boils at 45.3 °C
Heat = 2,601 × (0.41 × 15.3 + 84) ≈ 2,34,800 kcal
Jacket duty = 250 × 8 × (80 − 45.3) ≈ 69,500 kcal/h → about 3.4 h (770 kg/h)

The batch actually takes about 7 hours. Mid-way, the distillate rate is about 370 kg/h, the reactor pressure gauge reads 200 mbar and the vapour is at 62 °C. First, check what U the jacket is actually giving:

Heat going in ≈ 370 × 84 ≈ 31,100 kcal/h
Apparent U = 31,100 / (8 × (80 − 62)) ≈ 216 kcal/h·m²·°C

That is close to the design 250, so the jacket is fine. The problem is the pressure. At 200 mbar toluene boils at 62 °C, so the temperature difference across the jacket falls from about 35 °C to 18 °C and the recovery time roughly doubles to 7 hours. The next step is the vacuum: check for an air leak with a rate-of-rise test, check the pump on its own, and check whether the condenser is letting vapour through to the pump.

If instead the apparent U had come out at about 150, with the pressure at 100 mbar as planned, the jacket would be the problem (fouling, poor circulation, or air or condensate in a steam jacket). That case gives about 5.6 hours.

Keeping the next batch on time

  • Plan the recovery time before the batch with the heat load and recovery calculator, and note which limit it expects.
  • Fit a pressure gauge on the reactor itself, and record pressure, vapour temperature and receiver level every 30 minutes.
  • Keep a log of apparent U per batch. A steady fall is fouling; a sudden drop is usually a utility or vacuum problem.
  • Do a vacuum rate-of-rise test after any maintenance on manways, sight glasses or the vacuum line.

Questions people ask

Why is solvent recovery taking longer than expected?

Almost always one of three limits: not enough heat reaching the batch, a boiling point higher than planned (usually poor vacuum, or high boilers building up), or a condenser that can't keep up. Measure the distillate rate, the pressure at the reactor and the temperatures to find which.

How much does poor vacuum slow down a distillation?

A lot, because it raises the boiling point and cuts the temperature difference across the jacket. In the example above, the same toluene recovery takes about 3.4 h at 100 mbar and about 7 h at 200 mbar.

How do I know if the condenser is the bottleneck?

The pressure rises when you add heat, the vent is warm, the coolant is warm or barely warms up, and solvent appears at the vacuum pump. A healthy condenser holds the pressure steady as the boil-up increases.
