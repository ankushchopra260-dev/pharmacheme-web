Pump Power Calculation: From the Basics to the Plant Floor

Chemical Engineering · Pumps · Updated October 2026

Someone asks you to transfer 20 m³/h of solvent from a storage tank into a reactor. What size motor does the pump need? The answer takes about five minutes, if you know which numbers matter. This article starts from what “pump power” actually means and works up to the things that catch people out on a real plant: receiver pressure, motor margins, pump curves and NPSH.

The idea in plain words Basics

A pump does one job: it gives energy to a liquid so the liquid can get somewhere it would not go by itself. Higher up, into a vessel at higher pressure, or simply through a long pipe that resists the flow.

Think of carrying buckets of water up a staircase. The work you do depends on two things: how much water you carry each minute, and how high the stairs are. Pumps are the same. Flow is how much you carry; head is how high the stairs are. Multiply them (with the liquid's weight) and you get the power the liquid receives.

The pump is not perfect, so it needs more power at its shaft than it gives to the liquid. The motor is not perfect either, so it draws more from the supply than it gives to the shaft. That is why there are three powers, each bigger than the last:

  1. Hydraulic power: what the liquid actually receives.
  2. Shaft power (also called brake power): what the pump takes at its shaft. Hydraulic power divided by pump efficiency.
  3. Motor input power: what the motor draws from the electrical supply. Shaft power divided by motor efficiency. This is what you pay for.

What “head” really is Basics

Head is energy per unit weight of liquid, expressed as a height in metres. It sounds abstract, but it is a useful trick: a pump that develops 30 m of head can lift any liquid 30 m, whether it is water, toluene or a heavy brine. The pressure it makes changes with the liquid's density; the head does not. That is why pump curves are drawn in metres.

The total head a pump must deliver has three parts:

H = (z2 − z1) + (P2 − P1) / (ρ g) + hf
  • Static head, z2 − z1: the height from the liquid level in the suction tank to the delivery point.
  • Pressure head, (P2 − P1) / ρg: any pressure the receiver holds above the suction tank, turned into metres of this liquid. For water, 1 bar is about 10.2 m. For toluene (SG 0.87) the same bar is about 11.7 m.
  • Friction head, hf: losses in the pipe, bends, valves, strainers, flow meters and heat exchangers on the way. It grows with the square of the flow.

The formula, and where 367 comes from Basics

Hydraulic power (kW) = ρ × g × Q × H / 1,000
Shaft power (kW) = Hydraulic power / ηpump
Motor input power (kW) = Shaft power / ηmotor

ρ is density in kg/m³, g is 9.81 m/s², Q is flow in m³/s, H is total head in m, and the efficiencies are fractions (0.65, not 65).

On the plant, flow is read in m³/h, not m³/s. Put Q in m³/h and density as specific gravity, and all the constants fold into one number:

Hydraulic power (kW) = Q (m³/h) × H (m) × SG / 367
367 = 3,600 × 1,000 / (1,000 × 9.81). It is worth remembering.

If you think in horsepower, 1 hp = 0.746 kW.

Example 1: water to an overhead tank Working level

Water at 50 m³/h, total head 30 m (static lift plus friction, tank open to atmosphere). Pump efficiency 65 %, motor efficiency 92 %.

Hydraulic power = 50 × 30 × 1.0 / 367 ≈ 4.09 kW
Shaft power = 4.09 / 0.65 ≈ 6.29 kW
Motor input = 6.29 / 0.92 ≈ 6.84 kW

You would not buy a 6.84 kW motor; you pick a standard size. The next size up is 7.5 kW, and many plants would fit exactly that. Apply the stricter margin rule in the motor section below (125 % of 6.29 kW ≈ 7.9 kW) and it becomes the next size again, 9.3 kW (12.5 HP).

Example 2: solvent into a pressurised reactor Working level

This is closer to daily life in a pharma or chemical plant. Toluene (870 kg/m³, SG 0.87) is transferred at 20 m³/h into a reactor held at 2 barg under nitrogen. The storage tank is atmospheric. Static lift is 12 m; friction in the line, including a strainer and a flow meter, is 5 m. Pump efficiency 60 %, motor efficiency 90 %.

First turn the reactor pressure into head of toluene:

Pressure head = 200,000 / (870 × 9.81) ≈ 23.4 m
Total head = 12 + 5 + 23.4 ≈ 40.4 m

Then the power:

Hydraulic = 20 × 40.4 × 0.87 / 367 ≈ 1.92 kW
Shaft = 1.92 / 0.60 ≈ 3.20 kW
Motor input = 3.20 / 0.90 ≈ 3.55 kW

Look at where the head comes from: more than half of it is the reactor pressure. Forget it, and you would size the pump for 17 m instead of 40 m. The pump would run, but it would not deliver against a pressurised reactor. This is the single most common mistake in transfer pump sizing.

On the plant

Reactor pressure is not always constant. If the same pump also has to transfer while the reactor is being pressure-tested or vented to a scrubber, use the highest pressure it will see during transfer. And remember that the suction tank may be under nitrogen blanket too; use the difference.

Choosing the motor Working level

Two things push the motor size above the calculated number.

Margin. Pumps rarely run exactly at their duty point, liquids change, impellers wear and strainers choke. A widely used rule, from the API 610 pump standard, sets the motor nameplate rating against the pump's rated shaft power:

Pump shaft powerMotor rating at least
Up to 22 kW125 % of shaft power
22 to 55 kW115 %
Above 55 kW110 %

In Example 2, 3.20 kW × 1.25 = 4.0 kW. Where 4 kW IEC motors are stocked, that is the pick. Many Indian plants standardise on 3.7 kW (5 HP) instead, which is just short, so the next size, 5.5 kW (7.5 HP), is the safe choice.

End-of-curve power. On a normal centrifugal pump, shaft power keeps rising as flow increases. If the pump can ever run out to high flow, say when the reactor is empty and at atmospheric pressure so the head is much lower, it will draw more power than at the duty point. Read the shaft power at the right-hand end of the pump curve and make sure the motor covers it. Otherwise the overload trips on the first batch.

Positive displacement pumps (gear, screw, diaphragm) behave the opposite way: flow is nearly fixed and the power rises with discharge pressure. Size their motors at the relief valve setting, and never throttle their discharge.

Pump curves and why efficiency is not a constant Going deeper

So far we have used a single efficiency figure. In reality a centrifugal pump's efficiency depends on where it operates on its curve. Each pump has a best efficiency point (BEP). Run it at 80–110 % of the BEP flow and you get the catalogue efficiency, quiet running and long seal life. Run it far to the left (throttled hard) and efficiency falls, the liquid recirculates inside the casing, it heats up, and seals and bearings suffer. Far to the right, efficiency falls again and NPSH required shoots up.

Where a pump actually runs is set by two curves crossing: the pump curve (head falls as flow rises) and the system curve (static head plus friction, which rises with flow squared). Change either one and the operating point moves. Closing a discharge valve steepens the system curve; slowing the pump with a VFD lowers the pump curve. The second wastes far less energy, because power falls roughly with the cube of speed on a friction-dominated system. Our VFD article works through the numbers.

Viscous liquids are another trap. Catalogue curves are for water. Above roughly 20–50 cP, a centrifugal pump loses head, flow and especially efficiency; at a few hundred cP a gear or screw pump is usually the better choice. The Hydraulic Institute correction method gives the factors; for a quick check, expect efficiency to drop noticeably once viscosity gets into the tens of centipoise.

NPSH: the problem power calculations don't show Going deeper

A pump can have the right motor and the right head and still not work, because the liquid boils in its own suction. If the pressure at the impeller eye drops below the liquid's vapour pressure, vapour bubbles form and then collapse violently as the pressure rises. That is cavitation: a gravel-like noise, falling flow, and pitted impellers.

NPSH available = (Psurface − Pvapour) / (ρ g) + suction static height − suction friction
Keep NPSH available above the pump's NPSH required, with a margin of at least 0.5–1 m (more for hot or volatile liquids).

This bites hardest in pharma plants when pumping out of a vacuum receiver. Say toluene at 40 °C sits in a distillation receiver at 100 mbar abs. Toluene's vapour pressure at 40 °C is about 79 mbar, so the liquid is close to boiling:

(10,000 − 7,900) Pa / (850 × 9.81) ≈ 0.25 m
+ 2.0 m liquid height above the pump − 0.3 m suction friction ≈ 1.95 m NPSH available

A small transfer pump needing 2.5 m NPSH will cavitate. The fixes are physical: raise the receiver or lower the pump to gain height, use a short, large-bore suction line with no strainer pressure drop, cool the liquid, or break the vacuum with nitrogen before pumping out. A bigger motor does nothing.

Checking a running pump Going deeper

You can work out a running pump's real efficiency with three readings: flow, suction and discharge pressure, and motor input power.

  1. Head from the gauges: H = (Pdischarge − Psuction) / (ρ g), adding any height difference between the two gauges.
  2. Hydraulic power from flow and head, as above.
  3. Shaft power = motor input power (from a power meter, or √3 × V × I × PF) × motor efficiency.
  4. Pump efficiency = hydraulic power / shaft power.

If a pump that was 65 % efficient when new now gives 45 %, the impeller is worn, the wear rings are open or it is running far from BEP. That gap, multiplied by running hours and the power tariff, tells you whether an overhaul or a smaller impeller pays.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Leaving out the receiver pressure, or using gauge pressure on one side and absolute on the other.
  • Using static lift only and forgetting friction through strainers, flow meters and control valves.
  • Using water's density for a solvent or a brine, or forgetting that pressure in metres depends on density.
  • Sizing the motor for the duty point only, then tripping at run-out.
  • Stacking a margin on the flow, another on the head and another on the motor, and ending up with a pump that spends its life throttled.

Questions people ask

What is the formula for pump power?

Hydraulic power (kW) = ρ × g × Q × H / 1,000, with ρ in kg/m³, Q in m³/s and H in m. Divide by pump efficiency for shaft power, then by motor efficiency for motor input power.

What is the quick formula from m³/h?

Hydraulic power (kW) = Q (m³/h) × H (m) × SG / 367. For 50 m³/h of water at 30 m head, about 4.1 kW.

What is the difference between hydraulic power and shaft power?

Hydraulic power is what the liquid receives. Shaft power is what the pump takes at its shaft; it is higher because of losses inside the pump. Shaft power = hydraulic power / pump efficiency.

What is the pump capacity formula?

Turn the power formula round: Q (m³/h) = 367 × shaft power (kW) × pump efficiency / (H × SG). A 7.5 kW motor driving a 65 % efficient pump against 30 m of water gives roughly 55 m³/h. The pump curve gives the exact figure.

How do I convert pressure to head?

Head (m) = ΔP (Pa) / (ρ × 9.81). For water, 1 bar is about 10.2 m; for a lighter liquid the same pressure is more metres.

How much margin should a pump motor have?

A common rule is 125 % of shaft power up to 22 kW, 115 % from 22 to 55 kW and 110 % above that, then the next standard size. Also check the shaft power at the end of the pump curve.
