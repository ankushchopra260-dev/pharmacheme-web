Solvent Storage Tanks: Breathing Losses and Vents

Process Design · Storage · October 2026

A solvent tank farm loses solvent every day without a single leak. Each time a tanker fills a tank, the gas pushed out of the vent carries vapour with it; each sunny afternoon, the tank warms and breathes out a little more. The amounts look small per event and add up to tonnes a year. This article explains where the losses come from and the equipment that controls them and keeps the tank safe.

Why tanks lose solvent Basics

The space above the liquid in a solvent tank is saturated with vapour. Whenever gas leaves the tank, vapour leaves with it:

  • Filling (working) losses: each m³ of liquid pumped in pushes out about one m³ of saturated headspace gas.
  • Breathing losses: daytime heating expands the headspace and raises the vapour pressure; the tank breathes out. At night it cools and breathes in air, which then saturates again.

The amount depends almost entirely on the solvent's vapour pressure at the tank temperature. Acetone, MDC and methanol lose far more than toluene or xylene.

Estimating filling losses Working level

Solvent in vented gas (kg/m³) = Pvap × M / (R × T) Pvap in Pa from the Antoine equation, M in kg/mol, R = 8.314 J/mol·K, T absolute (°C + 273.15).

Toluene at 30 °C has a vapour pressure of about 4.9 kPa, so each m³ of vented gas carries 4,890 × 0.092 / (8.314 × 303) ≈ 0.18 kg. Filling 100 m³ a month loses about 214 kg of toluene a year from filling alone. The same tank of acetone loses several times more.

For full estimates including breathing losses, use the US EPA AP-42 Chapter 7 methods, which account for tank colour, diameter, daily temperature swing and paint condition.

Conservation vents and arresters Working level

A pressure-vacuum conservation vent (breather valve) stays closed over a small pressure range, for example opening at +20 mbar and −5 mbar. Small daily temperature changes are absorbed without venting, which cuts breathing losses. It also protects the tank: storage tanks are very weak, often designed for only tens of millibars, and a blocked vent can collapse a tank under vacuum when it is pumped out or cooled by rain.

On flammable solvent tanks, a flame arrester or an end-of-line deflagration arrester stops an external flame entering the tank through the vent. Arresters block with polymer, dust or ice, so they need inspection. A separate emergency vent (a hinged manhole cover or large relief) handles fire exposure, which the breather cannot.

Nitrogen blanketing Working level

A blanketing regulator admits nitrogen whenever tank pressure falls (during emptying or cooling) and the conservation vent releases gas when it rises. This keeps air out, so the headspace stays below the LOC and cannot burn, and keeps moisture out of hygroscopic solvents. See inerting and LOC.

Blanketing does not reduce filling losses: the gas pushed out is now nitrogen saturated with vapour instead of air saturated with vapour. Set the blanketing pressure below the vent's opening pressure, with enough margin that nitrogen isn't vented continuously.

Reducing losses Going deeper

  • Vapour balancing: connect the tank vent back to the road tanker during unloading, so the displaced vapour goes into the tanker instead of the air. It is the cheapest large reduction.
  • Vent condenser: cool the vented gas with chilled water or brine to condense most of the solvent. At 5 °C, toluene's vapour pressure falls to about 1.2 kPa, removing roughly three-quarters of the loss.
  • Insulation and white paint reduce the daily temperature swing and breathing loss.
  • Carbon adsorption or a scrubber for toxic solvents where the remaining emission must be very low.
On the plant

Watch the breather valve during a tanker unloading. A vent that blows continuously during filling is normal; one that blows when nothing is moving means the blanketing regulator is set above the vent pressure, and you are paying to vent nitrogen and solvent day and night.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Assuming nitrogen blanketing reduces filling losses.
  • Blanketing pressure set above the conservation vent setting.
  • Uninspected flame arresters blocked with polymer or ice.
  • No vacuum protection, risking tank collapse when emptied.
  • Unloading tankers without vapour balancing.

Questions people ask

What is a breathing loss?

Vapour pushed out when the tank warms during the day, with no liquid transfer.

How much solvent is lost when filling a tank?

About Pvap·M/(RT) per m³ filled; for toluene at 30 °C, 0.18 kg/m³.

Does nitrogen blanketing reduce emissions?

It prevents fires but not filling losses; displaced nitrogen carries the vapour out.
