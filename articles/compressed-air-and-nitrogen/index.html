Compressed Air & Nitrogen: Calculations and Savings

Utilities · Air & Gases · Updated October 2026

Compressed air is easy to use and expensive to make: of every 100 kW a compressor draws, only about 10–15 kW ends up as useful work at the user. Nitrogen is a safety utility in any plant handling flammable solvents. This article covers the calculations behind both: what leaks cost, how to find them, what pressure costs, how big a receiver should be, and how much nitrogen inerting really takes.

How a compressed air system works Basics

A compressor squeezes atmospheric air to 6–8 barg. Squeezing air heats it, so an aftercooler cools it and most of its moisture condenses and is drained. A dryer removes more, a receiver stores a buffer, and a pipe network carries it to the users: valves and actuators, instruments, pneumatic pumps, filter blowing, conveying.

The catch is efficiency. Most of the electricity a compressor draws becomes heat in the compressor, not useful work at the user. So every leak, every unnecessary bar of pressure and every use of air for cleaning or cooling is paid for several times over in electricity.

Air leaks: flow and cost Working level

Above roughly 0.9 barg, air through a hole reaches sonic speed (choked flow). The mass flow then depends only on the hole area and the upstream absolute pressure:

m (kg/s) = Cd × A × P0 × √[k / (R T) × (2/(k+1))(k+1)/(k−1)]
k = 1.4, R = 287 J/kg·K, T absolute (°C + 273.15), P0 in Pa abs, Cd ≈ 0.6–0.65 for a sharp hole

A 3 mm hole at 7 barg leaks about 0.43 m³/min of free air. At a typical 6.5 kW per m³/min that is 2.8 kW, around the clock: about ₹1.8 lakh a year at ₹8/kWh over 8,000 hours. Ten such leaks is ₹18 lakh.

Measuring leakage without finding every leak Working level

On a load/unload compressor, stop all air users (a weekend or a shutdown), let the system settle and time several load and unload periods:

Leakage (%) = Tload / (Tload + Tunload) × 100
Leak flow = leakage fraction × compressor free air delivery

Example: a 20 m³/min compressor loads for 40 s and unloads for 80 s with everything stopped. Leakage = 40/120 = 33 %, about 6.7 m³/min, or roughly 43 kW wasted. Plants without a leak programme are typically at 20–30 %; a good programme holds 5–10 %. Then find the leaks with an ultrasonic detector, tag them and fix them in batches: quick couplings, hose clamps, FRL drains stuck open, cylinder seals and flange joints are the usual places.

What pressure costs Going deeper

Adiabatic power = n × k/(k−1) × P1 × Q × [(P2/P1)(k−1)/(n k) − 1]
Shaft power = adiabatic power / overall efficiency (about 0.7–0.8)

For 10 m³/min in a single stage, the theoretical power at 7.5 barg is about 7.7 % higher than at 6.5 barg. The rule of thumb is 6–8 % per bar. Many plants run at 7.5–8 barg because one user at the far end needs 6 barg and the header has a large pressure drop. Fixing that pipe, or giving the one user a small booster, is cheaper than running the whole system 1.5 bar high. A leak also grows with pressure, since choked flow is proportional to absolute pressure.

Air receiver size Working level

V (m³) = Q (m³/min free air) × t (min) × 1.013 / (P1 − P2)

To carry a 10 m³/min surge for 1 minute while the pressure may fall from 7.5 to 6.5 barg needs about 10 m³. A receiver near a big intermittent user (filter press, centrifuge discharge, bag filter pulse jets, pneumatic conveying) stops a standby compressor starting for a few seconds of demand, and gives a dryer time to work.

Dryers and dew point Going deeper

Air leaving an aftercooler is saturated. A refrigerated dryer gives about +3 °C pressure dew point, fine for general plant air indoors. Instrument air, air for outdoor lines in winter and air touching product need a desiccant dryer, usually −40 °C pressure dew point. Heatless desiccant dryers use 15–20 % of the air for purge, so do not dry all plant air to −40 °C if only instruments need it.

Nitrogen for inerting Going deeper

Most solvent vapours cannot burn below about 10–12 % oxygen, the limiting oxygen concentration (LOC). Plants usually aim for 8 % or less, confirmed with an oxygen analyser.

Pressure or vacuum purge: O₂ after n cycles = O₂start × (Plow / Phigh)n (absolute)
N₂ per cycle (Nm³) = V × (Phigh − Plow) / 1.013
Sweep purge, well mixed: N₂ (Nm³) = V × ln(O₂start / O₂target)

Worked example

A reactor with 6 m³ of headspace:

  • Pressure purge to 2 barg, vent to atmosphere: each cycle multiplies the oxygen by 1.013/3.013 ≈ 0.34. After one cycle 7.1 % (too close to 8 %), after two 2.3 %. Two cycles use about 24 Nm³.
  • Vacuum to 200 mbar abs, break with nitrogen: one cycle gives about 4.1 % and uses about 4.8 Nm³. Vacuum purging is the most nitrogen-efficient.
  • Sweep purge: ln(21/8) × 6 ≈ 5.8 Nm³ with perfect mixing; allow at least 1.5 times that in practice, and check the oxygen at the vent.

An open manhole, a nitrogen header that runs low during a big transfer, or compressed air used to push solvent undoes all of this. Nitrogen for blanketing must also replace the liquid pumped out of a tank: 20 m³/h of liquid at 50 mbarg needs about 19 Nm³/h of nitrogen.

Questions people ask

How do you measure compressed air leakage?

With all users stopped, leakage % = load time / (load time + unload time) × 100. Multiply by the compressor capacity for the leak flow.

How much does lowering compressor pressure save?

About 6–8 % of compressor power per bar around 7 barg, plus less air lost through leaks.

Is nitrogen purity important for inerting?

Yes. 95 % nitrogen contains 5 % oxygen, so it can never purge below 5 %, and needs more cycles to reach 8 %. Choose the purity from the solvent's LOC and the margin you want.
