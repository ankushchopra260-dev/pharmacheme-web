Hydrogen Solubility in Solvents

Reaction Engineering · Hydrogenation

Hydrogen barely dissolves in anything. At 25 °C and 1 bar of hydrogen, a litre of methanol holds about 8 mg, toluene or IPA about 6 mg and water only 1.6 mg. The amount rises in proportion to pressure and, in organic solvents, slightly with temperature. Because the liquid holds so little, plant hydrogenations are almost always limited by how fast the gas dissolves, not by the catalyst.

Henry's law for hydrogen

At the pressures used in pharma and fine-chemical hydrogenations (up to about 100 bar), dissolved hydrogen follows Henry's law:

xH2 = pH2 / H(T)    pH2 = Ptotal, abs − pvap, solvent x mole fraction of hydrogen in the liquid; H Henry's constant (MPa), which depends on the solvent and temperature. Use the hydrogen partial pressure, not the gauge reading: add 1.013 bar to the gauge pressure and subtract the solvent's vapour pressure.

For hydrogen the Henry's constants are large: about 140 MPa in n-hexane, 320 MPa in toluene, 630 MPa in methanol and 7,200 MPa in water at 25 °C. A large constant means low solubility. The hydrogen solubility calculator has measured values for 16 solvents.

Pressure: the strongest lever

Solubility is directly proportional to the hydrogen partial pressure. In methanol at 25 °C:

Hydrogen pressureDissolved H2, mg/L
1 bar g14.7
4 bar g38.6
10 bar g86.4

The gas–liquid transfer rate is kLa × (C* − C), and C* is this solubility. So doubling the hydrogen pressure roughly doubles the maximum rate at which hydrogen can reach the catalyst. That is why a reaction that crawls at 2 bar g can run well at 8 bar g in the same reactor.

Temperature: the opposite of most gases

Most gases dissolve less as the liquid warms. Hydrogen in organic solvents does the opposite: dissolving is slightly endothermic, so solubility rises with temperature. Brunner's measurements at BASF show the mole fraction rising by about 15–25 % from 25 to 50 °C in ten common solvents. In water hydrogen solubility falls slightly up to about 50 °C and then rises slowly.

At a fixed total pressure there is a catch: the solvent's vapour pressure also rises and takes a growing share of the headspace. In toluene at 4 bar g the dissolved hydrogen goes from 29 mg/L at 25 °C to 36 mg/L at 60 °C and 39 mg/L at 80 °C. In methanol, whose vapour pressure climbs quickly, the gain stops at around 40–50 °C (an estimate: the methanol data end at 25 °C). In practice, temperature mostly speeds up the reaction itself; its effect on solubility is modest.

Which solvent holds the most

Per mole, non-polar solvents dissolve the most hydrogen: n-hexane about 7 × 10−4 mole fraction at 1 atm and 25 °C, toluene 3.2 × 10−4, DMF and NMP about 1.4 × 10−4. Per litre the picture changes, because small molecules pack more moles into a litre:

Between the common organic solvents the difference is at most two to three times, so a solvent change rarely rescues a slow hydrogenation on its own. Water is the outlier: a water-rich solvent mixture holds much less hydrogen than the dry solvent.

How much hydrogen is in your batch

Take 2,000 L of methanol at 25 °C under 4 bar g hydrogen:

pH2 = 5.013 − 0.169 (methanol vapour pressure) = 4.84 bar
x = 0.484 MPa / 626 MPa = 7.7 × 10−4 → 38.6 mg/L
Dissolved hydrogen in 2,000 L ≈ 77 g

Now compare with what the reaction needs. Reducing 1 kmol of a nitro group to an amine consumes 3 kmol of hydrogen, about 6 kg. The liquid holds about 1 % of that at any moment. Every gram the catalyst uses must be replaced by fresh gas dissolving from the bubbles. With a kLa of 0.1 s−1, the most methanol can take up at 4 bar g is roughly 7 mol of hydrogen per litre per hour. A large reactor with poor gas dispersion may reach only a tenth of that kLa. See gas–liquid reactions and kLa for the full picture.

What it means on the plant

  • Raise the pressure first. It raises solubility, and with it the transfer rate, in direct proportion. Check the reactor's design pressure and the relief settings first.
  • Then improve gas dispersion. A gas-inducing or hollow-blade impeller, a sparger under the impeller, and enough power per volume raise kLa far more than extra catalyst does.
  • Lab rates do not scale directly. A small autoclave with a fast stirrer can have several times the plant kLa. If the lab reaction was already mass-transfer limited, the plant will be slower unless pressure or agitation make up the difference.
  • Dissolved hydrogen leaves when the pressure drops. When the reactor is vented, hydrogen comes out of the liquid. Vent to a safe place and purge with nitrogen, typically three cycles, until hydrogen is below a safe level before opening the reactor or filtering the catalyst. See hydrogenation safety.
  • Samples fizz. A sample taken under pressure releases dissolved hydrogen as it depressurises. Handle it as a flammable gas source.

Questions people ask

How much hydrogen dissolves in a solvent?

Very little: at 25 °C and 1 bar of hydrogen, about 8 mg/L in methanol, 6 mg/L in toluene or IPA, and 1.6 mg/L in water. It rises in proportion to the hydrogen pressure.

Does hydrogen dissolve better at higher temperature?

In organic solvents, yes, by roughly 15–25 % from 25 to 50 °C. In water it falls slightly up to about 50 °C. At a fixed total pressure, the solvent's rising vapour pressure takes back part of the gain.

Why is my hydrogenation slow at plant scale?

Usually because hydrogen cannot dissolve fast enough. Higher pressure and better gas dispersion help more than extra catalyst.

Is dissolved hydrogen a hazard when the reactor is opened?

Yes: it comes out of the liquid as the pressure drops. Vent safely and purge with nitrogen before opening or filtering.
