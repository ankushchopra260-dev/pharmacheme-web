Relief Valve Sizing Basics

Process Design · Pressure Protection · October 2026

A relief valve sits on top of a vessel for years doing nothing, and then has a few seconds to do its one job. Whether it succeeds depends on decisions made long before: which scenario it was sized for, whether the pipe to it is too long, whether someone fitted a valve underneath it. This article explains how relief sizing works, from the basic idea to an API 520 calculation, and where the common traps are.

Why vessels need relief Basics

Every vessel has a pressure it was designed for. Control systems, alarms and trips keep it well below that pressure most of the time. But all of them can fail: a control valve sticks open, cooling water stops, a fire starts under the vessel. The relief device is the last line of protection: a purely mechanical device that opens when the pressure reaches its set point and lets enough fluid out to stop the pressure rising further.

“Enough” is the important word. A relief valve that opens but is too small simply slows the pressure rise, and the vessel still fails. Sizing is about matching the valve's capacity to the worst credible flow it must pass.

Set pressure, overpressure and MAWP Basics

  • MAWP (maximum allowable working pressure) is the highest pressure the vessel is certified for at its design temperature. It is on the nameplate.
  • Set pressure is where the valve starts to open, normally at or below the MAWP.
  • Overpressure is how far above MAWP the pressure may rise while the valve is discharging at full capacity: 10 % for a single valve on a process upset, 16 % with several valves, and 21 % for an external fire (ASME Section VIII and API 520/521).
  • Relieving pressure = set pressure + overpressure. Sizing uses this pressure.

Operating pressure should sit at least 10 % below set pressure, or the valve will simmer and leak.

Finding the governing scenario Working level

API 521 lists the causes of overpressure to consider. For a typical batch reactor in a pharma plant, the list usually narrows to:

  • External fire around the vessel.
  • Blocked outlet on a vent or condenser line while nitrogen or steam keeps flowing in.
  • Control valve failure on the nitrogen supply, steam to the jacket, or a pump discharging into the vessel.
  • Loss of cooling during reflux or reaction.
  • Runaway reaction or decomposition.
  • Tube rupture in a heat exchanger with a high-pressure side.

Each credible scenario gives a relieving load. The largest one, the governing scenario, sets the size. Write all of them down with the reasoning: a future reviewer needs to see that the runaway case was considered, not forgotten.

The fire case, worked through Working level

In a fire, the liquid inside boils and the vapour must escape. API 521 gives the heat absorbed by a vessel with adequate drainage and firefighting:

Q = 43,200 × F × Awetted0.82  (W) Awetted: wetted area in m², counted up to 7.6 m above grade. F: environment factor, 1.0 for a bare vessel, lower with approved insulation. Without adequate drainage, the constant is 70,900.

Take a 6 m³ reactor holding toluene with a wetted area of about 12 m², set at 3 barg:

  1. Q = 43,200 × 120.82 ≈ 43,200 × 7.67 ≈ 331 kW.
  2. Relieving pressure = 3 × 1.21 + 1.01 ≈ 4.64 bar abs. Toluene boils at about 175 °C at this pressure, where its latent heat is about 310 kJ/kg.
  3. Relief load = 331 / 310 ≈ 1.07 kg/s, about 3,850 kg/h of toluene vapour.

Sizing the orifice with API 520 Working level

For a gas or vapour in critical (choked) flow, which is almost always the case when the valve discharges to a header near atmospheric pressure:

A = W / (C × Kd × P1 × Kb × Kc) × √(T × Z / M) A in mm², W in kg/h, P1 relieving pressure in kPa abs, T in K, M molar mass. C = 0.03948 × √[k (2/(k+1))(k+1)/(k−1)] from the ratio of specific heats k. Kd = 0.975 for a preliminary valve size; Kb = 1 for conventional valves with low back pressure; Kc = 0.9 with a rupture disc upstream, otherwise 1.

For toluene, k ≈ 1.09 gives C ≈ 0.0247. With W = 3,850 kg/h, P1 = 464 kPa, T = 448 K and Z taken as 1: A ≈ 760 mm², or 1.18 in². Valves come in standard orifice sizes, labelled by letter in API 526, so the next size up is a J orifice (1.287 in²).

This is a first estimate. The final size uses the manufacturer's certified coefficient for the chosen valve, and Z from real gas data when the pressure is high.

Rupture discs and runaway reactions Going deeper

A rupture disc is a thin membrane that bursts at a set pressure. It opens fully in milliseconds, has no moving parts and is tight against leaks and corrosive vapours. But once burst, it stays open until replaced. Discs are used alone on reactors where a fast, large opening matters, and upstream of a relief valve to protect it from corrosion (with a pressure gauge between them to detect a leaking disc).

A runaway reaction is a different problem from fire. As temperature climbs, the batch foams and swells, so the relief discharges a two-phase mixture of liquid and vapour. Two-phase flow needs a much larger area than vapour alone, often several times larger, and if the reaction also produces gas the requirement grows again. These cases are sized with the DIERS methods using adiabatic calorimetry data (for example from a VSP or ARC test), and usually lead to a large rupture disc discharging to a catch tank. A relief valve sized only for the fire case gives no real protection against a runaway. See emergency relief and vent sizing.

Installation: where good sizing goes wrong Going deeper

  • Inlet pressure loss above 3 % of set pressure makes the valve open and close rapidly (chatter), which can destroy it. Keep inlet lines short and at least the valve's inlet size.
  • Back pressure from a long discharge line or a shared header reduces capacity. Above about 10 % of set pressure, use a balanced-bellows valve and the Kb correction.
  • No isolation valve under a relief device unless it is locked or car-sealed open under a written procedure.
  • A safe discharge: to a scrubber, flare, catch tank or a safe high point, never into a work area or back into a building.
  • Testing: bench-test valves at the interval the inspection plan sets, and record the as-found pressure. A valve found stuck is a near miss worth investigating.
On the plant

The most common finding in relief audits is not a wrong calculation. It is a change nobody linked to the relief: a bigger nitrogen regulator, a new reaction in an old reactor, a higher steam pressure on the jacket. Every one of those changes the scenarios, which is why relief belongs in management of change.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Sizing only for fire and never considering the runaway case.
  • Using the set pressure instead of the relieving pressure in the formula.
  • Isolation valves under relief valves left closed after maintenance.
  • Long, small-bore inlet lines that make the valve chatter.
  • Not revisiting relief sizing when a new product moves into an old reactor.

Questions people ask

What overpressure is allowed?

10 % above MAWP for a single valve on a process upset, 16 % with several valves, and 21 % for fire.

How is the fire case load calculated?

Q = 43,200 × F × A0.82 W with adequate drainage; divide by the latent heat at relieving conditions.

Can a relief valve protect against a runaway?

Usually not if sized for vapour only. Runaways need two-phase sizing with DIERS methods and calorimetry data.
