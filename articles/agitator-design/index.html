Agitator Design Basics

Process Design · Mixing · October 2026

Ask why a reactor has the agitator it has, and the answer is often “it came with the vessel”. Yet the agitator decides whether solids stay suspended, whether two liquid phases react, whether a crystallisation gives fines, and how fast heat reaches the jacket. This article explains what agitators do and how to read the numbers behind them.

What the agitator has to do Basics

Mixing is not one thing. Each duty needs something different:

  • Blending miscible liquids: needs bulk flow around the whole vessel.
  • Solids suspension: needs upward flow along the base to lift particles.
  • Liquid-liquid dispersion: needs shear to break drops, and flow to keep them dispersed.
  • Gas dispersion (hydrogenation, oxidation): needs high shear at the impeller to break bubbles.
  • Heat transfer: needs flow past the jacket wall.

Before choosing an impeller, decide which duty matters most for this process.

Radial and axial impellers Basics

A radial impeller (Rushton turbine, flat-blade turbine, the retreat-curve impeller of glass-lined reactors) throws liquid outward to the wall, where it splits into two loops, one above and one below. It produces high shear near the blades. An axial impeller (pitched-blade turbine, hydrofoil, marine propeller) pumps liquid down the shaft to the base and back up the walls in one big loop: efficient for blending and solids suspension.

Power number and power draw Working level

P = Np × ρ × N³ × D⁵ P in W, Np power number (a property of the impeller shape, constant in turbulent flow), ρ density in kg/m³, N speed in revolutions per second, D impeller diameter in m.

Typical turbulent power numbers in baffled vessels: Rushton turbine about 5, pitched-blade turbine about 1.3, hydrofoil about 0.3. Glass-lined retreat-curve impellers are lower and depend strongly on the baffling. A 0.6 m Rushton at 100 rpm in a liquid of 900 kg/m³ draws about 1.6 kW; a hydrofoil of the same size at the same speed draws about 0.1 kW, but moves a similar amount of liquid.

Two things follow from the equation. Power rises with the cube of speed, so a 20 % speed increase needs 73 % more power. And it rises with the fifth power of diameter, so a slightly larger impeller can overload a motor that was fine before. Specify the motor for the heaviest liquid and the highest speed the process will see, including start-up in a dense slurry.

Common scale-up measures are power per volume (W/m³) and tip speed (πND). Typical P/V: gentle blending 0.1–0.3 kW/m³; solids suspension 0.3–1; gas dispersion 1–3. See mixing scale-up.

Baffles and vortexing Working level

Without baffles, a centrally mounted impeller in a low-viscosity liquid mostly spins the liquid round as a whole. A vortex forms, air is drawn in, little top-to-bottom mixing happens, and the power number drops sharply. Standard practice is four wall baffles, each one-tenth to one-twelfth of the vessel diameter wide. Glass-lined reactors use one or two beaver-tail or finger baffles through the top nozzles, which give less complete baffling, so their mixing is gentler than a fully baffled steel vessel at the same speed.

Choosing an impeller Going deeper

DutyUsual choice
Blending low-viscosity liquidsHydrofoil or pitched-blade turbine
Solids suspensionPitched-blade or hydrofoil, pumping down, low clearance from the base
Gas dispersionRushton or concave-blade turbine, gas sparged under the impeller
Liquid-liquid dispersionPitched-blade or Rushton, depending on drop size needed
Viscous liquids (above roughly 10,000 cP)Anchor or helical ribbon, close to the wall
Glass-lined, corrosive serviceRetreat curve or glass-lined pitched-blade, with finger or beaver-tail baffle

Tall vessels need two or more impellers on the shaft, spaced about one diameter apart. Check that the lowest impeller is covered at the minimum batch volume; an impeller running at the surface whips in air and vibrates.

On the plant

Look into the manhole at full speed with a torch (safely, and only where permitted). A deep vortex, a still surface, or solids lying in the corner of the base tell you more about the mixing than any calculation.

Mistakes I see most often

  • Choosing an impeller without deciding the main mixing duty.
  • No baffles with low-viscosity liquids.
  • Raising speed or impeller size without checking motor power (N³, D⁵).
  • Lowest impeller uncovered at minimum volume.
  • Scaling up by rpm instead of P/V or tip speed.

Questions people ask

What is the power number of an impeller?

A constant for the impeller shape: about 5 Rushton, 1.3 pitched blade, 0.3 hydrofoil.

Why do reactors need baffles?

To stop swirl and vortexing and create top-to-bottom flow.

Which impeller is best for solids suspension?

An axial impeller pumping down, close to the base.
