Concepts in motion

Four ideas that come up in almost every process engineering interview. Move the slider and watch the equipment and the graph change together, then read the answer you would give.

Boiling Under Vacuum

Lower pressure × Same liquid = Lower boiling point

log10 P = A − B / (C + T)    (Antoine, toluene)

Toluene boiling in a still under vacuum

Say it in an interview

A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the pressure above it. Pull a vacuum and that pressure falls, so the liquid boils at a lower temperature. That's why solvents are recovered under vacuum: less heat, less damage to a heat-sensitive product, and hot water or low-pressure steam is enough.

On the plant

Toluene boils at about 111 °C at atmosphere and about 52 °C at 100 mmHg. Distilling it off a heat-sensitive product at that vacuum keeps the batch below 60 °C.

Go too deep and the condenser can't keep up: the vapour boils colder than the coolant can condense it, and solvent goes out through the vacuum pump.

Pump Affinity Laws

Flow ∝ N    Head ∝ N²    Power ∝ N³

Q2/Q1 = N2/N1    H2/H1 = (N2/N1)2    P2/P1 = (N2/N1)3

Centrifugal pump on a VFD, rated 1450 rpm

Say it in an interview

For the same pump at a different speed, flow changes with speed, head with speed squared and power with speed cubed. A small cut in speed gives a big cut in power: at 80% speed the pump needs only about 51% of the power.

On the plant

A pump throttled on its discharge valve burns energy across the valve. A VFD that slows the pump to the flow you need saves most of it. Trimming the impeller follows roughly the same laws, with diameter in place of speed.

The laws hold best on friction-only systems. With a large static head, slowing the pump cuts flow much faster, and below a certain speed it delivers nothing.

Reynolds Number

Density × Velocity × Diameter / Viscosity

Re = ρvD / μ    laminar < 2300 < transitional < 4000 < turbulent

Water at 20 °C in a 50 mm pipe

Say it in an interview

Re = ρvD/μ tells you whether flow is laminar or turbulent. In a pipe, below about 2300 the fluid moves in smooth layers; above about 4000 eddies mix it across the pipe. Turbulent flow transfers heat and mixes far better, which is why exchangers are designed for it, but it costs more pressure drop.

On the plant

Water at 1 m/s in a 50 mm line gives Re ≈ 50,000, well turbulent. A 500 cP oil at the same speed gives Re ≈ 90: laminar, with a much lower heat transfer coefficient in its exchanger.

Watch the velocity profile: parabolic when laminar, nearly flat when turbulent.

Boyle's Law

Pressure × Volume = Constant

PV = k  ⇔  P ∝ 1/V    (T constant, P absolute)

Nitrogen at constant temperature

Say it in an interview

For a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Squeeze it to half the volume and the pressure doubles, because the same molecules hit the walls twice as often. Always use absolute pressure: bar(a), not bar(g).

On the plant

Pressure purging with nitrogen: take a vessel from 1 to 4 bar(a) and vent back to 1 bar(a). Each cycle cuts the oxygen to a quarter, 21% to 5.25% to about 1.3%. Do the same sum in gauge pressure and you get the wrong number of cycles.
