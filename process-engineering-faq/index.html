Process Engineering FAQ

Practical answers for process, technology transfer, and production engineers — search a question or browse by area.

🔍

What is distillation?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Distillation separates components of a liquid mixture based on differences in volatility (boiling point) — the mixture is heated so the more volatile component vaporizes preferentially, and that vapor is then condensed back to a liquid richer in that component. It's one of the most common separation and purification techniques in chemical and pharmaceutical processing, used both to purify a product and to recover or remove solvents.

What is evaporation, and how is it different from distillation?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Evaporation removes a volatile solvent from a solution by vaporizing it, leaving behind a more concentrated solution or, if carried far enough, a solid residue — but unlike distillation, evaporation isn't primarily about separating and collecting the vapor as a purified product; the vapor is often just discarded or recovered as a byproduct. Distillation, by contrast, uses that vapor deliberately to achieve a separation between two or more components.

What is extraction?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Extraction transfers a dissolved compound from one liquid phase into another, immiscible liquid phase, based on the compound's relative solubility (partition coefficient) between the two phases — commonly used to pull a product out of a reaction mixture and into a cleaner solvent, or to remove impurities from a product stream. Liquid-liquid extraction is the most common form in chemical processing, though solid-liquid extraction (leaching) is also widely used.

What is filtration?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Filtration separates solid particles from a liquid (or gas) by passing the mixture through a porous medium that retains the solids while letting the liquid pass through. In chemical and pharmaceutical processing it's the standard way to isolate a crystallized or precipitated solid product from its mother liquor.

What is crystallization?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Crystallization is the process of forming solid crystals from a solution, typically by reducing the solubility of a dissolved compound — through cooling, evaporating solvent, or adding an antisolvent — so that the dissolved material comes out of solution as an ordered solid structure. It's widely used both to isolate a product and to purify it, since impurities are often left behind in the mother liquor.

What is drying, in a process engineering sense?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Drying removes residual liquid (usually solvent or water) from a wet solid, typically by applying heat, reducing pressure, or both, until the material reaches an acceptable residual moisture or solvent specification. It's usually the final step before a solid product is packaged, and it needs to balance thorough solvent removal against avoiding thermal degradation of the product.

What is absorption, and how is it different from adsorption?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Absorption is when a gas (or sometimes a liquid) is taken up into the bulk volume of a liquid or solid, becoming dissolved or incorporated throughout — like carbon dioxide absorbing into water. Adsorption, by contrast, is when a substance adheres to the surface of a solid (or sometimes liquid) without penetrating into the bulk, like activated carbon adsorbing color bodies from a solution. The distinction matters because it changes what's controlling the rate and capacity of the process — bulk solubility for absorption, surface area for adsorption.

What is centrifugation used for in process engineering?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Centrifugation uses rapid rotation to generate centrifugal force that separates components of different density — commonly used to separate solids from a liquid (as an alternative or complement to filtration) or to separate two immiscible liquid phases faster than gravity settling alone would achieve. In pharmaceutical and fine chemical manufacturing, centrifuges are frequently used to isolate crystallized solids from their mother liquor.

What is size reduction (milling/micronization)?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Size reduction mechanically breaks larger solid particles into smaller ones, using equipment ranging from simple mechanical mills to jet mills that use high-velocity gas to shatter particles through collisions. In pharmaceutical processing, controlling final particle size is often important for how a drug substance dissolves, flows, and is handled during formulation.

What is agitation/mixing, and why does it matter?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Agitation is the mechanical action of stirring a liquid or slurry, typically with a rotating impeller, to blend materials, suspend solids, disperse gases or a second liquid phase, or promote heat transfer. It matters because many chemical reactions and physical processes (like crystallization or extraction) depend on bringing materials into intimate contact at a rate the process actually needs — inadequate mixing can become the rate-limiting step even when the underlying chemistry is fast.

What is heat exchange, and what's a heat exchanger?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Heat exchange is the transfer of thermal energy from a hotter fluid to a cooler one without the two fluids mixing directly, typically across a solid wall (like a metal tube or plate). A heat exchanger is the equipment that does this — ranging from a simple jacketed vessel to dedicated shell-and-tube or plate heat exchangers — and it's fundamental to heating, cooling, condensing, and evaporating throughout chemical processing.

What is scrubbing, in the context of chemical plant operations?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Scrubbing removes unwanted gases, vapors, or particulates from a gas stream by contacting it with a liquid (or sometimes a solid) that absorbs or reacts with the contaminant, commonly used to treat vent gases before they're released to the atmosphere or to recover a valuable solvent vapor. A scrubber is the equipment that provides this gas-liquid contact, often using packing or trays to maximize contact area.

What is decantation?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Decantation separates two immiscible liquids, or a liquid from settled solids, simply by carefully pouring or draining off the upper layer once the mixture has been allowed to settle and separate by gravity. It's one of the simplest separation techniques, often used as a quick first step before a more thorough separation like filtration or centrifugation.

What's the difference between a batch process and a continuous process?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

A batch process handles a fixed quantity of material through a complete sequence of steps before starting the next batch — common in pharmaceutical and fine chemical manufacturing where flexibility, smaller volumes, and traceability of each batch matter. A continuous process runs material through the system steadily without starting and stopping, which is typically more efficient at very large scale and consistent product volumes, but less flexible for making different products or smaller quantities.

What is reflux, in a distillation context?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Reflux is the practice of condensing some of the vapor leaving a distillation column and returning it to flow back down the column, where it contacts rising vapor and improves the separation achieved (more theoretical stages of separation) for a given column. Increasing the reflux ratio (the amount refluxed relative to the amount taken off as product) generally improves separation but also increases the energy and time needed to complete the distillation.

What is a theoretical plate (or theoretical stage) in distillation?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

A theoretical plate represents one full equilibrium contact between vapor and liquid — the hypothetical ideal where the vapor leaving and liquid leaving that stage are in perfect equilibrium with each other. Real distillation columns are rated by how many theoretical plates' worth of separation they achieve, which is a standard way to compare and design separation equipment even though no real tray achieves perfect equilibrium.

What is residence time?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Residence time is how long a given amount of material actually spends inside a piece of equipment or a process step — for a continuous process, it's typically the vessel volume divided by the volumetric flow rate; for a batch process, it's simply the duration of that step. It's an important parameter because many processes (reactions, drying, heat transfer) need a minimum residence time to reach completion, and too little residence time is a common cause of incomplete processing.

What's the difference between yield, conversion, and selectivity?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Conversion is the fraction of starting material that has reacted at all; selectivity is the fraction of that converted material that went to the desired product rather than side products; and yield is the overall fraction of theoretical product actually obtained, which combines both conversion and selectivity (yield equals conversion times selectivity, roughly speaking). A reaction can have high conversion but poor yield if selectivity to the desired product is low.

What is viscosity, in practical terms?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to flow — a high-viscosity fluid (like honey) flows sluggishly and resists being pumped or stirred, while a low-viscosity fluid (like water) flows easily. It directly affects pump selection, mixing requirements, heat transfer rates, and filtration behavior, which is why it's one of the first physical properties checked when handling an unfamiliar material.

What is vapor pressure, and why does it matter for process engineering?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Vapor pressure is the pressure exerted by a substance's vapor when it's in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature, and it increases with temperature — a substance boils when its vapor pressure equals the surrounding pressure. It matters because it determines a solvent's volatility, flammability risk, evaporation rate, and how distillation or vacuum operations will behave for that material.

What is boiling point, and how does pressure affect it?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Boiling point is the temperature at which a liquid's vapor pressure equals the surrounding (ambient or system) pressure, causing it to vaporize throughout the bulk liquid rather than just at the surface. Reducing the surrounding pressure (as in vacuum distillation) lowers the boiling point, which is exactly why vacuum is used to distill heat-sensitive materials at a lower, safer temperature.

What is solubility?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Solubility is the maximum amount of a substance that can dissolve in a given solvent at a specific temperature (and sometimes pressure) before the solution becomes saturated and any additional material remains undissolved. Solubility typically changes with temperature, which is the basis for cooling crystallization — dissolving a compound hot, where solubility is higher, then cooling to force it back out of solution.

What is a slurry?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

A slurry is a mixture of solid particles suspended in a liquid, thick enough to still flow but containing a significant proportion of undissolved solids — common in crystallization (crystals suspended in mother liquor) and in charging solid raw materials as a pumpable mixture rather than a dry powder. Handling equipment (pumps, valves, agitators) needs to be specifically suited to slurries, since standard liquid-handling equipment can struggle with abrasive or settling solids.

What's the difference between a suspension and an emulsion?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

A suspension is solid particles dispersed in a liquid (like a slurry), while an emulsion is one liquid dispersed as tiny droplets within another liquid it doesn't naturally mix with (like oil droplets dispersed in water). Both are inherently unstable over time and will eventually separate unless stabilized, which is why suspensions and emulsions often need continued agitation or a stabilizing agent to remain uniform.

What is a catalyst?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without itself being permanently consumed in the reaction, typically by providing an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy. Catalysts are enormously important in chemical manufacturing because they can make reactions economically viable at much lower temperatures, pressures, or times than would otherwise be needed.

What's the difference between an exothermic and an endothermic reaction?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

An exothermic reaction releases heat as it proceeds (the products have lower energy than the reactants), while an endothermic reaction absorbs heat from its surroundings to proceed (the products have higher energy than the reactants). This distinction is central to process safety — an exothermic reaction can self-accelerate and potentially run away if the heat it generates isn't removed fast enough, which is exactly why reaction calorimetry is performed before scaling up a new exothermic process.

What is pH, in simple terms?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

pH is a scale (typically 0 to 14) describing how acidic or basic a water-based solution is, based on the concentration of hydrogen ions present — lower pH means more acidic, higher pH means more basic (alkaline), and pH 7 is neutral. It's one of the most fundamental measurements in chemical processing because it affects reaction rates, solubility, extraction behavior, corrosion, and product stability.

What does molarity mean, and how is it different from just 'concentration'?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Molarity is a specific, precisely defined way of expressing concentration — the number of moles of a substance dissolved per liter of solution. 'Concentration' is a more general term that could be expressed in many ways (molarity, weight percent, parts per million, grams per liter, etc.), so molarity is really just one particular, very common unit for expressing concentration precisely enough for chemical calculations like stoichiometry.

What is a unit operation?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

A unit operation is a single, well-defined physical processing step — like distillation, filtration, drying, or heat exchange — that can be analyzed and designed somewhat independently of the specific chemicals involved, because the same fundamental physics (heat transfer, mass transfer, fluid flow) applies regardless of the exact material. Breaking a complex chemical process down into a sequence of unit operations is the standard way chemical engineers analyze and design a plant.

What does validation mean in a pharmaceutical manufacturing context?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Validation is documented evidence that a process, piece of equipment, or analytical method consistently does what it's intended to do, produced by executing predefined tests or trial runs against predetermined acceptance criteria. It's a core regulatory requirement in pharmaceutical manufacturing — a process isn't considered proven simply because it worked once; it needs to be shown to work reliably and reproducibly under real operating conditions.

What's the difference between a raw material, an intermediate, and an API?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

A raw material is a starting substance brought into the process from outside (a reagent, solvent, or starting chemical); an intermediate is a substance produced partway through a multi-step synthesis that isn't the final desired product but is used to make it; and the API (Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient) is the final biologically active substance that goes into a drug product. A single synthesis might pass through several intermediates before reaching the API.

What is a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

An SOP is a documented, step-by-step set of instructions for carrying out a specific task or process consistently every time it's performed, covering everything from equipment setup to the exact sequence of actions and the acceptance criteria for each step. SOPs are fundamental to GMP manufacturing because consistency and reproducibility depend on everyone following the same validated procedure rather than relying on individual judgment each time.

What does 'unit operation scale-up' actually mean, in plain terms?

Basic Concepts & Terminology
▸

Scale-up means taking a process that's been developed and proven at a small (lab or pilot) scale and successfully reproducing the same result at a much larger, commercially useful scale — which is harder than it sounds, because many physical effects (mixing time, heat transfer rate, addition time) don't change proportionally with size. Good scale-up work identifies which of these physical effects actually control the process outcome and designs the larger-scale equipment and procedure around preserving those specific conditions.

Why does a reaction that works perfectly in the lab behave differently at pilot scale?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Scale-up changes physical parameters that don't scale linearly with volume: mixing time typically increases, heat transfer surface area per unit volume decreases, and addition/charging times get longer relative to the reaction rate. A reaction that's mixing-limited or heat-transfer-limited at large scale can behave completely differently even though the chemistry itself hasn't changed. Always ask what's actually controlling your reaction rate at lab scale before assuming it will scale directly.

What are the most important factors to check before scaling up a reaction?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Focus on heat transfer capability (can the plant reactor add/remove heat as fast as the reaction needs?), mixing time versus reaction rate, addition/charge time relative to reaction kinetics, and whether the reaction is adiabatic-safe if cooling is lost. Reaction calorimetry data (heat of reaction, adiabatic temperature rise) is usually the single most valuable piece of information before committing a new reaction to a larger vessel.

Why does mixing take longer at larger scale even with a similarly powerful agitator?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Mixing time generally scales unfavorably with vessel size for a fixed impeller tip speed or fixed power per unit volume — larger vessels have proportionally longer blend times because the ratio of impeller-swept volume to total volume shrinks. This is one of the most common reasons a reaction that seemed instantaneous on the bench becomes addition-rate or mixing limited in the plant.

What is a Haz-Op and when should one be performed?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

A Hazard and Operability study (Haz-Op) is a structured, team-based review of a process design or procedure where each process parameter (flow, temperature, pressure, level, etc.) is systematically examined with guide words like 'more,' 'less,' 'none,' and 'reverse' to identify what could go wrong and what the consequences would be. It should be performed before running any new process at pilot or production scale, and repeated whenever a process changes significantly. This describes the general concept — the specific scope, methodology, and team composition for a Haz-Op should follow your organization's process safety management procedures and applicable regulatory requirements.

Why is reaction calorimetry important before running a new process?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Calorimetry measures the heat of reaction and the rate at which heat is generated, which tells you whether your reactor's cooling capacity can keep up with the reaction, and what the adiabatic temperature rise would be if cooling were lost. Without this data, you don't actually know whether a runaway is possible — it's the single most important safety screening step before scaling up an exothermic reaction. This explains why calorimetry matters in general — the specific safety screening tests required for a given reaction should be determined by a qualified process safety specialist based on the actual chemistry involved.

What should a batch record capture?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

A batch record should capture every material charged (with lot numbers and quantities), every process step performed with actual times and observed conditions (temperature, pressure, pH, etc.), in-process sample results, deviations from the procedure, and who performed and verified each step. It needs to be complete enough that someone could reconstruct exactly what happened without being present.

What's the purpose of a process flow diagram in pilot plant work?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

A process flow diagram shows the sequence of unit operations, the equipment involved, and the major streams (raw materials, intermediates, waste) flowing between them. It's the reference document operators and engineers use to understand how the whole process fits together, and it's usually the starting point for a Haz-Op.

What are common mistakes to avoid when developing a process meant to be scaled up?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Common ones: relying on very fast additions or instantaneous mixing that won't be achievable at scale, using lab techniques that don't translate (like swirling a flask by hand), not characterizing the heat of reaction, assuming filtration or drying rates from a Buchner funnel will hold at plant scale, and not building in enough margin for longer hold times and addition times.

Why do batches typically take longer at pilot or plant scale than at the bench?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Charging, heating, cooling, and phase separations all take proportionally longer as equipment gets larger, because heat transfer area and addition rates don't scale at the same rate as volume. A charge that takes seconds from a pipette can take an hour from a plant-scale addition vessel, and a reaction that seemed to run to completion instantly on the bench may take hours to reach the same conversion once mixing and heat transfer become rate-limiting.

What does GMP mean for pilot plant operations?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Good Manufacturing Practice means documented, reproducible procedures, calibrated and qualified equipment, traceability of materials and actions (who did what, when), and a quality system that reviews deviations. Even in early-stage pilot work, building GMP-friendly habits (good batch records, equipment logs, clear procedures) makes the eventual transfer to full GMP manufacturing much smoother.

How do I choose the right type of agitator for a reactor?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

It depends on what you need to accomplish: turbine or pitched-blade impellers are good general-purpose choices for blending and heat transfer, anchor or helical impellers are better for viscous fluids where wall scraping matters, and high-shear impellers are needed for dispersing immiscible liquids or breaking up solids. Match impeller type to whether your duty is bulk blending, heat transfer, solids suspension, or gas dispersion — a single impeller type rarely does all of these well.

Why does my reaction seem to be mixing-limited at larger scale?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

If a reaction's rate depends on how quickly two phases or reagents are brought into contact, blend time becomes the limiting factor once the mixing rate falls below the intrinsic reaction rate. Signs of this include yield or selectivity dropping as scale increases even though temperature and stoichiometry are unchanged. The fix is usually to slow the addition rate, improve impeller design, or add the reagent closer to the impeller discharge.

What's the safest way to charge flammable solvents into a reactor?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Use closed, grounded and bonded transfer systems wherever possible, avoid free-fall or splash filling that generates static charge, purge the vessel with inert gas if the solvent's flash point is a concern, and never charge a flammable solvent into a vessel that may contain an ignition source or hot surface. Subsurface addition (dip-pipe below the liquid level) significantly reduces static generation compared to top-splash charging.

What causes poor reaction control (temperature excursions) during scale-up?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Usually a mismatch between the rate of heat generation (which can scale faster than expected if addition is fast) and the rate of heat removal (which is often limited by jacket area per unit volume, which shrinks as the vessel gets bigger). Slowing the addition rate, using a controlled semi-batch addition, or pre-cooling reagents are the standard fixes.

How do I know if a process sample is representative of the whole batch?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Sample from a well-mixed zone, ideally while the agitator is running, and avoid sampling right after a charge before it's had time to distribute. For heterogeneous or viscous systems, take samples from more than one location or depth and compare. If results vary noticeably between samples, that's a sign the batch isn't fully mixed yet.

What makes an extraction efficient or inefficient?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Extraction efficiency depends on the partition coefficient of the solute between the two phases, the volume ratio of the phases, and the number of extraction stages. A single extraction with a favorable partition coefficient can still leave significant product behind — multiple smaller extractions are almost always more efficient than one large one for the same total solvent volume.

Why does batch distillation performance change when I scale it up?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Larger stills often have proportionally less surface area for heat input relative to the batch volume, meaning distillation takes longer. Column efficiency (theoretical plates) can also differ if the plant column's packing or trays aren't equivalent to whatever was used at bench scale, changing the separation you actually achieve for the same reflux ratio.

What causes poor crystal quality — too many fines or hard agglomerates?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Fines are usually caused by excessive supersaturation (cooling or antisolvent addition too fast, or too much local supersaturation at the addition point), which favors nucleation over growth. Agglomerates often come from poor mixing that lets locally over-concentrated zones stick particles together, or from seeding technique issues. Slower, better-controlled addition rates and adequate seeding usually improve both problems.

What's the difference between cake filtration and depth filtration, and when do I use each?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Cake filtration collects a visible layer of solids on a filter medium and is used when you want to recover a solid product (like isolating a crystallized API). Depth filtration passes liquid through a thick porous medium to capture fine particulates for clarification, typically used for polishing a solution rather than recovering solids. Choosing the wrong one either clogs quickly (using depth media for heavy solids loading) or fails to clarify properly (using cake filtration for trace particulates).

Why does drying take so much longer at production scale than in a lab oven?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Larger cake beds have longer diffusion paths for moisture or solvent to escape, and heat transfer to the center of a large cake or through a jacketed dryer wall is slower per unit mass than in a thin lab tray. Poor airflow or vacuum distribution across a larger surface area compounds the problem. Bed depth and agitation (if using an agitated dryer) are usually the first things to check when drying is slower than expected. These are the usual suspects, but drying behavior is often product-specific — verify with actual scale-down or pilot trials rather than assuming a cause without checking.

What's the right way to clean a reactor between batches of different products?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Use a validated cleaning procedure appropriate to the residue's solubility (solvent rinse, caustic wash, or dedicated cleaning agent), verify cleanliness with a rinse sample or swab test where cross-contamination matters, and pay special attention to dead legs, gaskets, and agitator shafts where residue can hide. For potent or highly reactive residues, a documented cleaning validation is essential, not just a visual check.

Why does my centrifugal pump lose prime or stop pumping?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

The most common causes are air entrainment in the suction line, a suction lift that exceeds the pump's NPSH capability, a leaking suction-side fitting pulling in air, or the liquid flashing to vapor at the impeller eye (cavitation) because the available NPSH is too low. Check for suction-side air leaks first, then verify the liquid level and line sizing give enough NPSH margin.

What causes a rotary (gear or lobe) pump to stop delivering flow?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

Common causes include excessive clearance from wear letting liquid slip back internally, a relief valve stuck open or set too low, entrained air or vapor in the pumped fluid, or the fluid viscosity being outside the pump's design range. Rotary pumps depend on close internal clearances, so wear is a very common root cause of gradually declining output.

How do I size a pump correctly for a given application?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

Determine the required flow rate and total dynamic head (static lift plus friction losses plus any pressure the discharge needs to overcome), then check the pump curve at that operating point for adequate efficiency and to confirm available NPSH exceeds the system's required NPSH with margin. Undersizing head is the most common sizing mistake — friction losses in real piping, fittings, and valves are often underestimated. Treat this as a starting point, not a final spec — always verify against the actual pump curve and system curve for your specific installation before committing to an order.

What's the difference between a diaphragm pump and a peristaltic pump, and when should I use each?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

A diaphragm pump uses a flexing membrane and check valves to move fluid and can handle higher pressures and a wide range of chemical compatibility depending on diaphragm material; a peristaltic pump squeezes fluid through flexible tubing with rollers, keeping the fluid completely isolated from any pump internals, which is ideal for sterile, shear-sensitive, or highly corrosive fluids at lower pressures and flow rates. Choose peristaltic when you need to avoid any fluid contact with pump internals, and diaphragm when you need higher pressure or flow capability.

Why won't my diaphragm pump prime?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

Common causes are a worn or torn diaphragm, a stuck or fouled check/ball valve, air trapped on the suction side that the pump can't purge, or a suction lift beyond the pump's dry-priming capability. Diaphragm pumps rely on their check valves sealing properly to build suction — a valve held open by debris is one of the most common culprits.

What causes excessive pressure drop in a transfer line?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

Undersized pipe diameter for the flow rate, too many fittings and elbows (each adds equivalent length), a partially closed or fouled valve, or a highly viscous fluid all increase pressure drop. Pressure drop increases roughly with the square of velocity, so a line sized even slightly too small can cause a disproportionate increase in required pump head.

How do I select the right gasket material for a flange?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

Match the gasket material's chemical compatibility to the process fluid first (this eliminates most options quickly), then confirm it can handle the operating temperature and pressure, and consider whether the flange faces are smooth or serrated, which affects which gasket types will seal properly. When in doubt, a material compatibility chart cross-referencing the specific chemical and elastomer or fluoropolymer is the right first check, not just picking a common general-purpose gasket. This is a general starting point only — always verify against the specific gasket manufacturer's chemical compatibility data at your actual operating temperature and concentration before final selection.

Why does increasing fluid viscosity affect pump performance so much?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

Higher viscosity increases friction losses throughout the system (reducing achievable flow for a given pump head), reduces centrifugal pump efficiency and developed head, and can push net positive suction head requirements higher. Positive displacement pumps (like rotary or diaphragm) generally handle viscosity changes much better than centrifugal pumps, which is why viscous or shear-sensitive fluids are often better matched to PD pump types.

How do I pick the right size O-ring to fix a leaking seal?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

Measure the groove's inner diameter and cross-section width (or measure the failed O-ring itself if it hasn't distorted too much) and match against a standard O-ring size chart (AS568 dash sizes are the common reference). Always confirm the elastomer material is chemically compatible with the process fluid, not just that the dimensions match — a correctly-sized but incompatible O-ring will still fail.

How do I estimate how much liquid a storage tank or drum actually holds?

Liquid Handling & Fluid Systems
▸

For a vertical cylindrical vessel, volume is simply cross-sectional area times liquid height, but always subtract for any dished or sloped bottom, and never plan to fill to 100% of nominal capacity — leave adequate freeboard for thermal expansion and to prevent overflow during filling. Standard drum and tank capacity references (by diameter and standard sizes) are a faster check than calculating from scratch for common container sizes.

Why does my reactor take longer to heat up or cool down as batch size increases?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

Heat transfer area (jacket surface) scales with the square of a vessel's linear dimension, while volume scales with the cube — so larger vessels have proportionally less jacket area per unit volume, which directly increases heating and cooling times. This is one of the most predictable and unavoidable effects of scale-up, and it's why plant-scale temperature ramps are almost always slower than lab-scale ones.

How do I estimate the heat transfer coefficient of a jacketed reactor?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

The overall coefficient combines the inside film coefficient (driven by agitation and fluid properties), the wall and jacket-side resistances, and any fouling. In practice, it's most reliable to determine it experimentally by measuring the actual heating or cooling rate of a known mass and comparing to the temperature driving force, rather than relying solely on theoretical correlations, since fouling and real agitation conditions vary significantly from vessel to vessel.

What is LMTD and why does it matter for heat exchanger sizing?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

Log mean temperature difference is the properly-averaged temperature driving force between two fluids exchanging heat when that difference changes along the length of the exchanger (as it does in any real heat exchanger). Using a simple arithmetic average instead of LMTD will give an inaccurate (usually optimistic) estimate of the required heat transfer area, especially when the temperature difference varies a lot between the inlet and outlet ends.

Why won't my temperature control unit (TCU) reach its setpoint?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

Check first whether the heat transfer fluid flow rate through the jacket is adequate (a partially closed valve or air-locked line will starve heat transfer), whether the TCU's own heating or cooling capacity is actually sufficient for the vessel size and duty, and whether the fluid has degraded or lost its heat transfer properties over time. An undersized TCU relative to the vessel's heat duty is a very common root cause, especially after moving a process to a larger reactor.

How do I choose the right heat transfer fluid for my application?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

Match the fluid's useful temperature range to your process's full operating range (including any planned excursions), check viscosity at the coldest temperature you'll see (high viscosity kills circulation and heat transfer), and consider flammability and toxicity if a leak into the process is a credible scenario. Glycols are common for moderate temperature ranges; specialized synthetic heat transfer fluids or silicone oils are used for wider or more extreme ranges.

What's the difference between using a glycol solution and using a brine for cooling?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

Glycol/water mixtures are generally less corrosive and easier to handle but have a practical low-temperature limit around -30 to -40°C depending on concentration; brines (like calcium chloride solutions) can reach lower temperatures but are more corrosive to many common materials. Choose based on the coldest temperature you actually need and the material compatibility of your cooling loop.

Why does my process chiller lose cooling capacity in hot weather?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

Chillers reject heat to ambient air or cooling water, and their capacity drops as the temperature difference between the condenser and ambient shrinks — a chiller rated for a cold winter day can lose a significant fraction of its rated capacity on a hot summer day. Always check a chiller's capacity curve at the worst-case (highest) ambient or cooling water temperature you expect, not just the nominal rating.

How does cooling water inlet temperature affect condenser performance?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

Higher cooling water inlet temperature reduces the available temperature driving force across the condenser, directly reducing its heat rejection capacity — this can show up as poor vacuum distillation performance or incomplete condensation on hot summer days even when nothing else in the system has changed. If condenser performance is seasonal, cooling water temperature is usually the first thing to check.

How do I estimate the steam pressure needed to reach a given process temperature?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

Saturated steam temperature and pressure are directly linked (steam tables or a pressure-temperature chart give this relationship), so you size steam pressure based on the saturation temperature needed, plus a margin to overcome the added resistance of the heat exchanger or jacket wall. Remember you need the steam temperature to exceed your target process temperature by enough margin to actually drive heat transfer at a reasonable rate.

What causes heat transfer performance in a jacketed vessel to degrade over time?

Heat Transfer & Utilities
▸

Fouling is the most common cause — scale buildup, polymer residue, or corrosion products accumulating on the heat transfer surface act as an insulating layer that reduces the effective heat transfer coefficient. If heating or cooling times gradually get worse over many batches despite nothing else changing, fouling and the need for a cleaning cycle should be the first suspicion.

What's the difference between a single-phase and a three-phase motor?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

A single-phase motor runs on standard single-phase power (common in smaller equipment and residential-style circuits) and generally has lower efficiency and higher starting current relative to its size; a three-phase motor uses three alternating currents offset in phase, giving smoother torque, higher efficiency, and easier starting, which is why almost all significant industrial motors (pumps, agitators, compressors) are three-phase.

What's an intrinsically safe circuit and when do I need one?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

An intrinsically safe circuit is designed so it can never release enough electrical or thermal energy — even under fault conditions — to ignite a specified flammable atmosphere. You need intrinsically safe instrumentation and wiring in any area classified as hazardous due to flammable vapors, gases, or dusts, such as around solvent transfer points or inside a vessel headspace containing flammable vapor. Hazardous area classification and intrinsic safety requirements are governed by local electrical codes and should always be confirmed by a qualified electrical/process safety engineer for your specific site — this is background context, not a substitute for that review.

What does a Class 1, Division 1 rating mean for electrical equipment?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

It designates an area (Class 1) where flammable gases or vapors may be present, and where that flammable atmosphere is expected to exist under normal operating conditions (Division 1, as opposed to Division 2, where it would only be present under abnormal conditions like a leak or spill). Electrical equipment installed in a Division 1 area must be specifically rated for that classification — standard general-purpose equipment is not acceptable. Area classification for a specific plant or process must be formally determined by a qualified engineer following applicable codes (such as NEC or IEC standards) — this explains the concept, not a substitute for that formal classification.

Why is my electric motor drawing more current than its nameplate rating?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

Common causes are the motor being mechanically overloaded (driving a pump or agitator against more resistance than designed for), a bearing or alignment problem creating extra friction, low supply voltage forcing higher current to deliver the same power, or a developing winding fault. Sustained overcurrent will overheat the motor and shorten its life even if it doesn't trip immediately.

What's the difference between a thermocouple and an RTD?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

A thermocouple generates a small voltage from the junction of two dissimilar metals and works over a very wide temperature range with a fast response, but is less accurate; an RTD (resistance temperature detector) measures temperature via the predictable change in a metal's electrical resistance and is more accurate and stable but generally has a narrower range and slower response. For precise process control at moderate temperatures, RTDs are usually preferred; thermocouples are chosen for very high temperatures or fast transient measurements.

How does a 4-20 mA transmitter work, and why is it used?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

A 4-20 mA transmitter converts a sensor's measurement (temperature, pressure, level, etc.) into a proportional current signal between 4 and 20 milliamps, where 4 mA represents the low end of the measurement range and 20 mA the high end. Using a live zero of 4 mA (rather than 0 mA) means a broken wire or dead transmitter reads as 0 mA, which is easily distinguished from a genuine low reading — this is why it's the standard for reliable process instrumentation.

Why does my pH probe give inconsistent or drifting readings?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

Common causes include a fouled or coated glass membrane reducing responsiveness, a depleted or contaminated reference electrolyte, temperature effects not being compensated for, or the probe simply being past its calibration interval or end of life. Regular calibration against fresh buffer solutions and periodic cleaning are the standard first troubleshooting steps before assuming a probe needs replacement.

What causes an electric motor to overheat in continuous duty?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

Insufficient cooling airflow (blocked vents, wrong orientation), running at higher than rated load for the duty cycle, high ambient temperature, or voltage imbalance across phases in a three-phase motor can all cause overheating. Motors have a rated duty cycle and service factor — running continuously beyond what the nameplate specifies will shorten insulation life even without an obvious immediate fault.

How do I tune a PID controller that's oscillating around setpoint?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

Oscillation usually means the proportional gain is too high or the integral time is too short (too aggressive) for the process's actual response speed. Reduce the proportional gain first and observe, then adjust integral time — a systematic tuning method (like Ziegler-Nichols or trial-and-error step response testing) is more reliable than guessing individual parameters in isolation. Treat this as a general troubleshooting starting point — tuning approach and safe parameter ranges should be confirmed against your specific control system and process dynamics.

How do I size wire gauge correctly for an electrical circuit?

Electrical & Instrumentation
▸

Wire gauge needs to be sized for the expected current draw (including motor starting current, which can be several times the running current) with an adequate safety margin, and also checked against voltage drop over the run length — a wire that's technically rated for the current can still cause unacceptable voltage drop over a long run. Local electrical code tables for ampacity by wire gauge and insulation type are the standard reference, not a rule of thumb.

Is it more efficient to run a distillation at atmospheric pressure or under vacuum?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

Vacuum distillation lowers the boiling point of the material, which is essential for heat-sensitive compounds that would decompose at their atmospheric boiling point, and it can also improve separation for close-boiling or high-boiling mixtures. However, vacuum systems add complexity (leak-tightness, vacuum pump capacity) and can reduce relative volatility for some systems — the right choice depends on the specific compound's thermal stability and the separation being attempted, not a universal rule.

What happens to azeotrope composition under vacuum?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

Azeotrope composition is generally pressure-dependent and often shifts (and can sometimes disappear entirely) as pressure is reduced, because the relative volatilities of the components change differently with temperature. This means a mixture that's difficult to separate by simple distillation at atmospheric pressure may separate more easily under vacuum, or vice versa — it needs to be checked for the specific system rather than assumed.

What are good solvents for azeotropic removal of water from a reaction mixture?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

Solvents that form a low-boiling, water-immiscible (or partially immiscible) azeotrope are ideal, since the water separates cleanly in a Dean-Stark type trap after condensation — toluene and other aromatic or moderately non-polar solvents are classic choices for this reason. The solvent needs to be compatible with the reaction chemistry itself, so the choice always has to balance azeotropic behavior against chemical compatibility.

How do I choose a good solvent for crystallization?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

Look for a solvent (or solvent pair) where the target compound is quite soluble hot and much less soluble cold, giving a large usable window for cooling crystallization, while impurities remain soluble throughout. Solubility curves, and sometimes an antisolvent addition strategy, are the standard tools for screening candidate solvents rather than guessing from general polarity rules alone. Use this as a starting point for screening candidates, not a final answer — confirm with actual solubility data and small-scale trials for your specific compound before committing to a solvent at scale.

What does the NFPA flammability classification tell me about a solvent?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

NFPA flammability ratings (typically 0 to 4) reflect how readily a material will burn based on its flash point and boiling point, with higher numbers meaning a more severe fire hazard. This classification directly drives decisions about storage, electrical area classification, and handling precautions — a solvent with a low flash point needs much more stringent controls than one with a high flash point even if both are technically 'flammable.'

Why does solvent viscosity matter for distillation efficiency?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

Higher viscosity reduces mass transfer rates on distillation trays or packing, which can reduce the effective number of theoretical stages achieved and slow down the whole operation. It also affects reboiler and condenser heat transfer coefficients, so a viscous solvent mixture may need a longer distillation time or reduced throughput compared to a low-viscosity system with the same nominal separation.

What's the difference between a binary and a ternary azeotrope?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

A binary azeotrope involves two components that boil at a constant composition and temperature (neither simple distillation nor further heating changes the vapor composition), while a ternary azeotrope involves three components behaving the same way. Ternary azeotropes are especially relevant when a third component (often water) is present alongside the solvent pair you're trying to separate, and they can make an otherwise straightforward-looking distillation surprisingly difficult.

How do I estimate a solvent's boiling point at reduced pressure?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

The Clausius-Clapeyron relationship (or a nomograph / boiling point vs. pressure chart) lets you estimate how boiling point drops as pressure is reduced, using a known reference boiling point at one pressure. This is essential for planning a vacuum distillation of a heat-sensitive compound — you need to know what vacuum level will actually bring the boiling point below the compound's decomposition temperature.

Are there solvents that are restricted or discouraged for pharmaceutical use?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

Yes — regulatory guidance (such as ICH Q3C) classifies residual solvents into classes based on toxicity, with Class 1 solvents (like benzene) to be avoided entirely in pharmaceutical manufacturing unless justified, Class 2 solvents limited to specific concentration thresholds in the final product, and Class 3 solvents considered lower risk. Solvent selection for any pharmaceutical process needs to check the current regulatory solvent class, not just general chemical suitability.

What causes poor water removal during an azeotropic drying distillation?

Solvents & Distillation
▸

Incomplete phase separation in the Dean-Stark trap (often due to emulsification, especially if surfactant-like impurities are present), insufficient reflux, or simply not running the distillation long enough for equilibrium to be reached are the most common causes. If water removal stalls, check whether the two liquid phases are actually separating cleanly in the trap rather than assuming the chemistry is the problem.

What special precautions are needed when handling gaseous hydrogen?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

Hydrogen has an extremely wide flammability range and very low ignition energy, so leak-tight connections, adequate ventilation, elimination of ignition sources, and hydrogen-specific leak detection are all essential. Because hydrogen is also lighter than air and can accumulate at ceiling level, ventilation and detection strategies need to account for that, unlike heavier gases that pool near the floor.

How do I estimate how much gas is left in a compressed cylinder?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

For a gas that remains fully gaseous in the cylinder (like nitrogen or compressed air), remaining contents are roughly proportional to the pressure gauge reading relative to a full cylinder's pressure. For liquefied gases (like propane or CO2), pressure stays roughly constant until nearly empty, so pressure alone won't tell you how much is left — you need to weigh the cylinder and compare to its tare and full weights instead.

Why do gas cylinder valve connections differ between gases?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

CGA (Compressed Gas Association) standard outlet connections are deliberately gas-specific to prevent accidentally connecting incompatible gases to the wrong regulator or equipment — for example, preventing a flammable gas regulator from being connected to an oxidizer cylinder. Never force an adapter to make an incompatible connection work; the mismatch is a safety feature, not an inconvenience.

What's the safe way to warm up a gas cylinder in cold weather?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

Use only warm water or a rated cylinder heating jacket/blanket designed for the purpose — never use an open flame, electric heating element in direct contact, or any localized high-heat source, since uneven or excessive heating can dangerously raise internal pressure or, for some gases, create decomposition hazards. Gradual, even warming is the key principle.

How do I detect a gas leak safely?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

Use a leak detection solution (soap solution) brushed on fittings and connections and watch for bubbles, or a gas-specific electronic leak detector for more sensitive or continuous monitoring — never use a flame to check for a gas leak. For flammable or toxic gases, always confirm the area is adequately ventilated and consider fixed gas detection for anywhere a leak could accumulate.

What causes a vacuum system to fail to reach the desired vacuum level?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

Common causes include air leaks anywhere in the system (fittings, gaskets, valve stems), a vacuum pump that's undersized or has degraded internally (worn seals, contaminated oil in an oil-sealed pump), or condensable vapors overwhelming the pump's capacity. Systematically checking for leaks (isolating sections and watching for pressure rise) is usually more productive than assuming the pump itself has failed.

How do I estimate the compressor horsepower needed for a compressed air system?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

Required horsepower depends on the target discharge pressure, the flow rate (typically in standard cubic feet per minute) needed by your end uses, and the compressor's efficiency — reference tables relating horsepower to CFM at common pressure ratings are the standard starting point, then size up for future capacity needs since compressed air demand tends to grow.

What's the relationship between dew point and relative humidity?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

Dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated and water begins to condense, while relative humidity is the percentage of saturation at the current temperature — the same absolute moisture content gives a fixed dew point but a relative humidity that changes with temperature. For compressed air systems, dew point (not relative humidity) is the meaningful spec, since it tells you the actual temperature at which condensation will start to form in your lines.

Why do cryogenic liquids require special handling precautions?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

Cryogenic liquids can cause severe cold burns on contact with skin, generate huge volumes of gas as they warm (creating asphyxiation or overpressure risk in enclosed spaces), and can make many common materials brittle at their temperature. Proper PPE (face shield, insulated gloves), adequate ventilation, and using only compatible, pressure-rated containers and equipment are all essential.

What causes a gas pressure regulator to fail or behave erratically?

Compressed Gases & Vacuum Systems
▸

Worn seat seals allowing creeping outlet pressure over time, contamination or debris lodged in the seat, or a diaphragm that's degraded from age or chemical exposure are the most common causes. Regulators are precision devices — creeping or erratic downstream pressure is a strong sign it needs to be serviced or replaced rather than adjusted further.

How do I safely prepare a dilute acid or base solution?

Chemical Properties & Data
▸

Always add concentrated acid to water, never water to concentrated acid, since the heat of dilution can cause violent spattering if water is added to a concentrated acid. Add slowly with stirring and cooling if the dilution is significantly exothermic, and always know the target concentration and heat of dilution before starting, especially for strong acids like sulfuric acid.

What's the difference between pKa and pH?

Chemical Properties & Data
▸

pH describes the actual hydrogen ion concentration of a specific solution at a given moment, while pKa is an intrinsic property of a particular acid or base that describes its inherent strength — specifically, the pH at which that acid is exactly half dissociated. Knowing a compound's pKa tells you how its ionization (and often its solubility and extraction behavior) will change as you adjust the pH of a solution it's dissolved in.

How does temperature affect the aqueous solubility of a compound?

Chemical Properties & Data
▸

For most solid solutes, solubility in water increases with temperature, though the relationship isn't always linear and some compounds show unusual or even inverse behavior. This temperature dependence is exactly what makes cooling crystallization possible — dissolving a compound hot and cooling to force it back out of solution as crystals.

What is heat of solution and why does it matter?

Chemical Properties & Data
▸

Heat of solution is the heat absorbed or released when a substance dissolves, and it can be significant enough to noticeably heat or cool a solution during dissolution — some salts dissolving endothermically enough to freeze condensation on the outside of a vessel, others releasing enough heat to require cooling. Always check heat of solution before dissolving a large quantity of an unfamiliar material, especially at production scale where the total heat effect is large.

How do I choose the right buffer system for a target pH?

Chemical Properties & Data
▸

Pick a buffer whose pKa is close to (ideally within about one pH unit of) your target pH, since buffering capacity is strongest near the pKa; also consider whether the buffer components could interfere with your specific chemistry (phosphate buffers, for instance, can be problematic in systems sensitive to phosphate ions). A buffer selected far from its pKa will provide little real buffering capacity even at reasonable concentration.

What's the difference between molarity, molality, and normality?

Chemical Properties & Data
▸

Molarity is moles of solute per liter of solution; molality is moles of solute per kilogram of solvent (independent of temperature-driven volume changes, unlike molarity); normality accounts for the reactive capacity of a species (like equivalents of acid or base) rather than just the number of molecules, which matters for acids or bases that can donate more than one proton. Normality and molarity are only the same for simple, singly-reactive species.

What are common drying agents used for organic solvents, and how do I choose one?

Chemical Properties & Data
▸

Common choices include anhydrous magnesium sulfate and sodium sulfate for general-purpose drying, calcium chloride for some hydrocarbons (but incompatible with alcohols and amines), and molecular sieves for very thorough drying to low water content. Choice depends on compatibility with the specific solvent, the degree of dryness required, and how much drying capacity per unit weight is needed for practical use.

Why can a compound's solubility change so dramatically with a small temperature change?

Chemical Properties & Data
▸

Some compounds have very steep solubility-versus-temperature curves near a particular temperature range, meaning a small temperature shift causes a large solubility change — this is actually desirable for crystallization yield but can also cause unexpected premature crystallization (or dissolution) if temperature isn't well controlled during processing. Checking the actual solubility curve, not just endpoints, avoids surprises in the steep region.

What should a chemical hygiene plan include?

Safety & Materials
▸

It should cover hazard identification for the chemicals in use, required personal protective equipment and engineering controls (fume hoods, ventilation), emergency procedures for spills and exposures, employee training requirements, and designated responsibilities for oversight. It needs to be a living document that's actually followed and updated as new chemicals or processes are introduced, not just a filed compliance document.

How do I identify incompatible chemicals before storing them together?

Safety & Materials
▸

Check each material's safety data sheet for incompatibility warnings, and consult a chemical incompatibility reference chart for broad categories (oxidizers with flammables, acids with bases or cyanides, water-reactive materials with aqueous solutions, etc.). Segregated storage by hazard class, not just alphabetical or space-convenient storage, is the standard practice for avoiding dangerous unplanned reactions.

What class of fire extinguisher should I use for a solvent fire?

Safety & Materials
▸

Flammable liquid fires are Class B fires, which need an extinguisher rated for Class B (commonly dry chemical or CO2 types) — never use water on a flammable liquid fire, since it can spread burning liquid or, for water-reactive materials, worsen the situation. Always know the extinguisher ratings available in your area before you need them, not during an emergency.

How do I choose the right glove material for a specific chemical?

Safety & Materials
▸

Consult a glove chemical resistance/permeation chart cross-referencing the specific chemical against candidate glove materials (nitrile, neoprene, butyl rubber, etc.) — resistance varies enormously between materials for the same chemical, and breakthrough time (not just whether a material is 'resistant') matters for how long you can safely work with a given glove. A glove that resists brief splash contact may still fail with sustained immersion.

When is respiratory protection required versus just relying on ventilation?

Safety & Materials
▸

Engineering controls like fume hoods and local exhaust ventilation should be the first line of defense; respiratory protection is needed when those controls can't keep airborne concentrations below exposure limits, such as during certain maintenance tasks, spill response, or when handling powders with high inherent toxicity. Respirator use also requires fit testing and a formal respiratory protection program, not just handing out a mask.

What information should I always check on an MSDS/SDS before starting a new process?

Safety & Materials
▸

At minimum: flammability and reactivity hazards, incompatible materials, required PPE, exposure limits, and first aid/emergency procedures. For process development specifically, also check thermal stability data if available, since an SDS's hazard information is directly relevant to safety screening before scaling up a new reaction.

What's the right way to label a chemical container for hazard communication?

Safety & Materials
▸

Labels should identify the chemical (or mixture) by name, show the appropriate hazard pictograms and signal word, and include key hazard and precautionary statements, following the GHS (Globally Harmonized System) format used in modern hazard communication standards. Secondary containers (like a beaker of transferred material) still need this information, not just the original manufacturer's container.

How do I know if a gasket or elastomer is compatible with my process fluid?

Safety & Materials
▸

Check a material compatibility table cross-referencing the specific chemical against candidate elastomer and plastic materials — compatibility can vary from excellent to complete failure between chemically similar-looking materials (like different fluoropolymers), so specific data beats general assumptions. Also verify compatibility at your actual operating temperature, since resistance that's fine at room temperature can fail at elevated process temperatures. Compatibility charts are a screening tool, not a guarantee — always confirm with the specific manufacturer's data at your actual operating temperature and concentration, and consider a compatibility trial for critical applications.

What causes stainless steel to corrode in a process that's supposedly compatible?

Safety & Materials
▸

Localized attack (pitting or crevice corrosion) can occur even when general corrosion resistance looks fine on paper, especially in the presence of chlorides, at crevices or under deposits, or where welds haven't been properly passivated. Stainless steel's corrosion resistance depends on an intact passive oxide layer — anything that disrupts that layer locally (mechanical damage, chloride pitting, weld heat-affected zones) can cause corrosion even in an otherwise 'compatible' environment.

Why does borosilicate glass sometimes get attacked by certain chemicals?

Safety & Materials
▸

Strong bases (like concentrated sodium hydroxide) and hydrofluoric acid are the classic chemicals known to attack borosilicate glass, dissolving the silica network over time — this is different from most acids, which glass generally resists well. Extended exposure, elevated temperature, and concentration all accelerate glass attack, so a brief exposure that seems fine can still cause long-term degradation with repeated use.

What's the practical difference between 316 stainless steel and Hastelloy C-276 for corrosion resistance?

Safety & Materials
▸

316 stainless steel offers good general corrosion resistance and is much less expensive, making it the default choice for many processes, but it's vulnerable to chloride-induced pitting and stress corrosion cracking; Hastelloy C-276 is a nickel-based alloy with substantially better resistance to a wide range of aggressive chemicals, including many chloride-containing and oxidizing environments, at a significantly higher cost. Hastelloy is typically reserved for specific process conditions where 316 stainless is known to fail. These are general tendencies — always check corrosion data or run a coupon test for your specific process chemistry and concentration before finalizing a material choice.

How do I check material compatibility before running a new process in existing equipment?

Safety & Materials
▸

Cross-reference every wetted material in the equipment train (gaskets, seals, vessel material, pump internals) against the new process fluid's chemical compatibility, not just the vessel material itself — a single incompatible gasket or seal can fail even if the main vessel material is perfectly suitable. This is especially important when repurposing existing equipment for a new, previously-unused chemical.

What is galvanic corrosion and how do I avoid it in mixed-metal systems?

Safety & Materials
▸

Galvanic corrosion happens when two dissimilar metals are in electrical contact in the presence of an electrolyte (like moisture or a conductive process fluid), causing the more active (anodic) metal to corrode faster than it would alone. Avoid it by using compatible metal combinations close together on the galvanic series, or by electrically isolating dissimilar metals (insulating gaskets or fittings) where mixed-metal contact is unavoidable.

How do I convert between different pressure units like psi, bar, and mmHg?

Reference & Conversions
▸

The key conversions to remember are roughly 1 bar = 14.5 psi = 750 mmHg, and 1 atmosphere = 14.7 psi = 1.013 bar = 760 mmHg. For vacuum work, remember that mmHg (torr) figures are often quoted as either absolute pressure or as vacuum below atmospheric, so always confirm which convention a given reading or specification is using before converting.

What's a good cold bath mixture for reaching very low laboratory temperatures?

Reference & Conversions
▸

Ice/salt mixtures can reach roughly -20°C, dry ice with an organic solvent like acetone or isopropanol reaches about -78°C, and liquid nitrogen alone or with a slush-forming solvent can reach down to nearly -196°C. Choose the mildest bath that reaches your actual target temperature — going colder than necessary just wastes dry ice or liquid nitrogen and adds handling risk.

What do standard sieve sizes correspond to in particle size (microns)?

Reference & Conversions
▸

Standard sieve series (like U.S. mesh or Tyler mesh) relate a mesh number to an opening size in microns or inches, with higher mesh numbers meaning smaller openings — for example, 100 mesh is roughly 150 microns, while 325 mesh is roughly 44 microns. Always check a proper sieve conversion table for the specific standard in use, since different sieve series (U.S., Tyler, ISO) don't use identical numbering.

How do I choose between glass-lined, Hastelloy, and stainless steel for a reactor?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

The choice comes down to chemical compatibility with your specific reaction mass: glass-lined reactors handle a very wide range of acidic and corrosive chemistries well and are the default for many pharma processes, Hastelloy is chosen when the process involves conditions (like certain acidic or oxidizing environments, or where glass could be damaged by fluoride-containing reagents) that would attack glass, and standard stainless steel is typically reserved for more basic or less corrosive reaction masses where the added cost of Hastelloy isn't justified. Always confirm compatibility against the specific reagents and by-products at process temperature, not just the primary solvent. These are general tendencies, not guarantees — always confirm compatibility against the specific reagents, concentrations, and temperatures of your actual process, ideally with vendor data or a corrosion trial, before finalizing material selection.

How do I decide what type of agitator to use for a given reaction?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Start from three questions: is the mass homogeneous or heterogeneous (solids present), what's the approximate viscosity, and is the mixture acidic or basic (which affects vessel material and sometimes agitator choice indirectly). Low-viscosity homogeneous systems generally do well with pitched-blade turbines, flat-blade, or disk-blade impellers; medium-to-high viscosity systems benefit from anchor or close-clearance impellers that sweep the vessel wall; and gas-liquid-solid systems (like hydrogenations) typically need a gas-induction turbine or specially designed impeller to draw gas into the liquid.

What's the difference between geometric, kinematic, and dynamic similarity in scale-up?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Geometric similarity means all the linear dimensions of the vessel and impeller (diameter ratios, baffle width, liquid height) are scaled by the same ratio between the small and large vessel. Kinematic similarity adds the requirement that characteristic velocities scale proportionally, and dynamic similarity further requires that the relevant forces (like inertial versus viscous forces) scale proportionally too. In practice, full dynamic similarity is rarely achievable when more than two force effects matter, so real scale-up work usually picks the single scaling criterion (like constant power per volume or constant tip speed) that matters most for the specific process.

What does 'constant power per unit volume' mean as a scale-up criterion, and when should I use it?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

It means keeping the energy dissipation rate (power input divided by batch volume, often called epsilon) the same at both scales, which is a common approach for reactions where mass transfer or turbulence intensity controls performance. It's most appropriate for processes like liquid-liquid dispersions or gas-liquid mass transfer where turbulent energy dissipation directly governs how well phases are mixed or dispersed, rather than for reactions primarily controlled by bulk blend time.

When should I scale up using constant tip speed instead of constant power per volume?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Tip speed (the impeller's tangential velocity at its outer diameter) is the right scale-up parameter when maximum shear rate is the critical factor — for example, when a reaction needs high local shear to rapidly disperse an added reagent, or conversely when a precipitated solid is shear-sensitive and excessive tip speed would cause particle attrition or fines. If shear-sensitive solids or shear-driven dispersion are central to your process outcome, tip speed usually matters more than bulk power per volume.

What is blend time and why does it matter for scale-up?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Blend time is how long it takes the contents of a vessel to become homogeneous (commonly measured to a target like 95% uniformity), and it's especially important for semi-batch reactions where a reagent is added gradually and needs to disperse before it can react locally in an uncontrolled way. Blend time depends strongly on impeller type and vessel geometry — impellers designed mainly for wall-scraping (like anchors) tend to have much longer blend times than turbine-style impellers optimized for bulk circulation.

How do I determine whether mixing intensity actually matters for my semi-batch reaction?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

Run a set of small-scale experiments varying stirrer speed (and separately, addition rate) while holding everything else constant, then compare the reaction outcome (yield, selectivity, impurity profile). If varying stirrer speed changes the outcome, mixing is important and needs careful scale-up attention; if it doesn't, the reaction is likely kinetically controlled rather than mixing controlled, which gives more flexibility during scale-up.

What's different about scaling up gas-liquid-solid reactions like hydrogenations?

Scale-Up & Process Development
▸

These reactions need effective gas dispersion into the liquid in addition to normal liquid blending, so agitator selection typically shifts toward gas-induction turbines or specially designed impellers that actively draw headspace gas down into the liquid rather than standard turbines that only handle liquid-phase mixing well. Mass transfer of the gas into solution is often the rate-limiting step, so scale-up criteria need to prioritize gas-liquid mass transfer coefficient rather than just bulk blend time.

What's the advantage of an agitated Nutsche filter-dryer over a standard filter?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

An agitated Nutsche filter-dryer combines filtration, washing, and drying in the same vessel, which avoids transferring a wet cake to a separate dryer and reduces product handling, exposure, and potential for cross-contamination. It's especially valuable for potent or air-sensitive compounds where minimizing transfers and open handling steps is a priority, even though cycle times per batch can be longer than using dedicated, separate filtration and drying equipment.

When would I choose a pressure/vacuum Nutsche filter over an agitated one?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

A non-agitated pressure or vacuum Nutsche filter is a simpler, often lower-cost option when the cake doesn't need in-vessel mixing or discharge assistance — typically suitable for free-filtering solids that form a cake easily and don't require agitation to wash or dry effectively. If the cake tends to crack, channel, or dry unevenly without help, or if in-situ drying is needed, the agitated version becomes the better choice despite the added complexity.

When should I use a centrifuge instead of a Nutsche filter for isolation?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Centrifuges are generally faster for free-filtering solids and produce a lower-moisture cake through mechanical force rather than relying purely on pressure or vacuum differential, making them attractive for high-volume or time-sensitive processes. However, centrifuges can subject crystals to higher mechanical stress (risking attrition or fines) and are less suited to fine, slow-filtering, or fragile particles compared to a gentler Nutsche filtration.

What's a plate and frame filter press best suited for?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Plate and frame filter presses are well suited for clarifying filtration (removing fine particulates or polishing a solution) rather than recovering a bulk solid product, and they can handle relatively high solids loading in a compact footprint. They're less convenient than a Nutsche filter when the actual goal is isolating and washing a crystalline product cake, since cake discharge and washing are more labor-intensive.

How do I decide which filtration equipment to use for a new process?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Work through the properties of the actual solid: how fast does it filter on a small scale (a quick Buchner funnel test gives a useful first indication), how fragile are the crystals, do you need in-situ drying, and what throughput do you need. Fast-filtering, robust crystals with a need for washing and drying favor an agitated Nutsche; very fast-filtering robust solids at high throughput favor a centrifuge; and simple clarification duties favor a filter press. A quick lab-scale filtration trial is almost always more informative than trying to select equipment from theory alone.

Why is a small-scale filtration trial (like a Buchner funnel test) useful before selecting plant filtration equipment?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

A simple lab filtration trial gives you a first estimate of specific cake resistance, filterability, and how the cake behaves during washing (cracking, channeling), which are the properties that actually drive suitable equipment selection at plant scale. Skipping this step and assuming plant filtration will behave the same as a similar-looking process is a common cause of underperforming filtration equipment after scale-up.

What's the basic difference between vacuum tray drying and air tray drying?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

A vacuum tray dryer removes solvent under reduced pressure, which lowers the boiling point of residual solvent and allows drying at lower temperature — important for heat-sensitive or thermally unstable products — while an air tray dryer relies on heated air circulation at atmospheric pressure, which is simpler but exposes the product to higher temperature for a given solvent removal rate. Choose vacuum tray drying whenever the product's thermal stability or solvent's flash point makes atmospheric hot-air drying unsuitable.

When is a rotary cone vacuum dryer (RCVD) a good choice?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

An RCVD combines gentle tumbling agitation with vacuum drying in an enclosed, often double-cone shaped vessel, making it a good middle ground between static tray drying and a fully agitated dryer — it provides some mixing to improve drying uniformity and break up clumps without the higher shear of paddle or blade-type agitated dryers. It's commonly used where moderate agitation is beneficial but the product is somewhat shear-sensitive.

What causes uneven drying in a fluid bed dryer, and what should stay constant during scale-up?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Uneven drying is usually caused by inconsistent fluidization across the bed, often due to insufficient air velocity for the bed height or poor air distribution. During scale-up, the key challenge is that bed height typically increases at larger scale, which increases mechanical stress on particles even when you try to keep other conditions the same — you generally have to choose between keeping the fluidization velocity constant (preserving mechanical stress on particles) or keeping the air-to-product ratio constant (preserving drying time and temperature/humidity exposure), since both usually can't be held constant simultaneously. This is general guidance — the right choice between these tradeoffs is product-specific and should be confirmed experimentally rather than assumed.

How do I estimate the minimum fluidization velocity for a fluid bed dryer?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

The minimum fluidization velocity can be estimated theoretically from a force balance between gravity, buoyancy, and drag on the particles (using particle and fluid density, particle sphericity, and effective particle diameter), or determined experimentally by slowly increasing air velocity through a sample bed and noting the velocity at which the bed height stops increasing linearly and fluidization begins. The experimental method is generally more reliable for real, non-ideal particles than the theoretical correlations, which assume idealized particle shapes.

What is a spray dryer used for, and how is it different from other dryer types?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

A spray dryer atomizes a liquid solution or suspension into fine droplets within a hot gas stream, evaporating the solvent almost instantly to produce a dry powder directly from solution — unlike tray, RCVD, or fluid bed dryers, which dry an already-isolated wet solid cake. It's the right choice when you want to go directly from a solution to a powder with controlled particle size and morphology, rather than needing a separate crystallization and isolation step first.

When would I use an agitated thin film dryer (ATFD) instead of a standard batch dryer?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

An ATFD spreads material into a thin, continuously agitated film on a heated surface, giving very high heat transfer rates and short residence times, which makes it well suited to viscous, heat-sensitive, or difficult-to-dry materials (like concentrating a solution to near-dryness or handling a sticky paste) where a standard tray or agitated batch dryer would take far too long or risk product degradation. It's typically used for continuous or semi-continuous operation rather than simple batch cake drying.

What's the advantage of a Nauta (conical screw) dryer?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

A Nauta dryer uses an orbiting screw inside a conical vessel to provide gentle, thorough mixing with relatively low shear, which is useful for drying materials that need good blend uniformity without the particle attrition that higher-shear agitated dryers can cause. It's a good choice when you need reliable mixing throughout drying but the product is sensitive to mechanical stress.

How do I choose the right dryer type for a new product?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Consider the product's thermal and shear sensitivity, whether it's already isolated as a wet cake or still in solution, required throughput, and whether in-situ drying (combined with filtration) is valuable for containment. As a rough guide: shear-sensitive solids favor gentler options like tray, RCVD, or Nauta dryers; robust, fast-drying solids can tolerate fluid bed or agitated dryers; and solutions that need to go straight to powder point toward spray drying or an ATFD. Treat this as a rough starting filter, not a final decision — actual dryer selection should be confirmed with small-scale drying trials on the real material, since particle and formulation behavior can defy general guidelines.

What's the difference between an air jet mill and a multi mill?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

An air jet mill reduces particle size through high-velocity particle-particle collisions driven by compressed air or gas, producing very fine particles (often down to a few microns) without direct mechanical contact, which minimizes heat generation and contamination — ideal for potent or heat-sensitive materials needing micronization. A multi mill uses mechanical blades or hammers to impact and shear particles and is typically used for coarser size reduction where extremely fine particle size isn't required.

How do I decide which size reduction equipment to use?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

The target particle size range is the main driver: if you need very fine, narrow particle size distribution (micronization for improved dissolution or bioavailability), a jet mill is usually necessary; if a coarser, more moderate size reduction is sufficient, a multi mill or similar mechanical mill is simpler, cheaper to operate, and easier to clean. Material properties like heat sensitivity, potency (containment needs), and friability also factor into the choice.

What's the difference between supersaturation and the metastable zone in crystallization?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Supersaturation is the condition where a solution holds more dissolved solute than its equilibrium solubility at that temperature; the metastable zone is the range of supersaturation within which spontaneous (uncontrolled) nucleation doesn't occur, so crystal growth can proceed in a controlled way on existing seed crystals. If supersaturation is pushed beyond the metastable zone limit, spontaneous nucleation occurs, typically producing more, smaller crystals and less control over the final particle size and polymorph.

Why does the metastable zone width often narrow when a crystallization is scaled up?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Larger-scale vessels often have different mixing characteristics and cooling or addition rates achievable in practice, and a narrower metastable zone at scale means spontaneous nucleation can occur before intentional seeding takes place, leading to poor control over polymorph and particle size. This is why metastable zone width should be characterized experimentally as a function of agitation, cooling rate, and antisolvent addition rate before finalizing a crystallization's scale-up conditions. This is a common tendency, not a certainty for every system — metastable zone width should always be characterized experimentally for your specific compound and equipment rather than assumed from general rules.

Why is seeding important for a controlled crystallization, and what problems can occur with seed addition at scale?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Seeding provides existing crystal surface for controlled growth within the metastable zone, avoiding the loss of polymorph and particle size control that comes from spontaneous nucleation. At larger scale, some equipment makes it harder to add a small quantity of dry seed effectively (seeds may need to be added as a slurry instead), and ensuring the seed charge is evenly distributed and actually reaches and contacts the bulk solution can be more challenging than at lab scale.

How does mixing intensity affect crystallization outcomes?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Inadequate mixing can create localized zones of high supersaturation (hot spots) that trigger uncontrolled nucleation even if the bulk solution is within the metastable zone, while excessive agitation can narrow the metastable zone width or cause attrition of already-formed crystals. The right mixing intensity balances preventing local supersaturation spikes against avoiding unnecessary mechanical stress on the growing crystals — this is very product- and equipment-specific and needs to be evaluated experimentally.

Why do temperature measurements sometimes give misleading readings during a crystallization?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

A thermocouple can become coated with crystals during the batch, which insulates it from the true bulk temperature and gives false readings — this becomes a real process risk if the control system is operating in batch temperature control mode, since it may then adjust jacket temperature incorrectly and push the batch outside its intended operating range. Using jacket temperature control instead of relying solely on a batch temperature probe, and watching for coating tendency, are practical ways to avoid this issue.

What causes particle attrition during crystallization, and why does it matter?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

Attrition happens when crystals are broken apart by mechanical stress from agitation, especially for crystals that are inherently fragile or when agitation intensity or duration is higher than the crystal can tolerate. It matters because attrition shifts the particle size distribution finer, which can affect downstream filterability, drying behavior, and the final powder's flow and handling properties — so agitation parameters during crystallization often need to be a compromise between adequate mixing and minimizing particle damage.

What's involved in an energy and mass balance for sizing crystallization equipment?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

The energy balance accounts for the sensible heat needed to cool or heat the solution, the heat of crystallization released or absorbed as solid forms, and (for evaporative crystallizers) the latent heat of vaporization of any solvent removed — this determines the utility (cooling or heating) capacity needed. The mass balance uses the solubility difference between the starting and final conditions to calculate the theoretical crystallization yield, which in turn determines the vessel size needed for a target batch quantity.

Is heat of crystallization usually significant compared to other heat effects in the process?

Reactors, Mixing & Solids Processing
▸

For a cooling crystallization, the heat released by crystallization can be a meaningful fraction of the total heat that needs to be removed, though it's usually smaller than the sensible heat of cooling the bulk solution; for an evaporative crystallization, the heat of crystallization is typically negligible compared to the much larger latent heat needed to vaporize the solvent being removed. Which effect dominates depends on the specific system, so it's worth checking rather than assuming one is always negligible.
