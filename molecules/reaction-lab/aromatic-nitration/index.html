Reaction Lab · Aromatic substitution

Aromatic nitration with mixed acid

Sulfuric acid turns nitric acid into the nitronium ion, NO₂⁺, which attacks the aromatic ring. Nitration is fast and very exothermic, and the products and by-products can decompose violently, so it is one of the most hazardous common reactions. Control is all about dosing, cooling and never letting unreacted material build up.

Background and plant practice: Nitration, sulfonation and halogenation.

Gas evolvedNone normally; brown NOx if overheated
Moisture sensitivityMedium
HeatAbout 28 kcal/mol released
TypeAromatic substitution

Arene (benzene shown) + Nitric acid → Nitroarene + Water (H₂SO₄, 30–50 °C)

In the lab

  1. Charge sulfuric acid and cool to 5–10 °C.
  2. Dose nitric acid to make the mixed acid. This mixing is itself exothermic.
  3. Dose the arene slowly at 30–40 °C. It reacts as it goes in; the dosing rate is set by the cooling capacity.
  4. Hold at 40–50 °C until the arene is consumed. Brown NOx fumes mean oxidation side reactions: check temperature and acid strength.
  5. This flask is now the quench vessel with ice water; the reaction mass is run into it slowly. Once the acid is diluted, the nitroarene is denser than the water layer and settles to the bottom.
  6. Separate: diluted acid on top, crude nitroarene below. Wash the product to remove acid before any distillation.

Mechanism

  1. Sulfuric acid, the stronger acid, protonates the OH group of nitric acid.
  2. Water leaves, giving the nitronium ion NO₂⁺.
  3. A π bond of the ring attacks the nitrogen of NO₂⁺; one N=O π bond moves onto oxygen.
  4. Hydrogen sulfate removes the proton from the σ-complex and the ring becomes aromatic again.
  5. Products: the nitroarene and water. The water dilutes the acid, which is why spent acid is weaker and slower than fresh mixed acid.

What to take care of

  • Nitration is fast and strongly exothermic. Never add arene faster than the cooling can remove the heat, and stop dosing if the temperature does not rise as expected (accumulation).
  • Loss of agitation is dangerous: the arene floats on the acid and builds up, then reacts all at once when stirring restarts. Interlock the feed with the agitator.
  • Nitro compounds, especially polynitro and nitrophenols, can decompose violently at high temperature. Know the onset temperature (DSC) of the reaction mass and distillation residues.
  • Brown NOx fumes indicate oxidation; they are toxic and signal that conditions are drifting.
  • Never distil crude nitro products to dryness.

Gas evolution

No gas is formed in the main reaction (none normally; brown NOx if overheated).

By-products and how to control them

By-productCause and control
Dinitro compoundsToo much nitric acid, too strong acid or too high temperature. Use close to 1.0–1.05 eq and control temperature.
NitrophenolsOxidation side reactions. They are acidic and unstable; remove them by alkaline washing.
Isomers (ortho/meta/para)Set by the substituent; temperature and acid strength shift the ratio slightly.
Oxidation products and NOxHigh temperature or excess nitric acid. Keep conditions inside the developed window.

Moisture sensitivity: Medium

Water does not destroy the reagents, but it dilutes the mixed acid and lowers the nitronium concentration. A weaker acid reacts slowly, which lets unreacted arene build up: a hidden accumulation hazard. Check the acid strength of every batch.

Heat of reaction

About 28 kcal per mole of nitro groups introduced, plus heat from mixing the acids and diluting them in the quench. The adiabatic temperature rise of a typical batch is well above 100 °C, so cooling and dosing control are essential.

Plant tips

  • Reaction calorimetry and DSC data are a must before scale-up.
  • Use a dosing interlock on high temperature and on agitator failure.
  • Have a dump or drown-out tank sized for the batch.
