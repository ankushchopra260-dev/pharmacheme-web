Baeyer–Villiger oxidation of a ketone

A peracid adds to a ketone to give the Criegee intermediate. The group best able to stabilise positive charge then migrates from carbon to the adjacent oxygen as the weak O–O bond breaks, inserting an oxygen next to the carbonyl and giving an ester.

Background and plant practice: Oxidation reactions in plants.

Gas evolvedNone under normal operation
Moisture sensitivityLow
HeatExothermic
TypeOxidation

Ketone (acetophenone shown) + Peracetic acid → Phenyl acetate (ester) + Acetic acid (dichloromethane, 20–40 °C)

In the lab

  1. Charge the ketone in dichloromethane and cool.
  2. Dose peracetic acid solution slowly; the addition is exothermic.
  3. Stir until the ketone is consumed (HPLC or iodometric peracid check).
  4. Quench excess peracid with sodium thiosulfate or sulfite solution.
  5. Separate and wash out the acetic acid by-product.

Mechanism

  1. The peracid’s terminal oxygen attacks the ketone carbon; the ketone π bond moves onto its own oxygen, giving the Criegee intermediate.
  2. In the key step, the more electron-rich group (phenyl) migrates from carbon to the adjacent oxygen as the weak O–O bond breaks, expelling acetate and re-forming the carbonyl: this gives the ester, still bearing the proton from the peracid.
  3. The departed acetate removes the proton from the new ester oxygen, giving the neutral ester and acetic acid.
  4. Products: the ester, with oxygen now inserted between the carbonyl and the migrated group, and acetic acid.

What to take care of

  • Peracids are concentrated oxidisers; never let them contact reducing agents, metals or heat sources uncontrolled.
  • The migration step is also oxidative at the carbon; electron-rich or more substituted groups migrate faster, which sets regiochemistry on unsymmetrical ketones.
  • Quench all excess peracid before work-up or concentration; residual peracid is a runaway hazard on heating.
  • Esters can over-oxidise or hydrolyse under the acidic peracid conditions if held too long; control reaction time.

Gas evolution

No gas is formed in the main reaction (none under normal operation).

By-products and how to control them

By-productCause and control
Unreacted ketoneInsufficient peracid or too cold. Verify peracid titre before dosing.
Over-oxidation / ring-opened acidsProlonged exposure to strong peracid. Quench promptly at the target conversion.
Wrong regiochemistry esterThe less-migrating group inserted instead; check which group is more electron-rich before choosing substrate.

Moisture sensitivity: Low

The reaction tolerates some water (it is often run as an aqueous peracid solution), but excess water dilutes the peracid and slows the reaction.

Heat of reaction

Both the addition and the migration release heat; dose the peracid under cooling.

Plant tips

  • Titrate peracid strength before each batch; it decomposes on storage.
  • Keep a thiosulfate quench ready for any excess peracid before work-up.
  • Segregate peracid waste streams from reducing or combustible waste.
