Diazotisation of an aromatic amine

Nitrous acid, made from sodium nitrite and acid, turns an aromatic amine into a diazonium salt. Diazonium salts are the gateway to Sandmeyer reactions, azo dyes and many substitutions, but they are unstable: they lose nitrogen gas as they warm, and dry diazonium salts can explode.

Gas evolvedNitrogen
Moisture sensitivityNone
HeatExothermic; decomposition much more so
TypeNitrogen chemistry

Aromatic amine (aniline shown) + Sodium nitrite + HCl (2–3 eq) → Diazonium chloride + Water (water, 0–5 °C)

In the lab

  1. Charge water, hydrochloric acid and the amine: the amine hydrochloride forms a white slurry.
  2. Cool to 0–5 °C with brine in the jacket.
  3. Dose sodium nitrite solution below the surface, keeping below 5 °C. The solid dissolves as the diazonium forms.
  4. Check for a slight excess of nitrite with starch–iodide paper (it turns blue-black).
  5. Destroy the excess nitrite with sulfamic acid. This gives nitrogen gas bubbles.
  6. Use the cold diazonium solution at once in the next step. Never isolate or dry it.

Mechanism

  1. In acid, nitrous acid is protonated and loses water, giving the nitrosonium ion NO⁺.
  2. The amine nitrogen attacks the nitrogen of NO⁺.
  3. After loss of a proton, the N-nitrosamine rearranges: a proton moves from nitrogen to oxygen (through the solvent) as the N=N bond forms.
  4. The diazohydroxide is protonated on oxygen; the nitrogen lone pair pushes water out and the N≡N triple bond forms.
  5. Product: the aryl diazonium salt. Warm it, and it loses N₂: that is what the next step uses, and also why it must stay cold until then.

What to take care of

  • Keep the diazonium below 5 °C (or the limit from DSC for your compound). It decomposes as it warms, giving nitrogen gas and heat.
  • Never isolate dry diazonium salts unless they are a known stable form (e.g. tetrafluoroborate) and the hazard is studied.
  • Dose nitrite below the surface; added on top, it forms toxic brown NOx gas.
  • Use enough acid (2.5–3 eq) so free amine does not couple with the diazonium to form triazenes or azo compounds.
  • Remove excess nitrite before the next step; it causes side reactions and NOx.

Gas evolution

Per 100 kg of aromatic amine (aniline shown) (molar mass 93.1 g/mol), at full conversion:

GasMassVolume at 25 °CHandling
Nitrogen30.1 kg26.3 m³Not toxic, but its volume is large: plan the vent for the next step and for any decomposition. (released when the diazonium decomposes, or in the next step (Sandmeyer, hydrolysis))

The gas comes off as fast as the reaction runs. In a dosing-controlled step the peak gas rate is about the dosing rate in kmol/h times the moles of gas per mole, so size the vent and scrubber on that.

By-products and how to control them

By-productCause and control
PhenolFrom the diazonium reacting with water when warm. Keep cold and use it promptly.
Triazenes and azo compoundsFree amine coupling with diazonium when acid is too low. Keep excess acid.
NitrosaminesFrom nitrite with secondary amines present (also in other steps). A regulated impurity: control nitrite and its removal.
NOxFrom nitrite in strong acid at the surface. Dose sub-surface and keep a slight excess only.

Moisture sensitivity: None

The reaction runs in water. The concern is temperature and stability, not moisture.

Heat of reaction

Diazotisation releases a moderate amount of heat, removed by cold jacket. The decomposition of the diazonium releases far more and gives nitrogen gas, so thermal stability data (DSC) are needed before scale-up.

Plant tips

  • Interlock nitrite dosing on high temperature.
  • Have a plan to quench the diazonium if the next step is delayed.
  • Continuous flow is increasingly used for diazotisation because it keeps the inventory of diazonium small.
