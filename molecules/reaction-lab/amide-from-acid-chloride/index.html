Amide formation from an acid chloride

An amine attacks an acid chloride and the amide forms in minutes, releasing one mole of HCl. A base, usually triethylamine or aqueous alkali, takes up the HCl so that the amine is not used up as its salt. It is one of the most common bond-forming steps in API synthesis.

Gas evolvedNone while the base is present; HCl fumes without base
Moisture sensitivityHigh
HeatStrongly exothermic
TypeAcylation and activation

Acid chloride + Amine (methylamine shown) + Triethylamine → Amide + Triethylamine hydrochloride (toluene or THF, 0–10 °C)

In the lab

  1. Under nitrogen, charge the amine and triethylamine in the solvent.
  2. Cool to 0 °C.
  3. Dose the acid chloride slowly. Each drop reacts at once; the temperature rises and the hydrochloride salt starts to come out.
  4. Stir at 5–10 °C until the amine is used up (check by TLC or HPLC).
  5. Add water to dissolve the salt, then separate the layers.
  6. Amide in the organic layer, triethylamine hydrochloride in the water.

Mechanism

  1. The amine nitrogen attacks the carbonyl carbon of the acid chloride. The C=O π bond moves onto oxygen.
  2. The tetrahedral intermediate collapses: C=O re-forms and chloride leaves.
  3. Triethylamine takes the proton from nitrogen, giving the neutral amide and triethylamine hydrochloride.
  4. Products: the amide and the hydrochloride salt, which usually comes out as a white solid in non-polar solvents.

What to take care of

  • Fast and exothermic: control the temperature with the dosing rate.
  • Use at least one equivalent of base; without it half the amine is lost as its hydrochloride and HCl can fume.
  • Acid chlorides hydrolyse in moist air and solvent to the acid and HCl. Keep everything dry until the reaction is complete.
  • Thick slurries of the hydrochloride salt can stop stirring; leave enough solvent.

Gas evolution

No gas is formed in the main reaction (none while the base is present; HCl fumes without base).

By-products and how to control them

By-productCause and control
Carboxylic acidFrom water hydrolysing the acid chloride. Dry solvent and amine; dose the acid chloride last.
Diacylation (imide)Excess acid chloride on primary amides at higher temperature. Keep near 1.0–1.05 eq and cold.
EsterIf an alcohol is present (solvent or another group). Avoid alcohol solvents.
Ketene productsAcid chlorides with an α-hydrogen can lose HCl to a ketene with strong base. Keep cold and dose slowly.

Moisture sensitivity: High

The acid chloride reacts with water to give the carboxylic acid and HCl, lowering yield and adding an impurity that can be hard to remove. Dry solvent and amine are needed; the Schotten–Baumann version in water works only because the amine reacts faster than water.

Heat of reaction

Typically 20–30 kcal per mole of acid chloride, plus the heat of neutralising HCl with the base. Run as dosing-controlled.

Plant tips

  • Charge the acid chloride below the liquid surface or with good mixing to avoid local hot spots.
  • Check the amine assay before charging; the stoichiometry depends on it.
  • The aqueous layer contains triethylamine hydrochloride; it can be recovered with caustic.
