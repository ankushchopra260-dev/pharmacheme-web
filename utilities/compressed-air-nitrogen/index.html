Compressed Air & Nitrogen Calculations

Compressed air is one of the most expensive utilities per unit of useful energy, and nitrogen is a safety utility. Work out what leaks cost, size a receiver, estimate compressor power and plan nitrogen for purging and blanketing.

Why compressed air is expensive

Only about 10–15 % of the electricity a compressor draws comes out as useful work at the tool; the rest becomes heat in the compressor. A compressor making 10 m³/min at 7 barg draws about 60–65 kW. Leaks, pressure set higher than needed and air used for cleaning or cooling waste a large share of it.

Air leaks

Air through a hole (choked flow): m = Cd × A × P0 × √[k / (R T) × (2/(k+1))(k+1)/(k−1)]
Power wasted (kW) = leak (m³/min free air) × specific power (kW per m³/min)

Above about 0.9 barg the flow through a hole is choked, so it rises in step with the absolute pressure. With a discharge coefficient of 0.65, a 3 mm hole at 7 barg leaks about 0.43 m³/min. Ten such leaks waste about 4.3 m³/min, roughly 28 kW, or about ₹18 lakh a year at ₹8/kWh. A simple check: with all users stopped, time how fast the receiver pressure falls, or how long the compressor stays loaded.

Air receiver size

V (m³) = Q (m³/min free air) × t (min) × 1.013 / (P1 − P2)

To cover a 10 m³/min demand for 1 minute while the pressure falls from 7.5 to 6.5 barg needs about 10 m³ of storage. A receiver near a large intermittent user, such as a filter press, centrifuge or bag filter pulse cleaning, smooths peaks and stops a second compressor starting for a few seconds of demand.

Compressor power

Adiabatic power = n × k/(k−1) × P1 × Q × [(P2/P1)(k−1)/(n k) − 1]
Shaft power = adiabatic power / overall efficiency

n is the number of stages and k = 1.4 for air. For 10 m³/min to 7 barg in one stage, the adiabatic power is about 48 kW and the shaft power about 64 kW at 75 % efficiency, about 6.4 kW per m³/min. Every extra 1 bar of discharge pressure costs roughly 6–8 % more power, so set the compressor to the lowest pressure the users need.

Nitrogen for inerting

Pressure or vacuum purge: O₂ after n cycles = O₂start × (Plow / Phigh)n (absolute pressures)
N₂ per cycle (Nm³) = V × (Phigh − Plow) / 1.013
Sweep purge (well mixed): N₂ (Nm³) = V × ln(O₂start / O₂target)

For a 6 m³ reactor headspace purged to 2 barg and vented to atmosphere, each cycle cuts oxygen to about a third: 7.1 % after one cycle and 2.3 % after two. Most solvent vapours cannot burn below about 10 % oxygen (the limiting oxygen concentration), and plants commonly aim for 8 % or less, so one cycle leaves almost no margin. Two cycles use about 24 Nm³. Always confirm with an oxygen analyser; a sweep purge with poor mixing can leave pockets of air.

When a blanketed tank is pumped out, nitrogen must replace the liquid volume: 20 m³/h of liquid at 50 mbarg blanket pressure needs about 19 Nm³/h of nitrogen. Size the regulator for the fastest pump-out plus in-breathing when a warm tank cools quickly in rain.

Frequently asked questions

How much power does a compressed air leak waste?

Multiply the leak flow in m³/min by the compressor's specific power. A single 3 mm leak at 7 barg wastes about 2.8 kW, all day and all night.

Can I use compressed air instead of nitrogen to push a solvent?

No. Air adds oxygen to a flammable vapour space. Use nitrogen, or a pump, and keep the vessel inerted.

Pressure purge or sweep purge: which uses less nitrogen?

For a vessel that can take pressure, pressure purging is predictable and easy to check. Vacuum purging uses the least nitrogen. Sweep purging suits vessels that cannot take pressure or vacuum, but needs good mixing and an oxygen check at the vent.
