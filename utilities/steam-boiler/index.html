How a steam system works

The boiler burns fuel to turn feed water into steam. Steam travels through the mains to jackets, coils and heat exchangers, where it condenses and gives up its latent heat. The condensate drains through steam traps and, in a good system, returns hot to the boiler feed tank. Every kilogram of steam that leaks, every trap that blows through and every litre of condensate sent to drain has to be made again from cold water with more fuel.

Boiler fuel consumption

Fuel (kg/h) = S × (hg − hfw) / (η × GCV × 4.1868)
Evaporation ratio = S / Fuel

S is steam flow (kg/h), hg the enthalpy of saturated steam at the boiler pressure (kcal/kg, from steam tables), hfw ≈ 1 × feed water temperature (kcal/kg), η the boiler efficiency as a fraction and GCV the fuel's gross calorific value in kcal/kg.

Worked example

5,000 kg/h of steam at 10 barg, feed water at 80 °C, Indian coal at 4,000 kcal/kg, boiler efficiency 75 %:

hg at 10 barg (11 bar abs) ≈ 664 kcal/kg; hfw = 1 × 80 = 80 kcal/kg
Heat per kg = 664 − 80 = 584 kcal/kg
Fuel = 5,000 × 584 / (0.75 × 4,000) ≈ 973 kg/h → about 23 t/day
Evaporation ratio = 5,000 / 973 ≈ 5.1

Boiler efficiency: direct method

Measure steam (from a flow meter or feed water meter) and fuel (weighed or metered) over the same few hours at steady load, then turn the fuel formula around: η = S × (hg − hfw) / (Fuel × GCV × 4.1868). It is quick, but a 5 % error in the steam meter is a 5 % error in efficiency. The indirect (heat loss) method, which adds up flue gas, moisture, unburnt and radiation losses, tells you why efficiency is low.

Flash steam

Flash fraction = (hf,1 − hf,2) / hfg,2

Condensate leaving a trap is at the saturation temperature of the higher pressure. When it drops to the return line pressure it carries more heat than water can hold there, so part of it boils. From 8 barg to 0.5 barg about 12 % flashes: 2,000 kg/h of condensate gives roughly 250 kg/h of low-pressure steam. Collect it in a flash vessel and use it for a low-pressure heater, or it simply vents from the feed tank.

Condensate recovery

Returned condensate saves treated water, chemicals and the fuel to heat cold make-up water. A rule of thumb: every 6 °C rise in feed water temperature saves about 1 % of boiler fuel. Returning 3,000 kg/h at 90 °C instead of 30 °C make-up recovers about 1,80,000 kcal/h, worth roughly 60 kg/h of coal.

Steam leaks and failed traps

Napier's formula gives the steam through a sharp hole: m (kg/h) ≈ 0.412 × d² (mm²) × P (bar abs). A 3 mm hole at 7 barg passes about 30 kg/h, around 240 tonnes a year. Treat it as an upper estimate for a trap blowing through; a proper trap survey with ultrasound or temperature tells you which ones.

Where steam money is usually lost

  • Failed-open steam traps: a survey often finds 10–20 % failed in systems without a programme.
  • Condensate sent to drain instead of back to the feed tank.
  • Flash steam venting from the feed tank or condensate receivers.
  • Bare or wet insulation on mains, valves and flanges.
  • Too much excess air: aim for about 3–4 % O₂ in flue gas on oil or gas, 6–8 % on solid fuel.
  • Continuous blowdown set by habit instead of by the boiler water TDS.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate boiler fuel consumption?

Fuel = steam × (steam enthalpy − feed water enthalpy) / (efficiency × GCV). In the example above, 5,000 kg/h of 10 barg steam needs about 973 kg/h of 4,000 kcal/kg coal at 75 % efficiency.

What is a good evaporation ratio?

It depends on the fuel: about 4–5.5 for Indian coal, 3.5–4.5 for rice husk, 13–15 for furnace oil. A falling evaporation ratio on the same fuel means falling efficiency.

Is flash steam the same as live steam?

It is ordinary steam at the lower pressure, with the same latent heat as live steam at that pressure. It is only “free” if there is a low-pressure user that can take it.
